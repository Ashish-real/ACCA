Every company holds financial instruments — cash, receivables, investments, loans, payables, shares. IFRS 9 governs how these are classified, measured, and tested for impairment. It is one of the most complex standards in IFRS — but also one of the most important for banks, FinTechs, and any MNC with investments or significant debtors.

"Financial instruments are contracts that create rights and obligations to receive or pay cash, shares, or other financial assets. They are the plumbing of the financial system."
10.1
What is a Financial Instrument?

A financial instrument is any contract that gives rise to a financial asset of one entity and a financial liability or equity instrument of another entity.

💰
Financial Assets
  • Cash and cash equivalents
  • Trade receivables (debtors)
  • Investments in shares and bonds
  • Loans receivable
  • Derivative assets (options, futures)
Rights to Receive
📄
Financial Liabilities
  • Trade payables (creditors)
  • Bank loans and overdrafts
  • Bonds and debentures issued
  • Lease liabilities
  • Derivative liabilities
Obligations to Pay
📜
Equity Instruments
  • Ordinary shares issued by the entity
  • Represents a residual interest
  • No obligation to deliver cash
  • Critical distinction from liability — IAS 32
Residual Interest

These three standards work together to give a complete picture — ACCA SBR often tests scenarios spanning all three.
10.2
Financial Assets — The 3 Classifications
IFRS 9's Core Framework

IFRS 9 classifies financial assets into three categories based on two tests: the entity's business model for managing the asset, and the contractual cash flow characteristics of the asset.

ClassificationMeasurementWhere Gains/Losses GoExamples
Amortised CostEffective interest methodInterest income to P&L; no fair value movementsTrade receivables, loans held to collect
Fair Value through OCI (FVOCI)Fair valueFair value changes to OCI; interest/dividends to P&LDebt instruments held to collect AND sell; some equity investments
Fair Value through P&L (FVTPL)Fair valueAll gains/losses to P&LTrading investments, derivatives, default category
10.3
The Business Model & Cash Flow Tests
How to Classify Correctly

To determine classification, apply two tests in sequence. This is the most tested decision-tree in ACCA FR and SBR.

Test 1 — Business Model Test
What is the entity's objective for holding this asset?
↓
🎯
Hold to Collect
  • Objective: collect contractual cash flows (interest + principal)
  • Sales are infrequent and incidental
  • Passes business model test for Amortised Cost
🔄
Hold to Collect AND Sell
  • Objective: both collect cash flows AND sell asset
  • Regular sales activity is part of the strategy
  • Points toward FVOCI classification
📈
Other Business Models
  • Trading — buy and sell for short-term profit
  • Managed on a fair value basis
  • Defaults to FVTPL
↓
Test 2 — SPPI Test (Solely Payments of Principal & Interest)
Do the contractual cash flows represent SOLELY payments of principal and interest on the principal outstanding? If YES → proceeds to Amortised Cost or FVOCI based on business model. If NO (e.g. convertible bonds, equity-linked returns) → FVTPL.
💡 Equity Investments — Special Rule
Investments in equity instruments (shares) are normally measured at FVTPL. However, an entity can make an irrevocable election at initial recognition to present fair value changes in OCI (FVOCI) — but this election means no recycling to P&L ever, even on disposal. Only dividend income goes to P&L. This election is common for strategic, non-trading equity investments.
10.4
Initial & Subsequent Measurement
Worked Examples

All financial assets are initially measured at fair value. Transaction costs are added for Amortised Cost and FVOCI assets, but expensed immediately for FVTPL assets.

CategoryInitial MeasurementTransaction CostsSubsequent Measurement
Amortised CostFair valueAdded to costAmortised cost using effective interest rate
FVOCIFair valueAdded to costFair value — changes to OCI
FVTPLFair valueExpensed to P&L immediatelyFair value — changes to P&L

Worked Example — Bond Investment at Amortised Cost

Ashish Investments Ltd — 3-Year Bond

Purchase: Bond purchased for ₹9,52,000 (face value ₹10,00,000, 5% coupon, effective rate 7%)
Held to collect contractual cash flows → Amortised Cost classification

Year 1:
Interest income (P&L) = ₹9,52,000 × 7% = ₹66,640
Cash received (coupon) = ₹10,00,000 × 5% = ₹50,000
Difference added to carrying amount = ₹16,640
Closing carrying amount = ₹9,52,000 + ₹16,640 = ₹9,68,640

This continues until carrying amount = ₹10,00,000 (face value) at maturity. The effective interest method spreads the discount over the bond's life.

Worked Example — FVTPL Investment

Ashish Investments Ltd — Trading Shares

Shares purchased for ₹5,00,000 (brokerage ₹5,000 — expensed immediately)
Year-end fair value = ₹5,80,000

Gain to P&L = ₹5,80,000 − ₹5,00,000 = ₹80,000
Journal: Dr Investment ₹80,000 | Cr Gain on FV (P&L) ₹80,000
10.5
Financial Liabilities — Classification & Measurement

Most financial liabilities are measured at amortised cost using the effective interest method — the same logic as financial assets, but for what the entity owes.

🏦
Amortised Cost (Default)
  • Most liabilities: loans, bonds issued, trade payables
  • Effective interest rate method
  • Interest expense to P&L
  • Most common classification
Default Category
📊
FVTPL — Designated or Held for Trading
  • Derivative liabilities (always FVTPL)
  • Liabilities held for short-term trading
  • Fair value option to reduce accounting mismatch
  • Fair value changes to P&L
Less Common
⚡ Compound Financial Instruments — Convertible Bonds
A convertible bond contains BOTH a liability component (obligation to pay interest/principal) AND an equity component (option to convert to shares). IAS 32 requires splitting these:

Liability component = PV of cash flows discounted at market rate for a similar bond WITHOUT conversion option
Equity component = Total proceeds − Liability component (balancing figure)

This split is a classic ACCA SBR exam question — the equity component is NEVER subsequently remeasured.
10.6
Expected Credit Loss (ECL) Model
The Game-Changer for Banking

Before IFRS 9, impairment losses were only recognised when there was objective evidence of a loss event (incurred loss model). IFRS 9 introduced the Expected Credit Loss (ECL) model — a forward-looking approach that recognises expected losses BEFORE they happen.

🟢
Stage 1 — Performing
  • No significant increase in credit risk since initial recognition
  • Recognise: 12-month ECL
  • Interest calculated on gross carrying amount
  • Most loans/receivables start here
12-Month ECL
🟡
Stage 2 — Underperforming
  • Significant increase in credit risk since initial recognition
  • Recognise: Lifetime ECL
  • Interest still on gross carrying amount
  • Example: payments overdue but not yet defaulted
Lifetime ECL
🔴
Stage 3 — Non-Performing (Credit-Impaired)
  • Objective evidence of impairment (default, bankruptcy)
  • Recognise: Lifetime ECL
  • Interest calculated on NET carrying amount (after ECL)
  • Example: borrower in default, legal proceedings started
Credit-Impaired
ECL = Probability of Default (PD) × Loss Given Default (LGD) × Exposure at Default (EAD)
The core ECL formula — used by banks and FinTechs for provisioning

10.7
Equity vs Liability — IAS 32 Classification

One of the most important judgments in IFRS — is an instrument equity or a liability? The answer depends on substance, not legal form.

TestIf YES → LiabilityIf NO → Equity
Contractual obligation to deliver cash/assets?Redeemable preference sharesOrdinary shares (no redemption)
Mandatory dividend payments?Liability — like interestDiscretionary dividends — equity
Settlement in variable number of own shares?LiabilityFixed number of shares — equity
📌 Why This Classification Matters
If a preference share is classified as a liability, its "dividend" is actually interest expense — reducing profit. This affects: gearing ratios, EPS, debt covenants, and how the company appears to lenders and investors. Companies sometimes structure instruments carefully to achieve a desired classification — a major area of scrutiny for auditors and SBR exam questions.
10.8
Hedge Accounting — Introduction

Companies use derivatives (forwards, futures, options, swaps) to manage risk — currency risk, interest rate risk, commodity price risk. Hedge accounting allows the gain/loss on the hedging instrument to be matched with the gain/loss on the hedged item.

💵
Fair Value Hedge
  • Hedges exposure to changes in fair value of an asset/liability
  • Both hedging instrument and hedged item measured at FV through P&L
  • Example: hedging fixed-rate debt against interest rate changes
📅
Cash Flow Hedge
  • Hedges exposure to variability in future cash flows
  • Effective portion of gain/loss → OCI
  • Example: hedging future foreign currency purchases
🌍
Net Investment Hedge
  • Hedges currency exposure on investment in foreign operations
  • Common for MNCs with overseas subsidiaries
  • Effective portion → OCI (translation reserve)

Common Mistakes, Flashcards & Interview Q&A

Part A — Common Mistakes (Don't Repeat These)

❌ MISTAKE 1
Adding Transaction Costs to FVTPL Assets
Transaction costs are capitalised (added to cost) for Amortised Cost and FVOCI assets, but must be EXPENSED IMMEDIATELY to P&L for FVTPL assets — never added to the initial carrying amount. This is a frequently tested classification rule.
❌ MISTAKE 2
Confusing the Business Model Test with the Cash Flow Characteristics Test
Classification under IFRS 9 requires BOTH tests to pass for Amortised Cost/FVOCI: the business model test (is the asset held to collect cash flows, to sell, or both?) AND the SPPI test (are cash flows solely payments of principal and interest?). Passing only one test doesn't achieve the classification — both are required.
❌ MISTAKE 3
Calculating Interest on Gross Carrying Amount for Stage 3 (Credit-Impaired) Assets
For Stage 1 and Stage 2, interest income is calculated on the gross carrying amount. But once an asset moves to Stage 3 (credit-impaired), interest must be calculated on the NET carrying amount (after deducting the ECL allowance) — a detail often missed.
❌ MISTAKE 4
Treating All Convertible Bonds as Pure Liabilities
A convertible bond is a compound instrument under IAS 32 — it must be split into a liability component (PV of cash flows at market rate) and an equity component (residual). Recording the entire proceeds as a liability ignores the embedded conversion option's equity nature.
❌ MISTAKE 5
Applying the General Approach Instead of the Simplified Approach for Trade Receivables
IFRS 9 permits (and most companies use) a simplified approach for trade receivables — recognising lifetime ECL immediately without tracking stage migrations. Applying the full 3-stage general model to simple trade receivables is unnecessarily complex and not the examinable default for most ACCA FR questions.
❌ MISTAKE 6
Forgetting Equity Instruments Can Elect FVOCI (No Recycling)
For equity investments (not held for trading), an entity can irrevocably elect FVOCI at initial recognition. Unlike FVOCI for debt, gains/losses on equity FVOCI are NEVER reclassified ("recycled") to P&L, even on disposal — only dividend income hits P&L.

Part D — Interview Questions & Model Answers

QuestionWhat They Are TestingModel Answer
"What's the difference between the incurred loss model and the expected credit loss model?"Understanding the key IFRS 9 reform vs its predecessor IAS 39The incurred loss model (under old IAS 39) only recognised impairment when there was objective evidence of a loss event already occurring — often described as "too little, too late," notably during the 2008 financial crisis. IFRS 9's ECL model is forward-looking, requiring entities to recognise expected losses based on reasonable and supportable forecasts before an actual default occurs, providing earlier warning of credit deterioration.
"How would you classify a bond your company plans to hold until maturity?"Practical application of the classification frameworkIf the business model is to hold the asset to collect contractual cash flows (not to sell), and those cash flows are solely payments of principal and interest (passing the SPPI test), the bond would be classified at Amortised Cost — measured using the effective interest method, with no fair value remeasurement through profit or OCI.
"Why does IFRS 9 matter more for banks than for a typical manufacturing company?"Real-world sector awarenessBanks hold large portfolios of loans and financial instruments as their core business, making classification, ECL provisioning, and fair value measurement central to their financial statements and regulatory capital calculations. A manufacturer's financial instruments (cash, trade receivables, maybe some investments) are comparatively small relative to operating assets, so IFRS 9's impact, while still relevant, is less material to overall reported performance.
