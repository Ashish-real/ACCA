Large companies rarely operate as a single legal entity. Tata, Reliance, Infosys, Google, Amazon — all are groups made up of a parent company and many subsidiaries. Consolidated financial statements present the group as if it were one single economic entity — combining all the subsidiaries' assets, liabilities, income, and expenses with the parent's. This is one of the most technical — and most career-relevant — areas of ACCA.

"A group's financial statements tell the story of the whole empire — not just the head office. Consolidation is how that story gets told accurately."
11.1
What is a Group — and Why Consolidate?

A group consists of a parent company and one or more subsidiaries that it controls. Each company is a separate legal entity — but together they operate as one economic unit.

Ashish Holdings Ltd (Parent)
Prepares Consolidated Financial Statements
Ashish Consulting Ltd
Subsidiary — 80% owned
Controlled — Consolidate 100%
Ashish FinTech Ltd
Subsidiary — 100% owned
Controlled — Consolidate 100%
🎯
Why Consolidate?
  • Show the group's TOTAL economic resources and performance
  • Individual company accounts would understate the group's size
  • Investors need to see the whole picture, not just the parent
  • Required by IFRS 10 for all entities with subsidiaries
  • Listed companies MUST present consolidated accounts
Single Economic Entity View
📑
Parent vs Subsidiary vs Associate
  • Parent: entity that controls one or more entities
  • Subsidiary: entity controlled by another (the parent)
  • Associate: significant influence but not control (IAS 28)
  • Different accounting treatment for each relationship type

11.2
Control — The Key to Consolidation
IFRS 10

Under IFRS 10, an investor controls an investee if and only if it has ALL THREE of the following elements.

ElementWhat It MeansExample
1. Power over the investeeExisting rights that give current ability to direct relevant activitiesVoting rights, board appointment rights
2. Exposure to variable returnsReturns that vary based on investee's performanceDividends, changes in value of investment
3. Ability to use power to affect returnsLink between power and the variability of returnsCan use power to influence the amount of returns
✅
Usually = Control
  • Owning more than 50% of voting rights
  • Majority of board of directors appointed by parent
  • Power to govern financial and operating policies
Majority Ownership
⚠️
Control WITHOUT Majority Ownership
  • De facto control — large shareholding, rest widely dispersed
  • Potential voting rights (options, convertibles) currently exercisable
  • Contractual arrangements giving decision-making power
Substance over Form
🚫
Majority Ownership WITHOUT Control
  • Government or court-imposed restrictions on power
  • Entity in liquidation/administration — controlled by administrator
  • Rare but tested in SBR exam scenarios

11.3
Goodwill on Acquisition
IFRS 3 — Business Combinations

When a parent acquires a subsidiary, it usually pays more than the fair value of the identifiable net assets acquired. This excess is Goodwill — representing reputation, customer relationships, synergies, and future growth potential.

Goodwill = Consideration Paid + Fair Value of NCI
− Fair Value of Identifiable Net Assets Acquired
The Goodwill Calculation — performed at acquisition date

📌 What Happens to Goodwill After Recognition?
Goodwill is NOT amortised under IFRS. Instead, it is tested for impairment at least annually (IAS 36). If the recoverable amount of the cash-generating unit falls below carrying amount, goodwill is written down first — and impairment is never reversed. This is different from the old approach of amortising goodwill over its useful life.
11.4
Non-Controlling Interest (NCI)

When a parent owns less than 100% of a subsidiary, the remaining ownership belongs to outside shareholders — the Non-Controlling Interest (NCI). Even though the parent doesn't own 100%, consolidation includes 100% of the subsidiary's assets, liabilities, income, and expenses — with NCI's share shown separately.

📊
NCI at Acquisition — 2 Methods
  • Fair Value Method (Full Goodwill): NCI measured at fair value — goodwill includes NCI's share
  • Proportionate Method (Partial Goodwill): NCI = % of identifiable net assets — goodwill excludes NCI's share
  • Choice made on an acquisition-by-acquisition basis
  • Fair value method is more common in practice
IFRS 3 Choice
📈
NCI in the Balance Sheet
  • Shown within Equity — but separately from parent's equity
  • NCI at acquisition + NCI's share of post-acquisition reserves
  • Represents the outside shareholders' stake in the group
  • Updated every year for the subsidiary's profit/loss
Part of Equity
💰
NCI in the Income Statement
  • Profit for the year is split: Parent's share + NCI's share
  • Shown as "Profit attributable to NCI" below the total profit line
  • NCI % × Subsidiary's profit for the year
  • Adjust for fair value depreciation and intra-group items first
Profit Allocation
11.5
Consolidated Statement of Financial Position
Step-by-Step Approach

Preparing a consolidated SFP follows a consistent step-by-step process. ACCA exams test this heavily — memorise this sequence.

1️⃣
Add Across
  • Add 100% of parent's and subsidiary's assets/liabilities
  • Line by line — Cash + Cash, PPE + PPE, etc.
2️⃣
Calculate Goodwill
  • As shown in section 11.3
  • Include as a separate non-current asset
3️⃣
Fair Value Adjustments
  • Adjust subsidiary's assets to fair value at acquisition
  • Additional depreciation on the uplift each year
4️⃣
Eliminate Investment in Subsidiary
  • Parent's "Investment in Subsidiary" asset is cancelled
  • It's replaced by the subsidiary's actual net assets + goodwill
5️⃣
Calculate Retained Earnings
  • Parent's RE + Parent's share of subsidiary's POST-acquisition profits
  • Adjust for unrealised profits in intra-group transactions
6️⃣
Calculate NCI
  • NCI at acquisition + NCI's share of post-acquisition reserves
  • Shown separately within equity
💡 The Golden Rule of Consolidation
Pre-acquisition profits are NOT consolidated — they were already accounted for in goodwill. Only post-acquisition profits of the subsidiary are added to group retained earnings (parent's share) and NCI (NCI's share). This single rule explains most of the adjustments in a consolidation question.
11.6
Consolidated Income Statement

The consolidated Income Statement combines the parent's and subsidiary's results — but with key adjustments.

ItemHow It's Consolidated
Revenue, Cost of Sales, ExpensesAdd 100% of parent + 100% of subsidiary (line by line)
Subsidiary acquired mid-yearOnly include subsidiary's results from acquisition date onwards (time-apportion)
Intra-group salesEliminate from both revenue and cost of sales — group can't sell to itself
Unrealised profit in closing inventoryRemove the profit margin on goods still in group inventory
Fair value depreciation adjustmentAdditional depreciation charge on the fair value uplift
Profit attributable to NCINCI % × subsidiary's adjusted profit for the year
Profit attributable to owners of parentTotal group profit − NCI's share
11.7
Intra-Group Transactions & Eliminations
The Adjustments That Make or Break a Question

When companies within a group trade with each other, these transactions must be eliminated on consolidation — because from the group's perspective, no sale to an outsider occurred.

💸
Intra-Group Sales
  • Parent sells goods to subsidiary (or vice versa)
  • Eliminate the full sales/purchases amount from both Revenue and COGS
  • No impact on group profit if all goods are sold externally by year-end
Eliminate Both Sides
📦
Unrealised Profit in Inventory (PUP)
  • If goods sold within group remain in closing inventory
  • The profit margin on those goods is "unrealised" from group view
  • Reduce inventory value AND reduce group profit by the unrealised margin
  • Classic exam adjustment — Provision for Unrealised Profit (PUP)
PUP Adjustment
🔄
Intra-Group Balances
  • Receivable in one company's books = Payable in the other's
  • Both must be eliminated — cancel out on consolidation
  • Watch for cash/goods in transit causing mismatches
Cancel Out
💰
Intra-Group Dividends
  • Dividend paid by subsidiary to parent
  • Eliminate from parent's investment income
  • Eliminate from subsidiary's dividend paid (in retained earnings)
Eliminate Income & Expense

PUP Calculation Example

Unrealised Profit in Inventory — Worked Example

Parent sells goods to Subsidiary for ₹2,00,000 (cost to Parent ₹1,50,000) — markup = ₹50,000 (33.33% on cost, or 25% on selling price)
At year-end, Subsidiary still holds ₹80,000 of these goods (at transfer price) in closing inventory

Unrealised profit = ₹80,000 × 25% (margin on selling price) = ₹20,000

Adjustment:
Reduce Group Inventory by ₹20,000
Reduce Group Retained Earnings (Cost of Sales ↑) by ₹20,000
If Parent sold the goods, the PUP reduces Parent's RE (100%). If Subsidiary sold to Parent, PUP is split between Parent's RE and NCI based on ownership %.

11.12
Common Mistakes, Flashcards & Interview Q&A

Part A — Common Mistakes (Don't Repeat These)

❌ MISTAKE 1
Consolidating Only the Parent's % of Subsidiary Assets/Liabilities
Consolidation includes 100% of the subsidiary's assets, liabilities, revenue, and expenses — regardless of the parent's ownership percentage. The ownership % only affects the SPLIT of equity/profit between the parent and NCI, not the line-by-line addition of the subsidiary's figures.
❌ MISTAKE 2
Including Pre-Acquisition Profits in Group Retained Earnings
Only POST-acquisition profits of the subsidiary are added to group retained earnings — pre-acquisition profits were already captured in the goodwill calculation (as part of net assets acquired). Adding pre-acquisition profits again double-counts them.
❌ MISTAKE 3
Forgetting to Eliminate Both Sides of Intra-Group Transactions
Intra-group sales must be eliminated from BOTH revenue (seller's side) and cost of sales (buyer's side) — eliminating only one side leaves the consolidated figures unbalanced and overstates total group revenue.
❌ MISTAKE 4
Calculating PUP on the Full Intra-Group Sale Instead of Only Unsold Closing Inventory
The unrealised profit adjustment only applies to the portion of intra-group goods still held in closing inventory at year-end — goods already sold externally by year-end have "realised" the profit from the group's perspective and need no adjustment.
❌ MISTAKE 5
Amortising Goodwill
Under current IFRS 3, goodwill is NOT amortised — it is tested for impairment at least annually. Applying a systematic amortisation charge (an older accounting practice, still used in some other GAAPs) is incorrect under IFRS.
❌ MISTAKE 6
Confusing Subsidiaries, Associates, and Joint Ventures
Subsidiaries (control, typically >50%) are fully consolidated line-by-line. Associates (significant influence, typically 20-50%) use the equity method — showing only the investor's share of profit as a single line. Joint ventures (joint control) also use the equity method under IFRS 11. Applying full consolidation to an associate is a common exam error.

Part D — Interview Questions & Model Answers

QuestionWhat They Are TestingModel Answer
"Why do companies prepare consolidated financial statements?"Foundational purpose of consolidationA group operates through multiple legal entities, but investors and lenders need to evaluate the group as a single economic entity to understand its true financial position and performance. Consolidated statements combine the parent and all controlled subsidiaries into one set of accounts, eliminating intra-group transactions that would otherwise double-count revenue and distort the group's real economic activity.
"What is goodwill, and why isn't it amortised?"Depth of understanding on IFRS 3 treatmentGoodwill represents the premium paid over the fair value of identifiable net assets — reflecting synergies, brand value, and future growth potential that can't be separately identified. The IASB moved away from amortisation because it's difficult to estimate a meaningful useful life for goodwill; instead, it's tested for impairment annually, so any decline in value is recognised when it actually occurs rather than through an arbitrary systematic charge.
"What's the difference between a subsidiary and an associate, and how does the accounting differ?"Practical classification and treatment knowledgeA subsidiary is controlled by the parent (typically >50% ownership) and is fully consolidated — 100% of its assets, liabilities, revenue and expenses are combined line-by-line with NCI shown separately. An associate is one where the investor has significant influence but not control (typically 20-50% ownership) and is accounted for using the equity method — only the investor's share of the associate's profit appears as a single line in the income statement, and the investment is carried at cost plus share of post-acquisition profits on the balance sheet.
