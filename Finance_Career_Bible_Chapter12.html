12.1
Why Interpretation Matters

A single number — "Profit: ₹50 lakh" — tells you almost nothing on its own. Is it good? Bad? Better than last year? Better than competitors? Ratio analysis converts absolute numbers into relative, comparable insights.

🔍
Three Types of Comparison
  • Trend analysis: Compare to the same company's prior years
  • Cross-sectional: Compare to competitors / industry average
  • Target comparison: Compare to budget or benchmark
  • A ratio alone means nothing — context is everything

The Big 5
12.2
Profitability Ratios
How Much Profit is Made?

Profitability ratios measure how effectively a company converts revenue into profit at different levels of the Income Statement.

Gross Profit Margin
Gross Profit ÷ Revenue × 100
Measures pricing power and production efficiency. A falling margin may indicate rising costs or pricing pressure from competition.
Operating Profit Margin
Operating Profit (EBIT) ÷ Revenue × 100
Shows profitability from core operations before financing and tax. Useful for comparing companies with different capital structures.
Net Profit Margin
Profit After Tax ÷ Revenue × 100
The "bottom line" margin — what shareholders ultimately get to keep per rupee of revenue.
Return on Capital Employed (ROCE)
Operating Profit ÷ Capital Employed × 100
Capital Employed = Total Equity + Non-Current Liabilities. The single most important ratio — shows how efficiently capital generates profit.
Return on Equity (ROE)
Profit After Tax ÷ Shareholders' Equity × 100
Return generated specifically for ordinary shareholders. Higher gearing can artificially boost ROE — always check alongside ROCE.
Asset Turnover
Revenue ÷ Net Assets (Capital Employed)
How many rupees of revenue generated per rupee of capital employed. Links profitability to efficiency — the DuPont decomposition.
🎯 The DuPont Decomposition — ROCE Breakdown
ROCE = Operating Profit Margin × Asset Turnover

This shows ROCE can improve in TWO ways: (1) increase margins — better pricing or cost control, or (2) increase asset turnover — generate more revenue per rupee of assets. A company with falling ROCE — examiners want you to identify WHICH of these two drivers caused it.
12.3
Liquidity Ratios
Can Debts Be Paid Short-Term?

Liquidity ratios measure a company's ability to meet its short-term obligations. A profitable company can still fail if it runs out of cash (remember Chapter 3 — Profit vs Cash).

Current Ratio
Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities
A ratio above 1 means current assets cover current liabilities. Too high may mean inefficient use of resources; too low signals liquidity risk.
Quick Ratio (Acid Test)
(Current Assets − Inventory) ÷ Current Liabilities
Stricter test — excludes inventory (least liquid current asset). Better measure of immediate liquidity, especially for businesses with slow-moving stock.
Cash Ratio
Cash & Equivalents ÷ Current Liabilities
The most conservative liquidity measure — only counts actual cash. Used by lenders assessing immediate default risk.
💡 "Too High" Can Also Be Bad
A current ratio of 5:1 might look safe — but it could mean cash is sitting idle, inventory is excessive, or receivables aren't being collected. Capital tied up in working capital earns no return. ACCA examiners reward candidates who can argue BOTH sides — too low is risky, too high may signal inefficiency.
12.4
Efficiency (Activity) Ratios
How Well Are Assets Used?

Efficiency ratios — also called "working capital ratios" — measure how well a company manages inventory, receivables, and payables. Together they reveal the cash conversion cycle.

Inventory Days (Holding Period)
(Inventory ÷ COGS) × 365
Average days inventory is held before sale. Lower = faster moving stock = less capital tied up.
Receivables Days
(Trade Receivables ÷ Revenue) × 365
Average days taken to collect from customers. Rising receivables days may indicate collection problems or overly generous credit terms.
Payables Days
(Trade Payables ÷ Purchases or COGS) × 365
Average days taken to pay suppliers. Higher can mean better cash management — but too high may signal inability to pay or strained supplier relationships.
Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC)
Inventory Days + Receivables Days − Payables Days
Total time cash is tied up in working capital. A NEGATIVE CCC (e.g. Amazon model) means suppliers fund the business — extremely powerful.
Asset Turnover (Net Assets)
Revenue ÷ Total Assets (or Net Assets)
Overall measure of how efficiently total assets generate sales revenue.
Non-Current Asset Turnover
Revenue ÷ Non-Current Assets
Specifically measures how efficiently PPE and other long-term assets generate revenue — useful for capital-intensive industries.
📌 The Cash Conversion Cycle — Real Power
Imagine: Inventory Days = 30, Receivables Days = 45, Payables Days = 90
CCC = 30 + 45 − 90 = −15 days

This means the company collects cash from customers and sells inventory 15 days BEFORE it has to pay its suppliers. Effectively, suppliers are financing the business interest-free. This is the model behind companies like Amazon, Zara, and many FMCG giants — a key topic for ACCA FM and corporate finance interviews.
12.5
Gearing (Leverage) Ratios
How Much Debt vs Equity?

Gearing ratios measure the proportion of debt in a company's capital structure — and the risk that comes with it. High gearing amplifies both returns and losses.

Gearing Ratio (Debt to Equity)
Total Debt ÷ Total Equity × 100
Shows reliance on debt financing relative to shareholder funds. Higher gearing = higher financial risk but potentially higher ROE.
Debt to Capital
Total Debt ÷ (Total Debt + Total Equity) × 100
Alternative gearing measure showing debt as a proportion of total long-term capital — used in different jurisdictions/contexts.
Interest Cover
Operating Profit (EBIT) ÷ Interest Expense
How many times operating profit covers interest payments. Below 2x is often a red flag for lenders — a key covenant metric.
⚖️ The Gearing Trade-Off
Debt is usually cheaper than equity (tax-deductible interest, lower required return). High gearing can boost ROE in good years through financial leverage. But in bad years, fixed interest payments must still be made — increasing the risk of financial distress. ACCA FM tests both the WACC impact and the risk implications of capital structure decisions.
12.6
Investor Ratios
What Return for Shareholders?
Earnings Per Share (EPS)
Profit Attributable to Ordinary Shareholders ÷ Weighted Avg No. of Shares
The most widely quoted investor metric. EPS growth is a primary driver of share price movements.
Price/Earnings (P/E) Ratio
Share Price ÷ EPS
How much investors are willing to pay per rupee of earnings. High P/E suggests high growth expectations (or overvaluation).
Dividend Yield
Dividend Per Share ÷ Share Price × 100
Cash return relative to share price — important for income-focused investors.
Dividend Cover
EPS ÷ Dividend Per Share
How many times earnings cover the dividend paid. Low cover may signal an unsustainable dividend.

📝 Interpretation — Writing the Analysis (ACCA Exam Style)

Ashish Consulting Ltd significantly outperforms the industry on profitability — gross margin (61.6% vs 55%) and ROCE (43.4% vs 25%) indicate strong pricing power and highly efficient use of capital, likely reflecting the asset-light nature of a consulting business.

Liquidity is exceptionally strong — a current ratio of 4.4:1 is well above the industry's 1.8:1, suggesting the company holds significant cash reserves. While this provides safety, it may also indicate underutilised capital that could be deployed for growth or returned to shareholders.

One area of concern: receivables days (52.6) exceed the industry average (35), suggesting either generous credit terms or potential collection issues — worth investigating further.

Gearing is low (19.1% vs 45%) with very high interest cover (12.75x), indicating significant unused debt capacity — the company could raise debt financing for expansion at low risk.

🎯 The ACCA Interpretation Formula
For every ratio: (1) State the number and direction of change/comparison, (2) Explain what it MEANS in business terms, (3) Suggest a possible REASON, (4) Where relevant, link to OTHER ratios for a complete picture. Never just state numbers — examiners reward INSIGHT, not calculation alone.

12.12
Earnings Per Share (IAS 33)

Part A — Why EPS Matters More Than Almost Any Other Single Number

EPS is one of the most widely-quoted, most heavily scrutinised figures in financial reporting — it directly feeds the P/E ratio, drives analyst forecasts, and (rightly or wrongly) is often the single number investors fixate on most. IAS 33 standardises exactly how it must be calculated.

EPS Formulas
Basic EPS
Profit Attributable to Ordinary Shareholders ÷ Weighted Average Number of Ordinary Shares Outstanding During the Period
Diluted EPS
Adjusted Profit ÷ (Weighted Average Shares + All Potentially Dilutive Shares — from convertible bonds, share options, convertible preference shares)
🔑 Why Diluted EPS Matters
Diluted EPS answers: "What would EPS look like if every convertible instrument (bonds, options, convertible preference shares) were actually converted into ordinary shares?" It's ALWAYS lower than or equal to basic EPS (since dilution only ever adds shares, never reduces them) — giving investors a more conservative, "worst-case" view of per-share earnings that accounts for the company's full potential share count.

Part B — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: Basic and Diluted EPS With a Mid-Year Share Issue

Given: Profit attributable to ordinary shareholders = ₹4,50,00,000. Shares outstanding at 1 Jan = 1,00,00,000. On 1 July, the company issued 20,00,000 new shares at full market price (no bonus element). The company also has ₹2 Crore of 8% convertible bonds outstanding, convertible into 5,00,000 shares; tax rate = 25%.

Step 1 — Weighted average shares (Basic EPS):
Jan-Jun (6 months): 1,00,00,000 shares
Jul-Dec (6 months): 1,20,00,000 shares (after the issue)
Weighted average = (1,00,00,000 × 6/12) + (1,20,00,000 × 6/12) = 50,00,000 + 60,00,000 = 1,10,00,000 shares

Step 2 — Basic EPS:
Basic EPS = ₹4,50,00,000 ÷ 1,10,00,000 = ₹4.09 per share

Step 3 — Adjust profit for diluted EPS (add back after-tax interest saved if bonds convert):
Interest on bonds = ₹2,00,00,000 × 8% = ₹16,00,000
After-tax interest saved = ₹16,00,000 × (1 − 25%) = ₹12,00,000
Adjusted profit = ₹4,50,00,000 + ₹12,00,000 = ₹4,62,00,000

Step 4 — Diluted shares:
1,10,00,000 + 5,00,000 (from bond conversion) = 1,15,00,000 shares

Step 5 — Diluted EPS:
Diluted EPS = ₹4,62,00,000 ÷ 1,15,00,000 = ₹4.02 per share

Confirms the rule: Diluted EPS (₹4.02) is LOWER than Basic EPS (₹4.09) — exactly as IAS 33 requires, since including potential future shares can only dilute, never enhance, the per-share figure.
12.13
Non-Current Assets Held for Sale & Discontinued Operations (IFRS 5)

Part A — Two Related But Distinct Concepts

🏷️
Held for Sale (Balance Sheet)
  • A non-current asset (or disposal group) qualifies if it's available for IMMEDIATE sale in its present condition, and the sale is HIGHLY PROBABLE (management committed to a plan, actively marketing at a reasonable price, expected to complete within 12 months)
  • Measured at the LOWER of carrying amount and fair value less costs to sell — and depreciation STOPS once classified as held for sale
  • Presented SEPARATELY on the Balance Sheet, distinct from other non-current assets
Balance Sheet Classification
🚪
Discontinued Operations (Income Statement)
  • A component of the entity that has EITHER been disposed of, OR is classified as held for sale, AND represents a SEPARATE MAJOR LINE of business or geographical area
  • Its results are presented as a SINGLE LINE on the face of the Income Statement (post-tax profit/loss from discontinued operations), separate from continuing operations, with detail in the notes
Income Statement Presentation
🔑 Why This Distinction Matters for Analysis
Separating discontinued operations from continuing operations gives analysts a CLEANER view of ongoing, sustainable business performance — mixing in the results of a business segment that's being sold or shut down would distort trend analysis and make it harder to forecast future performance based on the CONTINUING business alone. This directly connects to Chapter 12's core interpretation skills (trend analysis, ratio comparability across periods).

Part B — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: Presenting a Discontinued Division

Given: A company's continuing operations generated Profit Before Tax of ₹8 Crore (tax 25% = ₹2 Crore, PAT ₹6 Crore). Its retail division — a genuinely separate major line of business — was formally classified as held for sale on 1 October, generating a loss of ₹1.5 Crore for the period it was owned, plus an additional ₹0.5 Crore impairment loss on writing the division down to fair value less costs to sell. No tax effect assumed on the division's loss for simplicity.

Income Statement Presentation:
Profit from Continuing Operations (after tax): ₹6,00,00,000
Loss from Discontinued Operations (after tax): (₹2,00,00,000) — combining the ₹1.5 Cr operating loss + ₹0.5 Cr impairment
Total Profit for the Year: ₹4,00,00,000

Key Point: An analyst looking ONLY at "Total Profit" (₹4 Crore) would understate how well the CONTINUING business is actually performing (₹6 Crore) — the separate discontinued operations line lets them correctly identify that the ₹4 Crore figure is depressed by a division being exited, not a genuine decline in the core, ongoing business.
12.14
Related Party Disclosures (IAS 24)

Part A — Why Related Party Transactions Need Special Scrutiny

A transaction with a related party (a parent company, a subsidiary, key management personnel, or a close family member of a director, for example) might NOT be conducted on the same arm's-length terms as a transaction with an unrelated third party — even if the transaction is entirely legal, it could still distort the reported financial position or be used to manipulate results.

Related Party CategoryExample
Parent / Subsidiary / Fellow SubsidiaryAny entity within the same corporate group
Associates & Joint VenturesEntities where the reporting company has significant influence or joint control
Key Management Personnel (KMP)Directors and senior executives with authority/responsibility for planning and controlling the entity's activities
Close Family Members of KMPSpouses, children, and others who might influence or be influenced by the KMP in dealings with the entity
🔑 What Must Be Disclosed
For material related party transactions, IAS 24 requires disclosure of: the NATURE of the relationship, the AMOUNT of the transactions, OUTSTANDING balances (including terms and whether secured), and any PROVISIONS for doubtful debts related to those balances — specifically so that users of the financial statements can assess whether the entity's reported position might have been affected by dealings that weren't conducted purely at arm's length.

Part B — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: Identifying and Disclosing Related Party Transactions

Scenario: During the year, a company (a) sold goods worth ₹50,00,000 to its 40%-owned associate at normal market prices, (b) paid ₹2,00,00,000 in total remuneration to its 5 key management personnel, and (c) purchased raw materials worth ₹30,00,000 from a supplier — a company owned by the CFO's brother — at prices 15% above the market rate charged to other customers.

Analysis:
(a) The associate IS a related party (significant influence exists at 40% ownership) — DISCLOSE the ₹50,00,000 transaction, even though it was at normal market prices (disclosure is required regardless of whether terms were fair, precisely so users can judge that for themselves).
(b) KMP remuneration IS specifically required disclosure under IAS 24 — DISCLOSE the ₹2,00,00,000, typically broken down by category (short-term benefits, post-employment benefits, etc.).
(c) The CFO's brother's company IS a related party (close family member of KMP) — DISCLOSE the ₹30,00,000 transaction, and the fact that pricing was 15% above market rate is a significant, specifically noteworthy detail that should be highlighted, since it suggests the transaction was NOT conducted at arm's length and may warrant further governance scrutiny (linking to Chapter 38's Corporate Governance content on conflicts of interest).
12.15
Common Mistakes, Flashcards & Interview Q&A

Part A — Common Mistakes (Don't Repeat These)

❌ MISTAKE 1
Calculating a Ratio Without Interpreting It
ACCA exams (and interviews) award marks primarily for INTERPRETATION, not calculation. A ratio number alone means nothing without context — is it improving or worsening, above or below industry average, and what does it suggest about the business? Always follow every calculated ratio with a "so what."
❌ MISTAKE 2
Using Closing Balance Sheet Figures Where Average Would Be More Accurate
For ratios like ROCE or Asset Turnover, using an average of opening and closing capital employed (where data is available) gives a more accurate picture than just the closing figure, especially if there were major changes during the year (e.g. a large acquisition near year-end). Many exam answers accept closing figures for simplicity, but the concept should be understood.
❌ MISTAKE 3
Comparing Ratios Across Companies With Different Accounting Policies
A company using FIFO vs one using AVCO, or one that capitalises R&D vs one that expenses it, will show different ratios even with identical underlying economics. Always check accounting policy notes before comparing ratios across companies.
❌ MISTAKE 4
Treating High Gearing as Automatically Bad
Gearing amplifies both good and bad outcomes — high gearing isn't inherently negative if the company generates stable, predictable cash flows to service the debt (e.g. utility companies). The interpretation depends on the industry, cash flow stability, and interest cover — not gearing level in isolation.
❌ MISTAKE 5
Ignoring Seasonality When Interpreting a Single Period's Ratios
A retailer's liquidity ratios at year-end just after the festive season will look very different from mid-year. Ratios should ideally be compared to the same period in prior years, not just the immediately preceding period, to avoid drawing conclusions from seasonal noise.
❌ MISTAKE 6
Using Revenue Instead of COGS/Purchases in Payables Days
Payables Days should ideally use Purchases (or COGS as a proxy) in the denominator — using Revenue mismatches a balance sheet figure related to purchases with an income statement figure related to sales, distorting the ratio's meaning.

Part D — Interview Questions & Model Answers

QuestionWhat They Are TestingModel Answer
"If you could only look at one ratio to assess a company, which would you pick and why?"Prioritisation and depth of understandingThere isn't a single perfect ratio, but ROCE is a strong choice because it links profitability to how efficiently capital is used — combining margin and asset turnover through the DuPont decomposition. That said, I'd want interest cover alongside it to check the company can service its obligations, since a high ROCE built on unsustainable debt is a red flag.
"A company's current ratio has improved from 1.2 to 2.5 over two years — is this necessarily good news?"Testing for nuanced, non-default interpretationNot necessarily — while it suggests improved short-term liquidity, it could also mean the company is accumulating excess inventory or struggling to collect receivables, both of which tie up cash inefficiently rather than reflecting genuinely improved financial health. I'd want to see the quick ratio and inventory/receivables days trends before concluding it's positive.
"How would you explain the Cash Conversion Cycle to a non-finance person?"Communication skill — explaining technical concepts simplyIt's the number of days between when a company pays cash out for inventory and when it collects cash in from customers. A shorter (or negative) cycle means the business needs less of its own cash to fund operations — suppliers effectively finance the working capital gap, which is why companies like Amazon, with negative CCCs, can grow rapidly without needing as much external capital.
