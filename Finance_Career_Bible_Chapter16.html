Technical knowledge gets you in the door. Ethics and professional judgment keep you there — and determine how far you go. Every major accounting scandal — Enron, WorldCom, Satyam, Wirecard — involved qualified, technically competent accountants who made the wrong ethical choice. ACCA embeds ethics into EVERY paper, every level, because it knows technical skill without integrity is dangerous.

"Your technical knowledge tells you WHAT you can do. Your ethics tells you what you SHOULD do. The gap between these two is where careers — and companies — are destroyed."

16.2
The 5 Fundamental Principles
IESBA Code of Ethics

Every ACCA member must adhere to 5 fundamental principles. These are not abstract ideals — they are the practical standard against which every professional decision is judged.

🎯
Integrity
Be straightforward and honest in all professional and business relationships. Don't be associated with information you know to be false or misleading.
⚖️
Objectivity
Do not allow bias, conflict of interest, or undue influence of others to override professional judgments.
📚
Professional Competence & Due Care
Maintain professional knowledge and skill at the level required. Act diligently and in accordance with applicable standards.
🔒
Confidentiality
Respect the confidentiality of information acquired through professional relationships. Don't disclose without proper authority, unless legal/professional duty to do so.
👔
Professional Behaviour
Comply with relevant laws and regulations. Avoid any conduct that discredits the profession — including on social media.
🔑 Memory Aid — "I Own Professional Conduct, Carefully"
Integrity · Objectivity · Professional Competence & Due Care · Confidentiality · Professional Behaviour. These 5 principles appear in EVERY ethics question — examiners often ask you to identify WHICH principle is threatened in a scenario.
16.3
The Conceptual Framework
Threats & Safeguards Approach

Rather than providing a rule for every possible situation, IESBA uses a principles-based "conceptual framework". Accountants must identify threats to the 5 principles, evaluate their significance, and apply safeguards to eliminate or reduce them to an acceptable level.

1
Identify threats — What could compromise the 5 fundamental principles in this situation?
2
Evaluate significance — Is the threat at an "acceptable level," or could it compromise compliance with the principles?
3
Apply safeguards — If significant, identify and apply safeguards to eliminate the threat or reduce it to acceptable level
4
Reassess — If safeguards are insufficient, decline or end the specific professional activity, or even resign from the engagement/employment
💡 Two Types of Safeguards
Safeguards created by the profession, legislation or regulation: Continuing education requirements, professional standards, monitoring and disciplinary processes, external review of reports.

Safeguards in the work environment: Quality control policies, conflict resolution processes, second partner review, rotation of senior personnel, disclosure to those charged with governance.
16.4
The 5 Categories of Ethical Threats

Every ethical dilemma in ACCA exams (and real life) falls into one or more of these 5 threat categories. Learning to RECOGNISE these categories is the most exam-relevant ethics skill.

1. Self-Interest Threat
A financial or other interest will inappropriately influence judgment or behaviour.
e.g. Auditor holding shares in the audit client
2. Self-Review Threat
Not appropriately evaluating results of a previous judgment or service performed by yourself or your firm.
e.g. Auditing financial statements you helped prepare
3. Advocacy Threat
Promoting a client's or employer's position to the point that objectivity is compromised.
e.g. Representing a client in a legal dispute against tax authority
4. Familiarity Threat
A long or close relationship leads to being too sympathetic or accepting of others' work.
e.g. Audit partner's close family member is the client's CFO
5. Intimidation Threat
Being deterred from acting objectively due to actual or perceived pressure.
e.g. Client threatens to switch auditors over a disagreement
🔑 Memory Aid — "SHAFI"
Self-Interest · H(Self-Review, think "Have I reviewed myself?") · Advocacy · Familiarity · Intimidation. In exams, scenarios often contain MULTIPLE threats simultaneously — practice spotting all of them, not just the obvious one.
16.5
Resolving Ethical Dilemmas
Step-by-Step Approach

When facing a real ethical dilemma at work — or answering an exam question — follow this structured approach.

1️⃣
Gather the Facts
  • What exactly is the situation?
  • Who are the stakeholders involved?
  • What information is missing?
2️⃣
Identify the Ethical Issues
  • Which of the 5 principles are at risk?
  • Which threat category applies?
  • Is this a legal issue too, or purely ethical?
3️⃣
Consider the Code & Firm Policies
  • What does the IESBA Code say?
  • What do internal firm policies require?
  • Are there relevant laws/regulations?
4️⃣
Identify Alternative Courses of Action
  • What options are available?
  • What are the consequences of each?
  • Who should be consulted (manager, ethics hotline)?
5️⃣
Document and Act
  • Document the issue and how it was resolved
  • Apply safeguards or escalate as needed
  • If unresolved — consider resignation as last resort
📌 "Acting in the Public Interest" — The Tiebreaker
When in doubt, ask: "What would protect the public interest?" This includes investors, employees, creditors, and the wider community — not just the immediate employer or client. This principle ultimately overrides loyalty to any single party, including your employer.
16.6
The EPSM Module — What to Expect

The Ethics and Professional Skills Module (EPSM) is an online, interactive, self-paced module — not a traditional exam. It's typically completed alongside Strategic Professional papers and is mandatory for ACCA membership.

🎮
Format
  • Interactive scenarios — you make choices, see consequences
  • Video content featuring real practitioners
  • Self-assessment quizzes throughout
  • Approximately 20 hours total — can be done in stages
Online & Interactive
📋
Content Areas
  • Ethical decision-making in realistic scenarios
  • Communication skills — written and verbal
  • Commercial acumen and business awareness
  • Leadership, innovation, and problem-solving
Holistic Coverage
⏰
When to Complete
    • Can be started any time after registration
    • Best done alongside or after Strategic Professional papers
    • Must be completed before applying for membership
    • No exam fee — included in subscription

    Professional Skills
    The Career Differentiators

    Beyond ethics, EPSM and Strategic Professional exams test specific professional skills that employers consistently rank as MORE important than technical knowledge for career progression.

    💬
    Communication
    Adapting tone, format, and complexity for different audiences — explaining technical accounting issues to a non-finance CEO requires a completely different approach than writing for an audit file.
    🔍
    Professional Scepticism
    Questioning assumptions, looking for evidence rather than accepting explanations at face value — the core mindset of every great auditor and analyst.
    💼
    Commercial Acumen
    Understanding how a business actually makes money, its competitive position, and how accounting decisions affect real business outcomes — not just technical compliance.
    🧩
    Analysis & Evaluation
    Breaking down complex problems, weighing evidence, and forming reasoned conclusions — especially when information is incomplete or conflicting.
    💡
    Innovation
    Identifying opportunities to use technology, new approaches, or creative solutions to improve processes — increasingly important as AI transforms finance (Chapter 42).
    👥
    Leadership
    Even in junior roles — taking initiative, managing your own work, supporting team members, and building toward people-management responsibilities.
    🎯 Why This Matters More Than You Think
    Big 4 promotion criteria from Senior Associate → Manager → Senior Manager weight communication, leadership, and commercial acumen HEAVILY — often more than pure technical knowledge at those levels (technical competence is assumed). Students who treat EPSM as a "tick-box" exercise miss a genuine opportunity to develop the skills that determine whether they reach ₹20 LPA or ₹80 LPA by year 7.

    In nearly every major accounting scandal, the technical accounting issue was SECONDARY — the root cause was an ethical failure: someone knew something was wrong and either participated, stayed silent, or didn't look hard enough. ACCA's emphasis on ethics exists precisely because technical brilliance cannot substitute for integrity.

    Common Mistakes, Flashcards & Interview Q&A

    Part A — Common Mistakes (Don't Repeat These)

    ❌ MISTAKE 1
    Identifying Only One Threat When Multiple Exist
    Exam scenarios frequently contain more than one simultaneous threat (e.g. a self-interest threat AND a familiarity threat in the same situation). Stopping analysis after finding the first, most obvious threat misses marks allocated to identifying all relevant threats.
    ❌ MISTAKE 2
    Jumping Straight to Safeguards Without Naming the Threat First
    The conceptual framework requires identifying the specific threat (and which fundamental principle it endangers) BEFORE proposing safeguards. Proposing generic safeguards without properly naming the threat category loses structured marks.
    ❌ MISTAKE 3
    Confusing Objectivity and Integrity
    Integrity is about being honest and not associating with false/misleading information. Objectivity is specifically about not letting bias or undue influence affect judgment. A scenario involving a conflict of interest tests Objectivity primarily, not Integrity — mixing these up is a common exam error.
    ❌ MISTAKE 4
    Assuming Confidentiality is Absolute
    Confidentiality has recognised exceptions — where disclosure is required by law, permitted by law and authorised by the client/employer, or there is a professional duty/right to disclose (e.g. reporting suspected money laundering). Treating confidentiality as an unbreakable rule in every scenario is incorrect.
    ❌ MISTAKE 5
    Treating Ethics as a "Soft," Low-Mark Topic
    Ethics is embedded across nearly every ACCA paper, including heavily in SBL and SBR at Strategic Professional level, and directly examined in EPSM. Deprioritising ethics revision in favour of purely technical topics is a strategic study mistake — ethics marks are often easier to secure with structured technique.

    Part C — Interview Questions & Model Answers

    QuestionWhat They Are TestingModel Answer
    "Describe an ethical dilemma you've faced (academic, work, or personal) and how you resolved it."Practical application of ethical reasoning — a very common Big 4/finance interview questionA strong answer describes a specific situation, identifies the competing pressures involved (even informally mapping to a "threat"), explains the reasoning process used to reach a decision, and reflects on the outcome — showing structured ethical thinking rather than just describing what happened.
    "Why do you think ethics is emphasised so heavily in the ACCA qualification?"Understanding of the profession's foundational trust requirementThe entire value of a professional accountant's opinion — whether an audit report, a tax filing, or financial advice — depends on stakeholders trusting that opinion is objective and honest. Without a strong ethical framework, that trust would collapse, undermining the credibility of financial markets that rely on audited, reliable information.
    "If a client pressured you to overlook a small accounting irregularity, what would you do?"Practical judgment and willingness to act on principle under pressureI'd first assess the materiality and nature of the irregularity, then apply the conceptual framework — identifying this as an intimidation and/or self-interest threat to objectivity and integrity. I'd document the concern, escalate internally to a senior colleague or ethics function if needed, and if the pressure continued, decline to be associated with the misleading information — even if it meant losing the client relationship.
