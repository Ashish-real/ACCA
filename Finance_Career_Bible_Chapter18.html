18.1
What Is Financial Analysis, Who Uses It & Why It Matters

Financial Analysis is the process of evaluating a company's financial statements to assess its performance, financial health, risk profile, and future prospects. It transforms raw accounting data into actionable business insight that stakeholders use to make decisions worth millions — or billions.

Unlike bookkeeping or accounting (which records what happened), financial analysis asks: What does this mean? Is it good or bad? Why did it happen? What will happen next? These are the questions that make financial analysts indispensable in every organisation.

🏦
Who Uses Financial Analysis
  • Investors & Fund Managers — Should I buy, hold, or sell this stock? Is this company undervalued?
  • Banks & Lenders — Should we give this company a ₹500Cr loan? Can they repay it?
  • Big 4 Auditors — Is this company a going concern? Are their financials fairly presented?
  • Big 4 Advisory — What is this company worth? Should our client acquire it?
  • FP&A Teams — How is our business performing vs budget? Where are we over/under?
  • Credit Rating Agencies — Should this company be rated AAA or BB?
  • Government & Regulators — Is this company compliant? Are they paying correct taxes?
  • Management — Are we hitting our targets? Which division is underperforming?
Universal Skill
🎯
What Financial Analysis Answers
  • Profitability — Is the company making money? Are margins growing or shrinking?
  • Liquidity — Can it pay its bills next month? Does it have enough working capital?
  • Solvency — Can it survive long-term? Is it over-leveraged with debt?
  • Efficiency — Is it using its assets well? Are receivables being collected on time?
  • Growth — Is revenue growing? At what rate? Is it sustainable?
  • Earnings Quality — Is the profit real? Is cash flow backing it up?
  • Valuation — Is the company cheap or expensive relative to peers?
  • Risk — What are the financial risks? What could go wrong?
8 Key Questions
💼
Why This Is Critical for Your Career
  • Tested directly in ACCA FR, FM, and SBR — often 20–30 marks per paper
  • Every Big 4 interview asks: "Tell me about a company and its financial performance"
  • FP&A roles: you analyse actuals vs budget every single month
  • Audit: analytical procedures use ratio analysis in every engagement
  • Advisory: financial due diligence is purely financial analysis at depth
  • The skill that separates accountants from finance professionals
  • You started at 17 — master this and you'll outthink people twice your age
Career Essential
18.2
The Three Financial Statements — The Analyst's Lens
Not accounting — analysis

You covered the preparation of financial statements in Chapters 3–11. Now we look at them differently — through the analyst's eyes. An analyst does not prepare statements. An analyst interrogates them.

StatementWhat It ShowsAnalyst's Primary QuestionsKey Risk if Ignored
Income Statement (P&L)Revenue, costs, and profit over a period — the story of trading performanceIs revenue growing? Are margins expanding or compressing? Where are costs rising? Is profit sustainable?Miss margin compression — company looks profitable but profitability is eroding quietly
Balance Sheet (SoFP)What the company owns (assets), owes (liabilities), and net worth (equity) at one point in timeHow much debt? Is leverage increasing? Are assets being used efficiently? Is equity growing?Miss over-leverage — P&L looks healthy but the company is drowning in debt
Cash Flow StatementActual cash moving in and out — operating, investing, and financing activitiesIs profit turning into cash? Is operating cash flow positive? Is the company burning cash? How is growth being funded?Miss the Enron/Satyam problem — profit exists on paper but company has no real cash
🔗 The Golden Rule — Always Read All Three Together
A company can show profit on the P&L but be running out of cash. Or show a strong balance sheet but have declining revenue. Or show great cash flow but be burning through assets. No single statement tells the full story. The three statements are like three witnesses to a crime — you need all three testimonies to know the truth. Senior analysts triangulate across all three before forming any conclusion.

How the Three Statements Connect

🔗 The Linkages Every Analyst Must Know

P&L → Balance Sheet: Net Profit flows into Retained Earnings (Equity) on the Balance Sheet. Profit grows equity.
Cash Flow → Balance Sheet: Closing cash on the Cash Flow Statement = Cash line on the Balance Sheet.
Balance Sheet → Cash Flow: Changes in working capital (receivables, inventory, payables) appear in Operating Cash Flow.
P&L → Cash Flow: Net Profit is the starting point of the indirect cash flow statement. D&A is added back.
If these linkages don't reconcile → there is an error (or fraud) somewhere.
18.3
How to Read an Annual Report Like an Analyst
Beyond the numbers

A company's financial statements are only a portion of its Annual Report. An experienced analyst reads the entire annual report — not just the numbers — because the non-financial sections often contain the most important context for understanding the numbers. Here is the professional approach.

Step 1
Chairman's / MD's Letter to Shareholders — Read this first. It gives the management's narrative of the year. Look for: How do they describe performance? Are they honest about challenges or spinning bad news? Compare what they say to what the numbers actually show. Inconsistency between narrative and numbers = red flag.
Step 2
Management Discussion & Analysis (MD&A) — This is the most analytically rich section. Management explains: business segments, key drivers of performance, risks they face, outlook for next year. Read it carefully — look for what they emphasise, what they downplay, and what they don't mention at all. Things left unsaid are often as important as things said.
Step 3
Auditor's Report — Check: Is it unqualified (clean)? Or does it contain qualifications, emphasis of matter paragraphs, or key audit matters? An emphasis of matter or going concern note is a serious signal. Key Audit Matters tell you what the auditor considered highest risk — these are exactly what analysts should focus on too.
Step 4
Accounting Policies (Note 1 / Note 2) — How does the company recognise revenue? What depreciation method do they use? How do they value inventory? These choices directly affect the numbers. A change in accounting policy mid-year can make profits look better artificially — always check if policies changed from prior year.
Step 5
Notes to the Financial Statements — The notes contain: breakdown of revenue by segment/geography, details of debt (terms, maturity, covenants), related party transactions, contingent liabilities (legal cases, guarantees), and detailed asset schedules. Companies sometimes bury bad news in the notes hoping nobody reads them. Analysts always read the notes.
Step 6
Related Party Transactions — Look for transactions between the company and its directors, promoters, or subsidiaries. Are they at market terms? Inflated related-party transactions are a common method of siphoning funds. In India, promoter-related transactions deserve extra scrutiny.
Step 7
Contingent Liabilities — These are potential liabilities not yet on the Balance Sheet — ongoing legal cases, tax disputes, guarantees given to subsidiaries. A company might look debt-free on the balance sheet but have ₹5,000Cr of contingent liabilities. Always check. These can crystallise and devastate a company.
Step 8
Five-Year Financial Summary — Most annual reports include a 5-year summary table. This is gold for trend analysis — you can immediately see revenue CAGR, margin trajectory, debt levels, and EPS growth over 5 years without building your own model.
💡 The Analogy — Annual Report as Iceberg
The financial statements (P&L, BS, CF) are the tip of the iceberg — visible, clean, formatted. The MD&A, notes, accounting policies, and contingent liabilities are the 90% below the water — where the real story lives. Inexperienced analysts only read the tip. Senior analysts dive deep below the surface.
18.4
Horizontal Analysis — Trends, CAGR & Multi-Year Reading
How is the company changing over time?

Horizontal Analysis compares financial data across multiple time periods — usually year-on-year. It tells you the direction, speed, and consistency of change. Is revenue growing? Are costs rising faster than revenue? Is debt increasing every year? One year is a snapshot. Three to five years is a trend — and trends are what analysts care about.

Horizontal Analysis — Key Formulas
Year-on-Year Change (%)
= (Current Year Value − Prior Year Value) ÷ Prior Year Value × 100
Most commonly used. Shows % change from one year to next.
Absolute Change (₹)
= Current Year Value − Prior Year Value
Gives the magnitude of change in money terms — useful alongside %
CAGR (Compound Annual Growth Rate)
= (Ending Value ÷ Beginning Value)^(1/n) − 1 × 100
n = number of years. Best measure of long-term growth rate. Smooths out volatile individual years.
Index Number (Base Year = 100)
= (Current Year Value ÷ Base Year Value) × 100
Converts all years to a common base. Makes multi-year comparison visual and intuitive.

Worked Example — Infosys Revenue Trend (5 Years)

YearRevenue (₹ Cr)YoY ChangeIndex (FY20=100)Analyst Signal
FY202090,791Base Year100Base established
FY20211,00,472+10.7%111Steady growth — COVID resilience
FY20221,21,641+21.1%134Strong acceleration — digital demand boom
FY20231,46,767+20.6%162Growth sustained — deal wins strong
FY20241,53,670+4.7%169Sharp deceleration — investigate macro/demand

📊 CAGR Calculation (FY2020 → FY2024)

CAGR = (1,53,670 ÷ 90,791)^(1/4) − 1
= (1.6927)^(0.25) − 1
= 1.1408 − 1
= 14.1% per year — strong 4-year growth story
🔍 Analyst Insight — Growth Rate Deceleration Is A Critical Signal
Revenue grew at 20%+ for two years, then crashed to 4.7% in FY24. This deceleration is more important than any single year's number. It signals: client budget cuts (macro slowdown), deal ramp-ups taking longer, or competitive pressure. Infosys management attributed it to client caution in BFSI (banking clients). An analyst must ask: Is this temporary (1-2 quarter blip) or structural (multi-year trend change)? The answer determines whether the stock is a buying opportunity or a value trap.

What to Apply Horizontal Analysis To

📋
P&L Items — Always Analyse
  • Revenue — growth rate and acceleration/deceleration
  • Gross Profit — is it growing faster or slower than revenue?
  • EBITDA — operating leverage or operating drag?
  • Net Profit — bottom line trend over 3–5 years
  • EPS — earnings per share trend (key for investor analysis)
🏛️
Balance Sheet Items — Always Analyse
  • Total Debt — is it increasing every year? Alarming if yes
  • Cash & Equivalents — growing or declining?
  • Trade Receivables — rising faster than revenue = bad sign
  • Inventory — piling up = demand slowdown or poor management
  • Equity — growing from retained profits = healthy
💰
Cash Flow Items — Always Analyse
  • Operating Cash Flow — consistent or volatile?
  • Free Cash Flow (OCF minus Capex) — growing over time?
  • Capex — is the company investing in growth or cutting back?
  • Dividends paid — stable, growing, or erratic?
  • OCF vs Net Profit ratio — earnings quality trend
18.5
Vertical Analysis — Common-Size Statements
Structure and composition

Vertical Analysis expresses every line item as a percentage of a base figure — Revenue on the P&L, or Total Assets on the Balance Sheet. This removes the distortion of company size, making it possible to compare a ₹500Cr company with a ₹50,000Cr company fairly. It also reveals whether the cost structure is changing over time — even when absolute numbers are growing.

📋
Common-Size Income Statement
  • Base = Revenue (100%)
  • COGS % — what portion of revenue goes to production cost
  • Gross Profit % — gross margin
  • Opex % — what % is spent on operating costs
  • EBIT % — operating profit margin
  • Net Profit % — bottom line margin
  • Key question: is COGS% rising? That means margin compression.
Profitability Structure
🏛️
Common-Size Balance Sheet
  • Base = Total Assets (100%)
  • Current Assets % — how liquid is the asset base?
  • Fixed Assets % — how capital-intensive is the business?
  • Debt % of total assets — how leveraged?
  • Equity % — how much is shareholder-funded?
  • Key question: is debt % rising? That's growing financial risk.
Capital Structure
🔍
Why Vertical Analysis Is Powerful
  • Compare companies of completely different sizes fairly
  • Spot if COGS is creeping up as % of revenue year-on-year
  • See if a company is becoming more or less debt-dependent
  • Track operating leverage — does opex% fall as revenue scales?
  • Standard tool in Big 4 audit analytical procedures
  • Used in ACCA FR questions — "prepare and comment on common-size"
Comparison Tool

Overall: quality improving within a modest growth environment. Retail and Jio segments driving margin improvement.
18.6
Cross-Sectional Analysis — Peer Comparison
How does it compare to competitors?

No ratio or trend exists in isolation — it only has meaning when compared to something. Cross-sectional analysis compares a company to its industry peers at the same point in time. It answers: Is this company better or worse than its competitors? Is it gaining or losing competitive advantage?

How to Do a Proper Peer Comparison

Step 1
Choose Genuine Peers — Compare companies with similar business models, sizes, and geographies. TCS vs Infosys vs Wipro is a valid peer group. TCS vs Tata Steel is not — different industries entirely. For Indian IT, the peer group is TCS, Infosys, Wipro, HCL Tech, Tech Mahindra.
Step 2
Adjust for Accounting Policy Differences — If one company uses SLM depreciation and another uses WDV, their asset values and profits are not directly comparable. Good analysts adjust for these differences before comparing — or at minimum acknowledge them when interpreting.
Step 3
Use the Same Metrics for All Peers — Calculate identical ratios for every company in the peer group. Don't use EBITDA margin for one and Net Profit margin for another. Consistency is essential for a fair comparison.
Step 4
Identify Outliers and Ask Why — If Company A has 25% net margin and all peers have 15%, ask why. Either Company A has a genuine competitive advantage (pricing power, cost structure, IP) or something in their accounting is inflating margins. Both possibilities must be investigated.

Example — Indian IT Sector Peer Comparison (Illustrative FY2024)

🔍 Analyst Insight From This Peer Table
TCS leads on margins (19.1% NPM) and scale, reflecting brand premium and client stickiness. HCL Tech is the growth leader (+8.4%) driven by its engineering services and products business. Infosys and Wipro are both decelerating — Wipro most severely (+1.2%). All four are virtually debt-free (D/E <0.15x) — typical for asset-light Indian IT. An investor choosing between these four would focus on: which company can re-accelerate growth soonest while maintaining margins?
18.7
Segment Analysis — Reading Divisional Breakdowns
Where is growth really coming from?

Large companies operate across multiple business segments or geographies. The consolidated P&L gives you the total picture — but segment analysis reveals which parts of the business are growing, which are declining, and which are dragging overall performance. IFRS 8 (Operating Segments) requires listed companies to disclose segment data in their notes — and analysts mine this data for insight.

🏢
Why Segment Analysis Matters
  • Consolidated revenue of ₹1,00,000Cr growing 10% looks uniform — but Segment A might be growing 25% while Segment B is declining 5%
  • The declining segment is hidden by the growing one in consolidated numbers
  • Segment margins reveal cross-subsidisation — a profitable segment funding a loss-making one
  • Geographic segments show exposure to high-risk or high-growth markets
  • Helps identify which segment drives value — and which destroys it
Hidden Insight
📊
What to Look For in Segment Data
  • Revenue mix shift — is the company moving toward higher-margin segments?
  • Segment EBIT margins — which segment is most profitable?
  • Segment capital employed — which segment requires most investment?
  • Intersegment eliminations — how much internal trading distorts segment revenues?
  • Segment asset base — are assets concentrated in one area?
5 Key Angles

🔍 Analyst Reading of Reliance Segments
O2C (petrochemicals/refining) is the biggest revenue contributor but has the lowest margin (9.5%) and near-flat growth (+2.1%) — it is a mature, commoditised business. Jio is the jewel — 19% EBIT margin and strong growth, and growing fast. Retail is a high-growth engine (+17.3%) though margins are thinner. Oil & Gas Exploration has extraordinary margins (61%) but is small. The key analytical insight: Reliance's future value creation is in Jio and Retail, not O2C — which dominates revenue but is low-quality earnings. This is invisible from consolidated numbers alone.
18.8
Quality of Revenue Analysis
Is this revenue real and sustainable?

Not all revenue is equal. ₹100Cr from a 5-year contracted client is very different from ₹100Cr from a one-time project. Quality of Revenue analysis looks beyond the top-line number to understand how reliable, predictable, and sustainable the revenue actually is.

🔁
Recurring vs One-Off Revenue
  • Recurring — subscription fees, maintenance contracts, retainers, SaaS licenses. Highly predictable. High quality.
  • One-off — one-time project fees, asset sales, government grants, litigation settlements. Cannot be relied upon for next year.
  • Red flag: company reports profit growth driven by one-off items. Strip these out — what is the underlying recurring performance?
  • Indian IT companies report "deal wins" — contracted future revenue. Large deal wins = high revenue quality signal.
Predictability
🌱
Organic vs Inorganic Growth
  • Organic growth — comes from selling more of existing products/services to existing and new customers. Sustainable, reflects real competitive strength.
  • Inorganic growth — comes from acquisitions. Revenue jumps because you bought a company, not because the business got stronger.
  • Always separate the two: "Ex-acquisition revenue growth was X%" is the real number.
  • Serial acquirers can show consistent revenue growth that masks stagnant organic performance.
Growth Quality
📜
Contracted vs Spot Revenue
  • Contracted — customer has signed a multi-year agreement. Analyst can forecast this with confidence. Airlines: long-term freight contracts. IT: multi-year outsourcing deals.
  • Spot — transactional, no long-term commitment. Highly volatile. Commodity traders, spot shipping, project-based firms.
  • Companies with high contracted revenue deserve premium valuation — earnings are more visible and less risky.
Visibility
🌍
Geographic & Customer Concentration
  • If 60% of revenue comes from one customer — that customer leaving = catastrophic
  • If 80% of revenue comes from the US — USD/INR movement directly hits profitability
  • Look for: "Top 5 customers" and "Revenue by geography" in notes
  • High concentration = high risk even if current revenue looks stable
  • Diversification across customers and geographies = higher revenue quality

18.9
The 7-Step Analytical Framework
How senior analysts structure their work

Professional analysts don't randomly calculate ratios and read numbers. They follow a structured, top-down framework that moves from big picture to specific findings. Using this framework makes your analysis complete, coherent, and presentable — in exams and in the real world.

Step 1
Understand the Business First — Before Touching Any Numbers
What industry is this? What is the business model — how does it make money? Who are the key customers? Who are the competitors? What macro factors affect it (interest rates, commodity prices, regulation, FX)? A retail company and an IT company have completely different "normal" margins and ratios. Without this context, your analysis will be meaningless.
Step 2
Read the Annual Report Strategically
Chairman's letter, MD&A, auditor's report, accounting policies, key notes. Form an initial view: What is management saying about performance? What risks are they flagging? Any auditor concerns? This qualitative reading frames your quantitative analysis — you know what to look for before you open the spreadsheet.
Step 3
Horizontal Analysis — Identify Key Trends
How has revenue changed over 3–5 years? What is the CAGR? Are costs growing faster than revenue (margin compression)? Is debt increasing? Is cash flow improving or declining? Calculate YoY% for all major P&L, Balance Sheet, and Cash Flow line items. Flag anything that changed significantly — positive or negative.
Step 4
Vertical Analysis — Understand Structure
Express the P&L as % of revenue and Balance Sheet as % of total assets. Has the cost structure changed? Is COGS% rising (input cost pressure)? Is debt as a % of assets increasing (leverage rising)? Compare this year's common-size to prior year — structural changes are often more revealing than absolute number changes.
Step 5
Ratio Analysis — Quantify the Picture
Calculate the key ratios across all five categories (Chapter 19). Don't calculate every ratio ever invented — select those most relevant to your analytical purpose. Profitability ratios if you're assessing investment quality. Liquidity + solvency ratios if you're assessing credit risk. Efficiency ratios if you're assessing operational performance. Always compare to prior years and industry peers.
Step 6
Cash Flow Deep Dive
Does Operating Cash Flow match Net Profit? High profit but negative operating CF = serious red flag (potential earnings manipulation or working capital problem). Is Free Cash Flow (OCF minus Capex) positive and growing? How is the company financing itself — debt, equity, or internal cash generation? Is it paying dividends from real profits or borrowing to pay dividends?
Step 7
Synthesise — Write the Story
Bring everything together into 4–6 key findings. This is the most important step and the one most people skip. Don't just list ratios — tell the story. "Company X is growing revenue at 15% CAGR with improving margins, strong cash generation, and a virtually debt-free balance sheet. However, receivables are rising faster than revenue — suggesting potential collection issues that could pressure cash flow in FY25. The key risk to monitor is..." This is what ACCA SBR markers and Big 4 managers want to see.
18.10
Red Flags — 15 Warning Signs in Financial Statements
What screams danger to an analyst

Red flags are signals in financial statements that indicate potential problems — financial distress, earnings manipulation, operational deterioration, or outright fraud. Learning to spot these is one of the most valuable skills in finance. These are tested in ACCA SBR, used daily by Big 4 auditors, and critical for any analyst role.

🚨 Red Flag 1 — Revenue Growing But Profit Falling
If revenue is growing 15% but net profit is growing only 3% or actually declining — costs are rising faster than revenue. This is margin compression. It could indicate: input cost inflation, wage pressure, aggressive discounting to win revenue, or rising competition forcing price cuts.
Real Example: Zomato in early growth phase — massive revenue growth, massive losses. Revenue growth alone is meaningless without profitability.
🚨 Red Flag 2 — Profit Growing But Operating Cash Flow Declining or Negative
This is the most dangerous red flag. Profit is an accounting number — it can be manipulated. Cash is real. If a company consistently reports profit but generates little or no operating cash flow, something is wrong. Either customers aren't paying (receivables problem), inventory is piling up, or the profit is being manufactured through aggressive revenue recognition.
Real Example: IL&FS (2018 collapse) — showed profits for years while cash flow was deteriorating. The collapse was sudden but the signs were in the cash flow statement.
🚨 Red Flag 3 — Receivables Growing Much Faster Than Revenue
If revenue grows 10% but trade receivables grow 40%, the company is booking revenue that customers haven't paid for — and may never pay. This could indicate: channel stuffing (forcing inventory on distributors), recognising revenue too early, or genuine collection problems with customers in financial difficulty.
Formula to check: Receivables Days (= Receivables ÷ Revenue × 365). If this number rises sharply year-on-year, investigate urgently.
🚨 Red Flag 4 — Inventory Piling Up
Rising Inventory Days (Inventory ÷ COGS × 365) signals either demand is slowing (goods not selling), product is becoming obsolete, or management overproduced. Stale inventory may need to be written down — reducing future profits. In retail, rising inventory relative to revenue is almost always a bad sign.
Check: Is inventory growing faster than revenue? Is the company in a fast-changing industry where products go stale quickly (tech, fashion, FMCG)?
🚨 Red Flag 5 — Debt Increasing Every Year
Some debt is normal and healthy. But if total debt is increasing every year, even as the company reports profits, it means the company is not funding itself from operations. Why is it constantly borrowing? Is it funding losses? Paying dividends from debt? Making acquisitions that don't generate returns? Each possibility requires investigation.
Check: Is Free Cash Flow (OCF minus Capex) consistently positive? If yes, debt should be manageable. If FCF is negative and debt is rising — serious concern.
🚨 Red Flag 6 — Frequent Changes to Accounting Policies
Changing from WDV to SLM depreciation. Changing revenue recognition from percentage of completion to completed contract. Changing inventory valuation from FIFO to weighted average. Each change can boost reported profit without any real business improvement. Companies sometimes change policies strategically to hit profit targets — analysts must adjust for these changes to see the true underlying performance.
Always compare Note 1 (Accounting Policies) across years. Any change must be disclosed — and the financial impact must be quantified.
🚨 Red Flag 7 — Interest Coverage Ratio Below 2x
ICR = EBIT ÷ Interest Expense. If this falls below 2x, the company is generating barely twice its interest cost from operations. A modest downturn in earnings could make it unable to pay interest — triggering covenant breaches and potential default. Below 1x means the company cannot cover its interest from current earnings — it is already technically in distress.
Red zone: ICR < 1.5x. Danger zone: ICR < 1.0x. Watch: Airlines, real estate companies, infrastructure firms — capital-intensive, high-debt sectors.
🚨 Red Flag 8 — Auditor Qualification or Emphasis of Matter
A clean (unqualified) audit opinion means the auditor found no material misstatements. An emphasis of matter, key audit matter on going concern, or qualified opinion is a serious signal. It means the auditor has concerns about specific items — going concern, uncertainty in valuations, significant litigation, or inability to verify certain figures. This is a direct warning to investors and analysts.
Always read the auditor's report — especially for smaller, less-followed companies where institutional analysts are not watching.
🚨 Red Flag 9 — Large Goodwill on Balance Sheet After Acquisitions
Goodwill arises when a company pays more for an acquisition than the fair value of its net assets. Large goodwill signals the company paid a premium believing in future synergies. If those synergies don't materialise, the goodwill must be impaired (written down) — causing a sudden large hit to profits. Companies with goodwill making up 30%+ of total assets are especially vulnerable.
Under IFRS, goodwill must be tested for impairment annually (IAS 36). A sudden goodwill impairment write-off is always a red flag in retrospect.
🚨 Red Flag 10 — Promoter Pledging Shares
In India, when promoters pledge their shares as collateral for loans, it signals they are under financial stress. If the stock price falls, lenders can sell the pledged shares — causing further price decline (a death spiral). Check: SEBI mandates disclosure of promoter pledging. High pledging % (above 30–40% of promoter holdings) is a serious warning sign for Indian investors.
Examples: Several Indian promoter-led companies collapsed after high pledging became public — Yes Bank, DHFL, Zee Entertainment all showed this pattern.
🚨 Red Flag 11 — Revenue from Related Parties at Non-Market Terms
If a company reports revenue from transactions with its own promoter, subsidiaries, or directors — and those transactions are at above-market prices — it may be inflating revenue artificially. Related party transactions must be disclosed in notes. An unusually high proportion of revenue from related parties is a signal of potential manipulation.
Check: Are related party revenues growing when external revenues are flat? Are the terms disclosed as "at arm's length"? Is the auditor flagging related party risks as a key audit matter?
🚨 Red Flag 12 — Negative Working Capital Worsening
For most businesses (not retail), working capital should be positive. If working capital is turning negative and worsening, the company is relying on short-term creditor financing to fund operations — which is fine if deliberate (like FMCG companies) but dangerous if it's because customers aren't paying and suppliers are demanding faster payment.
Calculate: Working Capital = Current Assets − Current Liabilities. Track this over 3–5 years. Sudden deterioration demands explanation.
🚨 Red Flag 13 — Sudden Change in Auditor
When a company suddenly changes its audit firm — especially mid-year or after a long relationship with a Big 4 firm — it is sometimes because the auditor raised concerns that management didn't want on record. Not always sinister (cost savings, rotation requirements), but when combined with other red flags, it warrants serious scrutiny.
In India, mandatory auditor rotation every 5 years for listed companies (Companies Act 2013). But mid-term changes outside of rotation are worth investigating.
🚨 Red Flag 14 — Profit Much Higher in Q4 Than Prior Quarters
If a company consistently books disproportionate profit in Q4 (year-end quarter), it suggests management is making aggressive year-end accounting entries — accelerating revenue recognition, deferring expenses, or reversing provisions — to hit full-year targets. Genuine business performance should be more evenly distributed across quarters.
Check: Quarterly results filings (BSE/NSE). Compare Q4 revenue and margin to Q1–Q3. A sudden Q4 spike every year is a pattern worth questioning.
🚨 Red Flag 15 — Large and Growing Contingent Liabilities
Contingent liabilities — disclosed in notes but not on the balance sheet — can be enormous. Legal cases, tax disputes, guarantees to subsidiaries, environmental liabilities. A company may appear debt-free on the balance sheet but have ₹10,000Cr of contingent liabilities that could crystallise. Always read the contingent liabilities note — and track whether the amounts are growing.
Vodafone India had AGR dues that were contingent for years — then crystallised into an actual ₹58,000Cr liability. Companies often fight tax disputes for decades before they become real.
18.11
Green Flags — What a Financially Healthy Company Looks Like
Positive signals to look for

Just as important as spotting red flags is recognising genuine financial strength. Here are the signals that indicate a company is in excellent financial health — the characteristics of companies that create long-term value.

✅ Revenue AND Profit Both Growing — With Stable or Improving Margins
The gold standard — top-line growth accompanied by profitability growth at the same or faster rate. This means the company has pricing power, cost discipline, and genuine competitive advantage. If net profit margin is expanding as revenue grows, the company is achieving operating leverage.
✅ Operating Cash Flow Consistently Exceeds Net Profit
When OCF > Net Profit every year, it means profit is fully backed by real cash — and then some. The company is collecting from customers faster than it's recognising revenue, and/or D&A (non-cash) adds back substantially. This is the hallmark of high earnings quality. TCS, HDFC Bank, Asian Paints all show this consistently.
✅ Declining or Stable Debt-to-Equity Over Time
A company that is gradually paying down debt while growing revenue is strengthening its balance sheet from operating cash flows. This means the business model is self-funding — it doesn't need to keep borrowing to grow. This is a strong signal of financial discipline and business quality.
✅ ROCE Consistently Above Cost of Capital (WACC)
Return on Capital Employed exceeding the Weighted Average Cost of Capital means the company is generating more return than what its capital costs — it is genuinely creating economic value. Companies that do this consistently for 10+ years are exceptional wealth creators. This is Warren Buffett's primary investment criterion.
✅ Receivables Days Stable or Declining
If customers are paying faster (or at the same rate) as the company grows revenue, it means the company has strong customer relationships, no collection problems, and possibly growing pricing power (customers pay early for discounts). Declining receivables days while revenue grows = excellent cash conversion.
✅ Strong and Growing Free Cash Flow
Free Cash Flow = Operating Cash Flow − Capex. A company that generates strong FCF can: pay dividends, buy back shares, make acquisitions, reduce debt, or invest in R&D — all without borrowing. FCF is the purest measure of financial health. Consistently growing FCF = compounding value creation.
✅ Clean Auditor's Report — Year After Year
A clean, unqualified audit opinion from a reputable (ideally Big 4) firm, year after year, with no emphasis of matter or key audit matters around going concern or valuation uncertainty, is a basic but important signal of financial statement reliability.
✅ Low Customer and Geographic Concentration
When no single customer represents more than 10–15% of revenue, and revenue is spread across multiple geographies, the company is resilient to single-event shocks. Diversification of revenue is a quality signal — it means the business has genuine market depth and is not dependent on any single relationship.
18.12
Common Mistakes in Financial Analysis
What fails candidates and analysts
❌
Reading Only One Statement
  • Always read P&L + Balance Sheet + Cash Flow together
  • Profit without cash flow is meaningless
  • Revenue growth without margin analysis is misleading
  • Each statement validates or challenges the others
❌
Ignoring Industry Context
  • A 5% net margin is terrible for software, fine for grocery retail
  • Current ratio of 0.8 — dangerous for manufacturer, normal for supermarket
  • Always benchmark against industry norms first
  • Business model determines what "normal" looks like
❌
Not Explaining WHY
  • In ACCA exams: calculation is 40% of marks, explanation is 60%
  • "Current ratio is 1.5" = calculation, not analysis
  • "Current ratio fell from 2.1 to 1.5 due to short-term debt drawn for new plant construction" = analysis
  • Always explain the business reason behind the number
❌
Not Adjusting for One-Off Items
  • A company sold its HQ building — profit is inflated this year
  • Strip out non-recurring items to see underlying performance
  • Analysts focus on "normalised" or "recurring" earnings
  • This year's exceptional item is not next year's profit
❌
Using Only One Year of Data
  • One year is a snapshot — three to five years is a trend
  • A great single year can mask structural decline
  • Always request minimum 3 years of data
  • COVID years (FY2020, FY2021) were distorted — use 5-year CAGRs to normalise
❌
Skipping the Notes to Accounts
  • Notes contain crucial context not in the main statements
  • Accounting policies, contingent liabilities, related party transactions
  • Companies hide bad news in notes hoping nobody reads them
  • Auditors and senior analysts always read notes — always

ACCA Exam Technique for Financial Analysis Questions
FR · SBR · FM · PM

Financial analysis questions appear across multiple ACCA papers — each with a different focus and format. Here's exactly how to approach each paper's style to maximise your marks.

ACCA PaperHow Analysis Is TestedKey TechniqueCommon Marks Available
FR (Financial Reporting)Prepare ratios from given financial statements, then interpret and compare 2 years or 2 companiesAlways calculate + interpret. Comment on accounting policy impact on ratios. Read notes.15–25 marks
SBR (Strategic Business Reporting)Integrated case study — analyse financials in the context of a business scenario, M&A, or ethical issueGo beyond numbers — discuss earnings quality, strategy implications, IFRS adjustments. Write like a CFO report.20–30 marks
FM (Financial Management)Ratio analysis linked to capital structure decisions, dividend policy, working capital managementLink ratios to decisions — "gearing is high, therefore the company should consider equity financing rather than more debt"10–20 marks

The Perfect ACCA Analysis Answer — Structure

📋 Universal Answer Template — Use Every Time

1. CALCULATE: Show the formula clearly, then compute. Show both years if comparison needed.
2. STATE THE CHANGE: "The ratio improved/deteriorated from X in FY23 to Y in FY24."
3. INTERPRET: "This suggests that the company's [profitability / liquidity / efficiency] has [improved / worsened]."
4. EXPLAIN WHY: "This is likely due to [rising material costs / new long-term debt raised / slower customer collections]."
5. IMPLICATION: "This may concern [investors / lenders / management] because [specific reason]."
6. LINK (SBR only): "In the context of the proposed acquisition, this declining liquidity ratio raises questions about..."
💡 ACCA FR Exam Tip — The "So What" Test
After writing any ratio-related sentence, ask yourself: "So what?" If your sentence is "The current ratio is 1.8" — so what? That's just a number. Force yourself to complete the thought: "The current ratio is 1.8, which is below the prior year's 2.3, suggesting that liquidity has deteriorated — likely due to the short-term debt drawn to fund the new warehouse. Management should monitor this closely as the ratio is approaching the 1.5x bank covenant threshold." That's the answer that gets full marks.

Full Case Study — Tata Motors End-to-End Analysis
All techniques applied together

Let's apply every technique from this chapter to Tata Motors — one of India's most interesting and complex companies, with multiple businesses (JLR, commercial vehicles, passenger vehicles) and a dramatic financial turnaround story. Data is illustrative for learning.

Step 1 — Business Understanding

🏭 Tata Motors — Business Overview

Business: Tata Motors is India's largest commercial vehicle manufacturer and owns Jaguar Land Rover (JLR) — a premium British car brand. Revenue split: ~65% JLR (UK/global), ~35% India (CV + passenger cars).

Key Drivers: JLR performance depends heavily on UK/Europe/China luxury car demand and chip supply (semiconductor shortage was a major issue FY21-22). India business driven by commercial vehicle cycle (infrastructure spend) and EV transition.

Macro Risks: USD/GBP/INR exchange rates, global recession affecting luxury demand, chip shortages, EV transition investment requirements, UK Brexit trade impacts.

Context for Analysis: Tata Motors had a dramatic turnaround — from massive losses in FY2020-21 (COVID + JLR restructuring) to strong profitability in FY2023-24 as JLR recovered and India business boomed.

Step 6 — Analyst Summary

📋 Tata Motors — Full Analyst Conclusion
Tata Motors has executed one of the most impressive corporate financial turnarounds in recent Indian corporate history. From a net loss of ₹11,441Cr in FY22, the company swung to a net profit of ₹31,807Cr in FY24 — a transformation driven primarily by the JLR business recovery (chip shortage resolution, strong SUV demand, pricing discipline) and robust India commercial vehicle cycle.

Strengths: Revenue accelerating (+26% in FY24), gross margins nearly doubled (15% → 24%), active debt reduction (D/E 3.52x → 1.31x), and outstanding cash quality (OCF 2.27x net profit). The balance sheet, while still leveraged, is on a clear improvement trajectory.

Risks to Monitor: D/E at 1.31x remains elevated vs peers. ROCE of ~12.8% is still below the company's cost of capital — value creation is nascent rather than established. JLR remains highly exposed to European and Chinese luxury demand — any macro downturn would pressure the recovery. EV transition requires significant further capex investment. India passenger vehicle segment needs to gain market share against Maruti and Hyundai.

Outlook: If JLR margins hold and India CV cycle continues, Tata Motors is on course to become a sustainably profitable, deleveraging business. Key metrics to track: JLR EBIT margin (target >8%), debt absolute level (<₹1,00,000Cr target), and India EV market share progress.
