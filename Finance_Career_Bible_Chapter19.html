19.1
What Is Ratio Analysis & How to Use It

Ratio Analysis converts raw financial statement numbers into comparable, interpretable metrics. A ratio alone is meaningless — it only becomes useful when compared to something.

📐
The Three Comparisons
  • Time Series — compare to prior years (is it improving?)
  • Cross-sectional — compare to industry peers
  • Targets / Covenants — compare to company goals or bank conditions
  • Always use at least 2 of these 3 in any analysis
Context First
🎯
Choose Ratios by Purpose
  • Credit analysis → liquidity + solvency ratios
  • Investment analysis → profitability + investor ratios
  • Operational review → efficiency ratios
  • Bankruptcy risk → Z-Score + interest coverage
  • ACCA exam → read the question to know which category
Purpose Driven
📝
ACCA Exam Rule
  • Calculation = ~40% of ratio marks
  • Interpretation = ~60% of ratio marks
  • Always state: what, what it means, why it changed
  • "Ratio improved from X to Y, suggesting..." = full marks
  • Never just write the number and move on
Exam Critical
19.2
Category 1 — Profitability Ratios
How well does it earn?
💰
Profitability
Measures how efficiently the company converts revenue into profit. Higher is generally better. Key for investors, management, and ACCA FR/FM papers.
Profitability Ratios — Formulas, Benchmarks & Interpretation
RATIO
FORMULA
BENCHMARK
Gross Profit Margin
Gross Profit ÷ Revenue × 100
Falling = COGS rising faster than revenue. Compare to industry peers.
Operating Profit Margin (EBIT)
EBIT ÷ Revenue × 100
Shows efficiency after opex. Falling = costs out of control.
Net Profit Margin
PAT ÷ Revenue × 100
IT: 15–25%. FMCG: 5–15%. Retail: 2–5%.
ROCE
EBIT ÷ (Total Assets − Current Liabilities) × 100
Must exceed WACC to create value. Most important single ratio.
Return on Equity (ROE)
PAT ÷ Shareholders' Equity × 100
India benchmark: >15% considered good.
Return on Assets (ROA)
PAT ÷ Total Assets × 100
Asset-light companies have higher ROA.
EBITDA Margin
EBITDA ÷ Revenue × 100
Proxy for operating cash generation. Widely used in valuation.
💡 GPM vs NPM Gap
If Gross Profit Margin is 40% but Net Profit Margin is 8%, the company has a cost problem below the gross profit line — high opex, heavy interest burden, or large depreciation. Track where margin is "lost" down the P&L.
19.3
Category 2 — Liquidity Ratios
Can it pay short-term debts?
💧
Liquidity
Measures ability to meet short-term obligations. Critical for creditors, banks, and auditors assessing going concern risk.
Liquidity Ratios
RATIO
FORMULA
BENCHMARK
Current Ratio
Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities
Ideal: 1.5–2.0x. Below 1.0 = danger zone.
Quick Ratio (Acid Test)
(Current Assets − Inventory) ÷ Current Liabilities
Ideal: ≥1.0. Removes illiquid inventory.
Cash Ratio
(Cash + Equivalents) ÷ Current Liabilities
Most conservative. Used in stressed scenarios.
Operating Cash Flow Ratio
Operating Cash Flow ÷ Current Liabilities
Best real-world check — uses actual cash. >1.0 preferred.
⚠️ The Inventory Problem
Current Ratio includes inventory — but inventory is NOT instantly cash. A retailer with ₹500Cr of slow-moving stock looks liquid on Current Ratio but may struggle to pay suppliers. Quick Ratio strips out inventory for a realistic picture. In ACCA FR, always calculate both and comment on the difference.
19.4
Category 3 — Solvency & Leverage Ratios
Can it survive long-term?
🏗️
Solvency & Leverage
Measures long-term financial stability and debt reliance. High leverage amplifies returns but increases financial distress risk.
Solvency & Leverage Ratios
RATIO
FORMULA
BENCHMARK
Debt-to-Equity (D/E)
Total Debt ÷ Shareholders' Equity
IT: <0.5x. Manufacturing: 1–2x. Rising D/E = increasing risk.
Gearing Ratio
Total Debt ÷ (Total Debt + Equity) × 100
<50% = lower risk. >70% = highly leveraged. Key ACCA FM ratio.
Interest Coverage (ICR)
EBIT ÷ Interest Expense
>3x = safe. <1.5x = danger. Lenders set 2x minimum covenant.
Debt-to-Assets
Total Liabilities ÷ Total Assets
<0.5 = healthy. Rising = growing financial risk.
Net Debt / EBITDA
(Total Debt − Cash) ÷ EBITDA
<3x healthy. >5x over-leveraged. Widely used in M&A.
19.5
Category 4 — Efficiency (Activity) Ratios
How well does it use assets?
Efficiency Ratios
RATIO
FORMULA
BENCHMARK
Inventory Days (DIO)
(Inventory ÷ COGS) × 365
FMCG: 30–60d. Manufacturing: 60–120d. Rising = slow sales.
Receivables Days (DSO)
(Trade Receivables ÷ Revenue) × 365
Rising = customers paying late = cash pressure.
Payables Days (DPO)
(Trade Payables ÷ COGS) × 365
Higher = using supplier financing. Too high = damages relationships.
Cash Conversion Cycle
Inventory Days + Receivable Days − Payable Days
Negative = excellent (Amazon). Rising = working capital problem.
Asset Turnover
Revenue ÷ Total Assets
Rising = improving asset utilisation.
Fixed Asset Turnover
Revenue ÷ Net PP&E
Declining = underutilised fixed assets.

What does the market think?
Investor & Market Ratios
RATIO
FORMULA
BENCHMARK
EPS
PAT ÷ No. of Shares Outstanding
Growing EPS = positive. Most reported corporate metric.
P/E Ratio
Market Price ÷ EPS
India avg: 20–25x. High = growth expectation. Compare to sector.

PEG Ratio
P/E ÷ Earnings Growth Rate
<1 = potentially undervalued relative to growth. Adjusts P/E for growth.
19.7
The DuPont Analysis — Linking Ratios Together

DuPont decomposes ROE into three drivers — showing exactly WHERE returns are coming from.

🔗 The DuPont Formula

ROE = Net Profit Margin × Asset Turnover × Financial Leverage
(PAT ÷ Revenue) × (Revenue ÷ Total Assets) × (Total Assets ÷ Equity)
💰
Driver 1 — Profitability
  • Net Profit Margin — profit per ₹1 revenue
  • Improved by: pricing power, cost cuts
  • Apple's strategy: extremely high margins
⚙️
Driver 2 — Efficiency
  • Asset Turnover — revenue per ₹1 of assets
  • Improved by: more revenue or fewer assets
  • Walmart's strategy: high turnover, low margins
🏗️
Driver 3 — Leverage
  • Debt amplification — borrowed money boosts equity returns
  • Higher leverage = higher ROE but higher risk
  • Banks: very high leverage by design

📋 Perfect Ratio Answer Template

1. CALCULATE: State formula + calculate both years clearly.
2. COMPARE: "The ratio improved/deteriorated from X in FY23 to Y in FY24."
3. INTERPRET: "This suggests profitability/liquidity/efficiency has improved/worsened."
4. WHY: "Likely due to rising material costs / new debt / slow collections."

19.9
Limitations of Ratio Analysis
📅
Historical Data Only
  • Ratios based on past statements — say nothing about future
  • A healthy-looking company can collapse next quarter
🎨
Accounting Policy Differences
  • Different depreciation methods change asset values and profit
  • FIFO vs Weighted Average changes COGS and inventory
  • Makes cross-company comparison unreliable
📊
No Absolute Benchmarks
  • No universal "good" ratio — always industry-specific
  • Current ratio 0.8 = dangerous for manufacturer, normal for supermarket
🎭
Window Dressing
  • Companies pay down short-term debt before year-end to boost current ratio
  • Look at quarterly data, not just annual
🔢
Ignores Non-Financial Factors
  • Brand strength, management quality, innovation not captured
  • Qualitative analysis must complement ratios
🏭
Conglomerate Problem
  • Diversified companies — average ratios hide segment problems
  • Always request segment-level data from notes to accounts

🔑 Why D-Mart Has Current Ratio Below 1 — And That's Fine
D-Mart collects cash from customers instantly (retail) but pays suppliers on 30–60 day credit. So current liabilities exceed current assets — yet the business is extremely healthy. Business model determines what "normal" looks like. Never apply manufacturing benchmarks to a retailer.
19.11
Ratio Analysis for Banks & NBFCs
A Completely Different World

Standard ratios do not apply to banks and NBFCs. You cannot calculate a meaningful current ratio or D/E for HDFC Bank — debt is their raw material, not a risk indicator. Banks have their own specialised ratio framework.

Banking-Specific Ratios — India Context (RBI Framework)
RATIO
FORMULA / MEANING
BENCHMARK
NPA Ratio (Gross)
Gross Non-Performing Assets ÷ Gross Advances × 100
% of loans that are bad/defaulting
<2% excellent. >5% = serious concern. PSU banks historically high.
Net NPA Ratio
Net NPAs ÷ Net Advances × 100
After provisions made for bad loans
<1% = well-provisioned bank.
Capital Adequacy Ratio (CAR / CRAR)
Tier 1 + Tier 2 Capital ÷ Risk-Weighted Assets × 100
Buffer to absorb losses — RBI mandated
RBI minimum: 11.5%. Higher = safer bank.
Net Interest Margin (NIM)
(Interest Income − Interest Expense) ÷ Average Earning Assets × 100
Bank's core profitability spread
Private banks: 3–5%. PSU banks: 2–3%.
CASA Ratio
Current + Savings Account Deposits ÷ Total Deposits × 100
Low-cost funding mix — current/savings are cheaper than FDs
>40% = strong. HDFC Bank: ~45%. Lower CASA = higher funding cost.
Credit-to-Deposit Ratio (CDR)
Total Loans ÷ Total Deposits × 100
RBI comfortable range: 70–80%. Too high = liquidity risk.
Return on Assets (ROA) — Banks
Net Profit ÷ Average Total Assets × 100
Top Indian private banks: 1.5–2.5%. PSU banks: 0.5–1%.
Provision Coverage Ratio (PCR)
Provisions Made ÷ Gross NPAs × 100
>70% = well-covered. Higher PCR = bank has cushion for bad loans.
💡 Why NPA Ratio Is The Most Watched Banking Ratio In India
India went through a massive NPA crisis in 2015–2018 where PSU bank NPAs crossed 10–15%. RBI's Asset Quality Review forced banks to recognise hidden bad loans. GNPA ratio rising = bank's loan book is deteriorating = earnings at risk = potential capital raise needed. Every ACCA SBR and FM student should understand this — it's directly tested in financial institutions questions.
19.12
Quality of Earnings Analysis
Is the Profit Real?

Two companies can show identical net profit — but one's earnings are rock solid and the other's are accounting smoke and mirrors. Quality of Earnings analysis separates real cash-backed profit from manipulated accounting profit.

💵
Cash Conversion Ratio
  • Formula: Operating Cash Flow ÷ Net Profit
  • >1.0 = earnings backed by real cash (high quality)
  • <0.7 = profit not converting to cash — investigate
  • Consistently <1 = potential revenue recognition manipulation
  • Warren Buffett uses this before every investment
Most Important QoE Ratio
📐
Accrual Ratio
  • Formula: (Net Income − Operating CF) ÷ Total Assets
  • Measures how much profit is accrual-based vs cash-based
  • Higher accrual ratio = more accounting estimates in profit
  • Negative accrual ratio = cash exceeds profit = excellent quality
  • Used by forensic accountants to detect earnings management
Forensic Tool
🚨
Red Flags — Low Earnings Quality
  • Receivables growing much faster than revenue (booking fake sales)
  • Revenue recognised before cash received — channel stuffing
  • Expenses capitalised as assets to inflate current profit
  • Frequent changes to accounting policies or estimates
  • Auditor qualifications or emphasis of matter paragraphs
  • Related-party transactions at non-market terms
Warning Signs
✅
High Earnings Quality Signals
  • Operating CF consistently exceeds net profit
  • Conservative revenue recognition policy
  • Clean audit report — no qualifications
  • Stable accounting policies across years
  • Receivables days stable or declining
  • EBITDA to free cash flow conversion ratio >80%
Green Flags
🔍 The Satyam Scandal — Quality of Earnings Failure
Satyam Computers (2009) showed ₹5,040Cr of cash on its balance sheet that didn't exist. The company had been inflating revenue and fabricating bank statements for years. A simple check — does operating cash flow match net profit? — would have raised immediate red flags. This is why auditors and analysts must never skip cash flow analysis. Profit without cash is fiction.

Altman Z-Score — Bankruptcy Prediction
Can This Company Survive?

The Altman Z-Score (1968, Edward Altman) is a multi-ratio formula that predicts the probability of corporate bankruptcy within 2 years. It is used by Big 4 auditors for going concern assessments, by banks for credit decisions, and by analysts evaluating distressed companies.

Altman Z-Score Formula — Manufacturing Companies (Original)
COMPONENT
FORMULA
WEIGHT
WHAT IT MEASURES
X1

× 1.2
Short-term liquidity relative to size
X2
Retained Earnings ÷ Total Assets
× 1.4
Cumulative profitability — age and earning power
X3

× 3.3
Operating efficiency — return on assets before leverage
X4

× 0.6
Market's confidence vs debt burden
X5

× 1.0

🎯 Z-Score = 1.2X1 + 1.4X2 + 3.3X3 + 0.6X4 + 1.0X5

Z-Score RangeZoneInterpretation
> 2.99Safe ZoneLow bankruptcy risk — financially healthy
1.81 – 2.99Grey ZoneUncertain — monitor closely, moderate risk
< 1.81Distress ZoneHigh bankruptcy probability within 2 years
💡 Z-Score in Practice — Big 4 Use
ACCA auditors use Z-Score (or modified versions) when assessing going concern under ISA 570. If a company scores in the Distress Zone, the auditor must evaluate whether the going concern assumption is appropriate — and may need to include an emphasis of matter or modified opinion. In credit analysis, banks use Z-Score variants as one input into loan approval decisions.

Common Mistakes, Flashcards & Interview Q&A

Part A — Common Mistakes (Don't Repeat These)

❌ MISTAKE 1
Using Market Cap Instead of Retained Earnings for X2 in Altman Z-Score
The X2 component is specifically Retained Earnings ÷ Total Assets, capturing cumulative reinvested profitability — mixing this up with a market-based measure changes the entire risk assessment and misrepresents the company's earned equity.
❌ MISTAKE 2
Applying the Original Manufacturing Z-Score Formula to Service or Tech Companies Without Adjustment
The original Z-Score was calibrated on manufacturing companies with significant tangible assets. Altman developed modified versions (Z' for private companies, Z'' for non-manufacturers/service firms) — applying the manufacturing formula directly to an asset-light IT company like TCS can give misleading results.
❌ MISTAKE 3
Comparing Bank Ratios to Non-Financial Company Benchmarks
Banks and NBFCs have fundamentally different capital structures (high leverage is normal, not a red flag) and use specific metrics like NPA ratio, CAR, NIM, and CASA that don't apply to regular companies. Using a manufacturing company's "healthy" D/E benchmark on a bank is meaningless.
❌ MISTAKE 4
Ignoring the DuPont Breakdown When Explaining ROE Changes
A change in ROE could come from margin, asset turnover, OR financial leverage changes — each with very different implications. Explaining "ROE improved" without breaking down which DuPont component drove it misses the deeper analytical insight examiners look for.
❌ MISTAKE 5
Treating a Single Ratio in Isolation as Conclusive
A strong current ratio alone doesn't mean a company is financially healthy if its debt-to-equity is dangerously high, or if its quality of earnings is poor. Ratio analysis requires triangulating across categories (profitability, liquidity, gearing, efficiency) — not relying on one favourable number.
❌ MISTAKE 6
Not Adjusting Ratios for Industry Norms
A current ratio of 0.9 might be a red flag for a manufacturer but perfectly normal for a supermarket chain with fast inventory turnover and negative working capital. Applying universal "good" or "bad" thresholds without industry context leads to incorrect conclusions.

Part C — Interview Questions & Model Answers

QuestionWhat They Are TestingModel Answer
"What is the Altman Z-Score and when would you use it?"Awareness of practical distress/credit risk toolsThe Altman Z-Score is a multi-ratio formula combining liquidity, profitability, leverage, market value, and asset efficiency into a single score predicting bankruptcy probability within 2 years. It's used by auditors assessing going concern, banks in credit risk decisions, and analysts screening for financially distressed companies — scores above 2.99 signal safety, below 1.81 signal high distress risk.
"Explain the DuPont analysis and why it's useful."Understanding of ratio decomposition, a favourite technical interview topicDuPont decomposes Return on Equity into three drivers: Net Profit Margin (profitability), Asset Turnover (efficiency), and the Equity Multiplier (financial leverage). It's useful because two companies can have identical ROE for completely different reasons — one might be highly profitable with low leverage, another might be thin-margin but highly leveraged — and DuPont reveals which driver is actually responsible, which matters enormously for assessing sustainability and risk.
"Why can't you compare a bank's financial ratios directly to a manufacturing company's?"Sector-specific financial literacyBanks operate with fundamentally different business models — high leverage is structural (deposits are liabilities funding loan assets), and their key risks (credit quality, interest rate exposure) are captured by specialised metrics like NPA ratio, CAR, and NIM rather than standard gearing or current ratios. Applying manufacturing-company benchmarks to a bank would flag normal banking leverage as dangerously high, which would be a fundamental misread of the sector.
