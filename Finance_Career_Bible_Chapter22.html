Corporate Finance is the field that answers the biggest questions every CFO faces: Where should we invest? How should we fund it? How much should we return to shareholders? Everything in Chapters 18–21 was about understanding and projecting financial performance. Corporate Finance is about making the decisions that shape that performance — at the highest level of any organisation.

"Corporate finance is the science of making decisions that maximise the long-term value of a business for its owners — while balancing the interests of every stakeholder who depends on it."
22.1
What Is Corporate Finance — The Three Core Decisions

Every corporate finance decision falls into one of three categories. These three decisions collectively determine the financial health, growth trajectory, and value of the company. The CFO owns all three.

📈
Decision 1 — The Investment Decision
  • Question: Where should we deploy capital to create the most value?
  • Which projects do we invest in? Which do we reject?
  • Should we build a new factory or acquire a competitor?
  • Should we invest in R&D or return cash to shareholders?
  • Tools: NPV, IRR, Payback Period, ARR — covered in Section 22.11
  • Also called Capital Budgeting or Capital Allocation
  • The most value-creating (or value-destroying) decision a company makes
Capital Allocation
🏦
Decision 2 — The Financing Decision
  • Question: How should we fund our investments and operations?
  • Debt (bonds, loans, debentures) or Equity (shares, retained earnings)?
  • How much debt is too much? What is the optimal capital structure?
  • Should we issue new shares (diluting existing shareholders) or borrow more?
  • What is our cost of capital and how do we minimise it?
  • Tools: WACC, Gearing analysis, Modigliani-Miller — Sections 22.6, 22.7, 22.8
Capital Structure
💰
Decision 3 — The Dividend Decision
  • Question: How much profit should we return to shareholders vs retain for growth?
  • Pay a dividend? How much? How often?
  • Buy back shares instead of paying dividends?
  • Retain all earnings and reinvest into high-return projects?
  • What signals does the dividend send to the market?
  • Theories: MM Dividend Irrelevance, Bird-in-Hand, Signalling Theory — Section 22.9
Return of Capital
🔗 How the Three Decisions Interact
These three decisions are deeply interconnected — you cannot make one in isolation. If you make a big Investment Decision (build ₹500Cr new plant), you must make a Financing Decision (borrow ₹300Cr + equity ₹200Cr), which affects your Dividend Decision (less free cash flow → lower dividend capacity). Getting all three right simultaneously is the CFO's core challenge — and why Corporate Finance is one of the most intellectually demanding fields in finance.
22.2
The Goal of Corporate Finance — Shareholder Value vs Stakeholder Theory

Before making any corporate finance decision, we need to understand: what is the company trying to achieve? This sounds obvious but has been one of the most debated questions in business for decades.

📊
Traditional View — Shareholder Wealth Maximisation
  • The primary goal of a company is to maximise the wealth of its shareholders
  • Shareholders are the owners — they bear residual risk, so they should receive primary focus
  • Measured by: share price appreciation + dividends = Total Shareholder Return (TSR)
  • Driven by: Milton Friedman — "The Social Responsibility of Business Is to Increase Its Profits"
  • All major corporate finance theory (WACC, NPV, capital structure) is built on this premise
  • Criticism: encourages short-termism, ignores social/environmental impact
Traditional
🌍
Modern View — Stakeholder Value Creation
  • A company has responsibilities to ALL stakeholders — shareholders, employees, customers, suppliers, community, environment
  • Long-term shareholder value is best achieved by serving all stakeholders well
  • A company that exploits employees or pollutes its environment creates long-term risks that destroy shareholder value
  • Business Roundtable (2019): 181 US CEOs signed a statement abandoning shareholder primacy in favour of stakeholder capitalism
  • ESG investing reflects this — investors price in stakeholder performance
  • Chapter 46 covers ESG in depth
Modern
🎯
For ACCA — What to Write
  • ACCA FM: primary objective = shareholder wealth maximisation. This is the exam framework.
  • ACCA SBR / P1: broader stakeholder approach. Discuss agency theory, CSR, ethical considerations.
  • In FM: all investment decisions evaluated by whether they increase NPV / shareholder wealth
  • In SBR: challenge this — should stakeholder interests also be considered? What are the conflicts?
  • Real world: companies increasingly balance both. Know the debate.
Exam Context

Agency Theory — The Problem Between Owners and Managers

🔍 Agency Theory — Critical for ACCA FM & SBR

The Problem: Shareholders (principals) own the company but managers (agents) run it. Managers may make decisions that benefit themselves (higher salaries, empire building, risk aversion) rather than maximising shareholder wealth.

Agency Costs: Costs incurred to align manager behaviour with shareholder interests — monitoring costs (audits, board oversight), bonding costs (management contracts, covenants), residual loss (value lost despite controls).

Solutions: Performance-related pay (bonuses, stock options), executive share schemes (managers become shareholders), strong board oversight and independent directors, transparent reporting and audit, threat of takeover (managers performing poorly risk company being acquired and themselves being replaced).

ACCA Exam: Agency theory is tested in both FM (financial decisions context) and SBR (corporate governance context). Know the problem, the costs, and the solutions thoroughly.

Sources of Finance — Debt vs Equity Deep Dive

Every company needs finance — to fund operations, invest in growth, and manage working capital. The two fundamental sources are Debt (borrowed money that must be repaid with interest) and Equity (ownership capital that shares in profits and losses). Understanding the characteristics, costs, and trade-offs of each is the foundation of capital structure theory.

FeatureDebt FinancingEquity Financing
Repayment ObligationMust be repaid — principal + interest. Legal obligation regardless of profitability.No repayment obligation. Equity is permanent capital unless shares bought back.
CostInterest — tax-deductible (reduces tax bill). Lower nominal cost than equity.Dividends (not tax-deductible) + capital gain expectation. Higher required return than debt.
Risk to CompanyHigh — failure to pay interest triggers default, potential insolvency. Increases financial risk.Low — no mandatory payment. If company makes no profit, no dividend needs to be paid.
Risk to InvestorLower — debt holders paid before equity in liquidation. Secured lenders first priority.Higher — equity holders last in line in liquidation. Residual claim only.
Control ImpactLenders have no voting rights but impose covenants (restrictions on behaviour).New share issue dilutes existing shareholders' ownership % and voting power.
Tax ImpactInterest is tax-deductible — creates a "tax shield." Actual cost of debt = rate × (1−tax rate).Dividends paid from post-tax profits — no tax shield. More expensive after-tax.
FlexibilityLess flexible — covenants restrict financial freedom. Cannot easily defer interest payment.More flexible — dividends can be cut in difficult times without triggering default.
Signals to MarketModerate debt signals confidence in stable cash flows. Excessive debt signals desperation or over-leverage.New share issue sometimes seen as negative signal — management thinks shares are overvalued. Rights issue may signal balance sheet stress.

Types of Debt Finance

🏦
Bank Loans & Term Loans
  • Fixed amount borrowed at agreed interest rate for defined period
  • Repaid in instalments (amortising) or bullet (lump sum at end)
  • Secured (collateral required) or unsecured (based on creditworthiness)
  • Typically has covenants: minimum ICR, maximum gearing, minimum liquidity
  • Most common form of debt for Indian companies
Most Common
📜
Bonds / Debentures
  • Debt securities issued to public capital markets — traded on exchanges
  • Fixed coupon rate + face value repaid at maturity
  • Cheaper than bank loans for large companies (wider investor base)
  • In India: NCDs (Non-Convertible Debentures) common for large corporates
  • Credit rating (CRISIL, ICRA) determines pricing — AAA rated bonds cheapest
Capital Markets
🔄
Convertible Debt
  • Debt that can be converted into equity at a predetermined price
  • Lower interest rate than straight debt — conversion option is valuable to holder
  • Popular with startups — allows debt financing without immediate dilution
  • If company performs well, debt converts to equity — no repayment needed
  • If company underperforms, debt remains as debt — must be repaid
Hybrid
💳
Short-Term Debt (Working Capital)
  • Overdraft — flexible borrowing up to a limit. Interest only on amount drawn.
  • Cash Credit — similar to overdraft, common in India for working capital
  • Commercial Paper — short-term (up to 1 year) debt securities for large corporates
  • Invoice Discounting / Factoring — sell receivables to raise immediate cash
  • Used for day-to-day working capital needs — not long-term investment
Working Capital
📊
Equity Finance — Types
  • Retained Earnings: Most important and cheapest equity source — reinvesting profits. No transaction costs, no dilution.
  • Rights Issue: Offering new shares to existing shareholders at discount. Maintains proportional ownership.
  • IPO (Initial Public Offering): First-time listing on stock exchange. Raises large equity capital but expensive (legal, underwriting costs) and requires ongoing disclosure.
  • Private Equity / VC: Institutional investors take equity stake in private companies.
  • Preference Shares: Hybrid — fixed dividend like debt but no repayment obligation. No voting rights.

22.4
Cost of Equity — CAPM & Dividend Growth Model
ACCA FM Core — Always Examined

The Cost of Equity (Ke) is the minimum return shareholders require to invest in the company — it is the opportunity cost of their capital. Unlike debt, equity has no contractual payment — but shareholders have an implicit required return based on risk. Two models calculate this: CAPM and the Dividend Growth Model.

Cost of Equity — Two Models
CAPM (Capital Asset Pricing Model)
Ke = Rf + β(Rm − Rf)

Rf = Risk-free rate (Government bond yield — typically 10-yr G-Sec rate in India)
β (Beta) = Systematic risk of the share vs the market. β=1 moves with market. β>1 more volatile. β<1 less volatile.
Rm = Expected market return (historical average stock market return)
(Rm − Rf) = Equity Risk Premium (ERP) — extra return for taking equity risk vs risk-free
Dividend Growth Model (Gordon Growth Model)
Ke = D1/P0 + g

D1 = Expected next dividend (D0 × (1+g))
P0 = Current share price (ex-dividend)
g = Constant dividend growth rate (sustainable long-term growth)
Used when: company pays regular dividends with stable growth rate

CAPM — Worked Example

📊 Calculate Cost of Equity Using CAPM

Given: Risk-free rate (Rf) = 7% (India 10-yr G-Sec yield). Beta (β) = 1.3 (company is 30% more volatile than market). Market Return (Rm) = 14%.

Calculation:
Ke = Rf + β(Rm − Rf)
Ke = 7% + 1.3 × (14% − 7%)
Ke = 7% + 1.3 × 7%
Ke = 7% + 9.1%
Ke = 16.1%

Interpretation: Shareholders of this company require a minimum return of 16.1% per year to compensate them for the level of systematic risk they are taking. Any investment the company makes must generate at least this return — otherwise it is destroying shareholder value.

Dividend Growth Model — Worked Example

📊 Calculate Cost of Equity Using Dividend Growth Model

Given: Current share price P0 = ₹250. Most recent dividend D0 = ₹12. Dividend has grown at 6% per year consistently.

Calculation:
D1 = D0 × (1+g) = 12 × 1.06 = ₹12.72
Ke = D1/P0 + g = 12.72/250 + 0.06 = 5.09% + 6% = 11.09%

Interpretation: Shareholders require 11.09% return — 5.09% as dividend yield and 6% as expected capital growth. If the company earns less than 11.09% on its equity, the share price will fall as shareholders sell and invest elsewhere.
✅
Beta — Understanding Systematic Risk
  • β = 1.0: Moves exactly with market. Average market risk.
  • β > 1.0: More volatile than market. Higher risk → higher required return. Example: cyclical stocks (steel, autos), tech growth stocks.
  • β < 1.0: Less volatile than market. Lower risk → lower required return. Example: utilities, FMCG, pharmaceuticals — defensive stocks.
  • β = 0: No correlation with market (risk-free asset theoretically)
  • In India: Beta published by NSE, BSE for all listed companies
  • Beta reflects only SYSTEMATIC risk (market-wide) — not company-specific risk (which can be diversified away)
Systematic Risk
⚠️
Limitations of Each Model
  • CAPM limitations: Assumes single-period model. Beta based on historical data — may not reflect future risk. Assumes perfect capital markets. Difficult to estimate Rf and Rm precisely. Ignores company-specific factors.
  • DGM limitations: Only applicable to dividend-paying companies with stable growth. Very sensitive to growth rate assumption — small change in g dramatically changes Ke. Cannot be used for zero-dividend or high-growth companies where g > Ke.
  • In practice: Both models are calculated and judgment applied to reach a final Ke estimate
ACCA Exam Points

Cost of Debt — Pre-Tax, Post-Tax & Yield to Maturity

The Cost of Debt (Kd) is the effective interest rate the company pays on its debt financing. Unlike equity, debt interest is tax-deductible — so the true after-tax cost of debt is lower than the stated interest rate. This tax shield is one of the key reasons companies use debt.

Cost of Debt — Formulas
Post-Tax Cost of Debt (Simple)
Kd (post-tax) = Interest Rate × (1 − Tax Rate)
Example: 10% loan, 25% tax rate → Kd = 10% × (1−0.25) = 7.5%
The tax deductibility of interest reduces the effective cost by the tax rate.
Yield to Maturity (YTM) — For Bonds/Debentures
YTM = approximate IRR of bond cash flows
YTM ≈ [Annual Coupon + (Redemption Value − Market Price)/n] ÷ [(Redemption Value + Market Price)/2]
Used when bond trades at a discount or premium to face value. Market price reflects current required return, not the coupon rate. Post-tax YTM = YTM × (1−tax rate).
Irredeemable Debt (Perpetual)
Kd = Annual Interest / Market Price of Debt × (1 − Tax Rate)
For perpetual bonds or irredeemable debentures — no redemption date.

YTM Worked Example

📊 Calculate Post-Tax Cost of Redeemable Debt

Given: ₹100 face value bond, 9% annual coupon, redeemable at par (₹100) in 5 years, current market price ₹94. Tax rate 25%.

Step 1 — Approximate YTM:
Annual Coupon = ₹9. Discount on redemption = (100−94)/5 = ₹1.2 per year.
Average investment = (100+94)/2 = ₹97.
Pre-tax YTM ≈ (9 + 1.2) / 97 = 10.2/97 = 10.5%

Step 2 — Post-Tax Cost:
Kd = 10.5% × (1 − 0.25) = 7.9%

Why is the YTM higher than the 9% coupon? Because the bond trades at ₹94 (below par) — the investor gets ₹9 coupon + ₹6 capital gain on redemption. Total return is higher than the coupon alone. From the company's perspective, this is its true cost of this debt.

WACC — Weighted Average Cost of Capital
The Most Important Single Number in Corporate Finance

WACC is the blended cost of all capital a company uses — weighted by the proportion of each source in the total capital structure. It represents the minimum rate of return a company must earn on its investments to satisfy all capital providers (both debt and equity). Investments that earn above WACC create value; investments that earn below WACC destroy value.

WACC Formula
WACC
WACC = Ke × [E/(E+D)] + Kd(1−T) × [D/(E+D)]

Ke = Cost of Equity (from CAPM or DGM)
Kd(1−T) = Post-tax Cost of Debt
E = Market Value of Equity (shares outstanding × current share price)
D = Market Value of Debt (market price of all debt instruments)
E/(E+D) = Equity weight as proportion of total capital
D/(E+D) = Debt weight as proportion of total capital
Note: Always use MARKET values for weights — not book values

Full WACC Calculation — Worked Example

📊 WACC Calculation for XYZ Ltd

Given:
Shares: 500 million shares at market price ₹120 each → Market Value of Equity = ₹6,000Cr
Debt: ₹2,000Cr bonds at market value (trading at par)
Cost of Equity (CAPM): 15.5%
Cost of Debt (post-tax): 8.25% (12% coupon × (1−0.3125) where tax rate = 31.25%)
Tax Rate: 31.25%
SourceMarket Value (₹Cr)WeightCostWeighted Cost
Equity6,0006,000/8,000 = 75%15.5%75% × 15.5% = 11.63%
Debt (post-tax)2,0002,000/8,000 = 25%8.25%25% × 8.25% = 2.06%
Total / WACC8,000100%—13.69%
Interpretation: XYZ Ltd's WACC is 13.69%. Any investment it makes must generate at least 13.69% return to create value for shareholders and satisfy debt holders. An investment earning 10% would destroy value. An investment earning 18% would create significant value (EVA of 4.31% above WACC).
🔑 WACC — Critical Rules for ACCA FM
1. Always use MARKET values, not book values, for weights. Book value of equity (from Balance Sheet) is historical cost of share capital + retained earnings — it bears no relation to what the market values the company at today.

2. Cost of debt is POST-TAX. Interest is tax-deductible — always multiply pre-tax Kd by (1−tax rate) before using in WACC.

3. WACC is company-specific. A company's WACC reflects its specific risk profile and capital structure. Using another company's WACC for investment appraisal is only valid if the investment has the same risk profile as the whole company.

4. WACC is used as the discount rate in NPV calculations — covered in Section 22.11.
22.7
Capital Structure — How Much Debt Is Right?

Capital structure is the mix of debt and equity a company uses to finance its assets. The central question: is there an optimal capital structure that minimises WACC and maximises firm value? This has been one of the most debated questions in finance since the 1950s.

📉
Why Adding Debt Can Reduce WACC
  • Debt is cheaper than equity (lower required return, tax shield)
  • Replacing some equity with debt reduces the blended WACC initially
  • Tax shield on interest is real economic value — reduces the company's tax burden
  • At low leverage levels, adding debt reduces overall cost of capital
  • This is the "benefit" side of the debt decision
Benefit of Debt
📈
Why Too Much Debt Increases WACC
  • As debt increases, financial risk increases — higher chance of default
  • Equity holders demand higher return to compensate for increased risk → Ke rises
  • Debt holders also demand higher interest rate at high leverage → Kd rises
  • Financial distress costs emerge — legal costs, management distraction, customer/supplier concerns
  • Agency costs of debt increase — lenders impose restrictive covenants
  • Beyond a certain point, WACC rises as these costs outweigh the tax shield
Cost of Debt
🎯
The Optimal Capital Structure
  • Exists at the point where WACC is minimised
  • Beyond this point, adding more debt increases WACC and reduces firm value
  • In practice: no precise formula — judgment required
  • Each industry has different "normal" gearing levels based on cash flow stability
  • Stable cash flow businesses (utilities, FMCG) can support more debt safely
  • Cyclical, volatile businesses (commodities, airlines) should maintain lower gearing
  • The Trade-Off Theory formalises this — tax shield vs financial distress costs
The Sweet Spot

Factors Affecting Optimal Capital Structure

FactorEffect on Optimal Debt LevelExample
Cash Flow StabilityMore stable cash flows → can support higher debt (interest easily covered)Utilities, toll roads, subscription businesses → high debt capacity
Asset TangibilityMore tangible assets → better collateral → lenders willing to lend moreReal estate, manufacturing → high tangible assets → higher debt capacity

Industry NormsCompanies tend to match peer capital structures — deviation raises questionsIndian banks: high leverage (by design). IT companies: minimal debt.
22.8
Modigliani-Miller Theorems — The Theory Behind Capital Structure
ACCA FM — Always Examined

Franco Modigliani and Merton Miller (both Nobel Prize winners) developed the theoretical foundation of capital structure in 1958–1963. Their theorems are essential for ACCA FM — examiners love testing whether students understand the logic, not just the conclusion.

🌐
MM Without Tax (1958) — Proposition I
  • Conclusion: In a perfect capital market with no taxes, the value of a firm is independent of its capital structure
  • "It doesn't matter whether you use debt or equity — firm value is determined by operating cash flows, not financing"
  • Intuition: investors can create personal leverage (borrow themselves to invest in unlevered firms) — so corporate leverage adds no value
  • Key assumption: No taxes. No transaction costs. No bankruptcy risk. Perfect information. Rational investors.
  • These assumptions don't exist in reality — but the theorem shows WHAT makes capital structure matter: imperfections (taxes, bankruptcy costs, information asymmetry)
No Tax World
💸
MM With Tax (1963) — Proposition I Revised
  • Conclusion: With corporate taxes, firm value INCREASES with leverage — because interest is tax-deductible
  • Value of Levered Firm = Value of Unlevered Firm + PV of Tax Shield
  • Tax Shield = Tax Rate × Amount of Debt (if permanent debt)
  • Implication: companies should use as much debt as possible to maximise tax shield
  • Problem with this conclusion: If debt is always better, all firms should be 100% debt-funded — clearly not the case. MM with tax ignores financial distress costs.
  • The Trade-Off Theory adds financial distress costs to MM with tax to get to a realistic optimal capital structure
Tax World
📊
MM Proposition II — Cost of Equity
  • Conclusion: As a firm increases leverage, the cost of equity (Ke) rises to exactly offset the benefit of cheaper debt — leaving WACC unchanged (in no-tax world)
  • Intuition: shareholders see increased financial risk as leverage rises → demand higher return → Ke rises
  • Formula: Ke (levered) = Ke (unlevered) + (Ke(unlevered) − Kd) × D/E
  • With tax: WACC declines as leverage increases (tax shield benefit)
  • The rising Ke partially offsets the tax shield benefit — but not fully (unlike no-tax world)
Prop II
📚
Assumptions of MM & Real-World Limitations
  • Perfect capital markets: No transaction costs, no taxes — doesn't exist
  • No bankruptcy costs: Financial distress is costly in reality — legal fees, management distraction, customer/supplier concerns
  • Symmetric information: In reality, managers know more than investors (information asymmetry) → Pecking Order Theory
  • ACCA exam: Always state MM assumptions when discussing, then explain how real-world violations make capital structure matter
Reality Check
💡 How to Answer ACCA FM Questions on MM
ACCA FM questions on Modigliani-Miller typically ask you to: (1) explain MM's conclusions in a specific scenario, (2) calculate the change in firm value from a change in capital structure, or (3) critically evaluate MM's assumptions and explain why real-world capital structure decisions differ from MM predictions.

Always structure your answer: State the relevant MM proposition → State the key assumption → Apply to the scenario → Then challenge the assumption with real-world factors (taxes, bankruptcy costs, agency costs, signalling). This structure reliably scores 70%+ marks.
22.9
Dividend Policy — How Much to Pay Shareholders

The dividend decision is the third core corporate finance decision — how much of the company's profit should be paid out to shareholders as dividends, and how much should be retained for reinvestment? This seemingly simple question has generated decades of theoretical debate.

🌐
MM Dividend Irrelevance Theory
  • Modigliani-Miller (1961): in perfect capital markets, dividend policy is irrelevant to firm value
  • Investors don't need dividends — they can create "homemade dividends" by selling shares
  • What matters is investment policy (NPV of projects), not how profits are distributed
  • Assumptions: no taxes, no transaction costs, perfect information — don't hold in reality
  • Real-world taxes and transaction costs make dividend policy matter
Theoretical
🐦
Bird-in-Hand Theory
  • Gordon and Lintner: investors prefer dividends (certain) to capital gains (uncertain)
  • "A bird in the hand is worth two in the bush" — dividend now vs capital gain later
  • Investors will pay a premium for dividend-paying stocks — reducing the company's cost of equity
  • Implication: companies should pay dividends to maximise share price and minimise Ke
  • MM counter-argument: the certainty of dividends is an illusion — risk depends on operating cash flows, not payment method
Pay Dividends
📣
Signalling Theory
  • Dividends convey information to the market about management's confidence in future earnings
  • Dividend increase: Management signals confidence — expects earnings to be sustainably higher. Share price typically rises.
  • Dividend cut: Powerful negative signal — management signals earnings struggle. Share price often falls significantly.
  • This is why companies are reluctant to cut dividends even in bad times — the signalling cost is high
  • Lintner (1956) study: managers smooth dividends and resist cutting them
Market Signal
👥
Clientele Effect
  • Different investors have different dividend preferences based on their tax position and income needs
  • High dividend preference: Retired investors needing income, pension funds (often tax-exempt on dividends)
  • Low dividend preference: High-tax-bracket investors who prefer capital gains (taxed at lower rate), growth investors who want company to reinvest
  • Companies attract a "clientele" of shareholders who match their dividend policy
  • Implication: changing dividend policy may cause a clientele shift — disrupting the investor base
Investor Types
🔄
Share Buybacks vs Dividends
  • Alternative to dividends — company buys back its own shares from the market
  • Buyback advantages: Flexible (no ongoing commitment), signals shares are undervalued, increases EPS (fewer shares outstanding), more tax-efficient in some jurisdictions
  • Dividend advantages: Regular income for investors, stronger commitment signal, preferred by income-focused investor clientele
  • Indian context: SEBI allows buybacks. TCS, Infosys both use buybacks actively to return excess cash
  • Global trend: US companies increasingly prefer buybacks over dividends (Apple, Microsoft, Google)
Modern Alternative
22.10
Working Capital Management — The Day-to-Day Finance

While long-term capital structure decisions are made rarely, working capital management is ongoing — every day. Working capital is the difference between current assets and current liabilities — it represents the day-to-day funding needed to run the business. Poor working capital management is one of the most common causes of business failure, even for profitable companies.

🔄
The Operating Cycle
  • Cash → Buy Raw Materials → Store as Inventory → Convert to WIP → Finished Goods → Sell on Credit → Receive Cash
  • The time this cycle takes determines how much working capital is needed
  • Longer cycle = more cash tied up = more working capital finance needed
  • CCC (Cash Conversion Cycle) = Inventory Days + Receivable Days − Payable Days
  • Shorter CCC = less cash tied up = more efficient working capital management
CCC
📤
Receivables Management
  • Objective: collect cash from customers as quickly as possible without damaging relationships
  • Credit policy: who gets credit? How much? For how long? What terms?
  • Early payment discounts — offer 2% discount for payment within 10 days. Evaluate using annualised discount cost.
  • Invoice factoring: sell receivables to a third party for immediate cash (at a discount)
  • Invoice discounting: use receivables as collateral for a loan — maintain customer relationship
  • Dunning process: systematic follow-up for overdue invoices — reminders, phone calls, legal action
Collect Faster
📦
Inventory Management
  • Hold too much: cash tied up, storage costs, risk of obsolescence
  • Hold too little: stockouts, lost sales, production disruptions
  • EOQ Model: Economic Order Quantity — optimal order size that minimises total ordering + holding costs. Formula: EOQ = √(2 × Annual Demand × Order Cost ÷ Holding Cost per unit)
  • JIT (Just-in-Time): Order materials to arrive exactly when needed — zero inventory. Requires reliable suppliers. Used by Toyota, major manufacturers.
  • Buffer stock: minimum safety stock level to prevent stockouts
EOQ & JIT
📥
Payables Management
  • Objective: maximise time to pay suppliers (use their credit) without damaging relationships or incurring late penalties
  • Taking full credit terms is legitimate working capital management — paying on day 30 when terms are 30 days
  • Stretching payables beyond terms damages supplier relationships and may result in worse terms or supply disruption
  • Evaluate early payment discounts: "2/10 net 30" = 2% discount if paid in 10 days, otherwise full amount due in 30 days. Annualised cost = substantial — often worth taking.
  • Supply chain finance (reverse factoring): large companies help suppliers get early payment via bank at a small cost
Pay Slower

EOQ Worked Example

📊 Economic Order Quantity Calculation

Given: Annual demand = 12,000 units. Order cost = ₹500 per order. Holding cost = ₹2 per unit per year.

EOQ = √(2 × Annual Demand × Order Cost ÷ Holding Cost)
EOQ = √(2 × 12,000 × 500 ÷ 2)
EOQ = √(60,00,000)
EOQ = 2,449 units per order

Number of orders per year = 12,000 ÷ 2,449 = 4.9 orders ≈ 5 orders per year
Reorder interval = 365 ÷ 5 = every 73 days

This is the quantity that minimises total inventory costs. Ordering more means fewer orders but higher holding costs. Ordering less means more orders but lower holding costs. EOQ is the exact balance point.

Investment Appraisal — NPV, IRR, Payback, ARR
The Investment Decision in Practice

Investment appraisal is the set of techniques used to evaluate whether a proposed capital investment creates value. Should we build the new factory? Buy the new machine? Launch the new product line? These techniques answer the question quantitatively — and ACCA FM tests them extensively.

Investment Appraisal Methods — Formulas & Interpretation
Net Present Value (NPV)
NPV = Σ [Cash Flow_t / (1+r)^t] − Initial Investment
r = discount rate (WACC). t = time period. Sum all discounted cash flows and deduct initial outlay.
Decision rule: Accept if NPV > 0. Maximise NPV when choosing between projects.
NPV is the gold standard — directly measures value created in absolute ₹ terms. Theoretically superior to all other methods.
Internal Rate of Return (IRR)
IRR = discount rate that makes NPV = 0
Calculated by trial and error or interpolation: IRR ≈ a + [NPVa/(NPVa−NPVb)] × (b−a)
Decision rule: Accept if IRR > Cost of Capital (WACC).
Expresses return as a %, easy to compare to cost of capital. Limitation: may give multiple IRRs for non-conventional cash flows. Does not rank mutually exclusive projects correctly (use NPV instead).
Payback Period
Payback = Year before full recovery + (Remaining cost ÷ Cash flow in recovery year)
Decision rule: Accept if Payback < target period. Shorter payback = better.
Simple, intuitive, focuses on liquidity and risk. Major limitation: ignores time value of money and cash flows after payback. Not theoretically sound but widely used in practice for quick screening.
Discounted Payback Period
Same as payback but uses discounted cash flows (PV of each year's CF)
Improvement on simple payback — accounts for time value of money. Still ignores cash flows after payback date. Better than simple payback but inferior to NPV.
Accounting Rate of Return (ARR)
ARR = Average Annual Profit (after depreciation) ÷ Average Investment × 100
Average Investment = (Initial Investment + Scrap Value) ÷ 2
Decision rule: Accept if ARR > target rate. Higher ARR = better.
Uses accounting profit, not cash flow. Ignores time value of money. Easy to calculate. Widely used by non-finance managers but theoretically weak. ACCA FM: know its limitations.

🔑 NPV vs IRR — When They Conflict, Use NPV
For independent projects (accept/reject decision): NPV and IRR give the same answer — both say accept if NPV>0 and IRR>WACC.

For mutually exclusive projects (which ONE to choose): NPV and IRR sometimes give conflicting rankings. Always use NPV.

Why NPV wins: IRR assumes all intermediate cash flows are reinvested at the IRR rate — an unrealistic assumption when IRR is very high. NPV assumes reinvestment at WACC — a realistic assumption. NPV also measures absolute value creation, while IRR measures a % return that ignores project scale (a 50% IRR on ₹1L investment is less valuable than a 20% IRR on ₹100L investment).

Risk in Corporate Finance — Business Risk vs Financial Risk

All corporate finance decisions involve risk. Understanding the distinction between different types of risk — and how they affect the cost of capital — is essential for ACCA FM and real-world decision-making.

🏭
Business Risk (Operating Risk)
  • Definition: The risk inherent in the company's operating activities — uncertainty in EBIT arising from the nature of the business
  • Driven by: revenue volatility, operating leverage (fixed vs variable cost mix), industry cyclicality, competition
  • High operating leverage = high fixed costs = small revenue change → large EBIT change = high business risk
  • Examples: Airlines (high fixed costs — planes, routes) have high business risk. Service businesses (variable costs) have lower business risk.
  • Cannot be reduced by changing capital structure — it is driven by the business model
  • Reflected in the company's asset beta (ungeared beta)
Operating Risk
💳
Financial Risk (Leverage Risk)
  • Definition: Additional risk imposed on shareholders by the use of debt financing
  • Debt creates fixed interest obligations — if EBIT falls, interest still must be paid → PAT is more volatile than EBIT
  • Higher gearing = higher financial risk = higher required return by equity shareholders (Ke rises)
  • Financial risk can be controlled by management through capital structure decisions
  • Reflected in the difference between equity beta (geared) and asset beta (ungeared)
  • Formula: Equity Beta = Asset Beta × [1 + (1−T) × D/E]
Leverage Risk
🌍
Systematic vs Unsystematic Risk
  • Systematic (Market) Risk: Risk that affects all companies — recessions, interest rate changes, inflation, geopolitical events. Cannot be diversified away. Measured by Beta. Compensated by the market (built into required returns).
  • Unsystematic (Specific) Risk: Risk unique to one company or industry — management failure, product recall, factory fire, key client loss. CAN be diversified away by holding a portfolio of stocks. Not compensated by the market — investors should diversify rather than being paid for avoidable risk.
  • CAPM only compensates for systematic risk — which is why Beta (systematic risk) is used, not total volatility.
CAPM Foundation
🔄
Adjusting Beta for Capital Structure — Ungearing & Regearing
  • When appraising a project in a new industry, use the beta of comparable companies in that industry — not the company's own beta
  • Step 1 — Ungear (remove financial risk): Convert equity (geared) beta to asset (ungeared) beta using MM formula: βa = βe × E/(E + D(1−T))
  • Step 2 — Regear (add company's financial risk): Adjust the proxy asset beta to reflect the appraising company's own capital structure: βe = βa × [1 + (1−T) × D/E]
  • This gives a project-specific WACC — more appropriate than company WACC for cross-industry investments

Corporate Finance in India — Real World Context

Corporate finance theory is developed largely in a Western (US/UK) context. India has a unique corporate finance landscape — driven by promoter-dominated ownership, RBI regulation, SEBI oversight, and the specific characteristics of Indian capital markets.

🏛️
Promoter-Dominated Capital Structure
  • Most large Indian companies are promoter-controlled (founding family retains majority stake)
  • Promoters are reluctant to dilute equity — prefer debt to fund growth
  • This creates higher leverage than theoretically optimal in some cases
  • Promoter share pledging is a uniquely Indian risk — pledged shares create a vicious cycle in market downturns
  • SEBI mandates disclosure of promoter pledging — always check this for Indian company analysis
India Specific
💰
Indian Capital Markets — Key Features
  • BSE (Bombay Stock Exchange) and NSE (National Stock Exchange) — primary equity markets
  • SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) — regulates capital markets
  • RBI — regulates banking and monetary policy, directly affects cost of debt
  • High retail investor participation (growing post-COVID) — SIP investments driving equity markets
  • Indian bond market relatively underdeveloped vs equity market — most corporate debt through bank loans
  • External Commercial Borrowings (ECBs) — large companies borrow in foreign currency for cheaper rates
Market Structure
📊
Indian Dividend Tax Context
  • Pre-2020: Dividend Distribution Tax (DDT) paid by company — investors received dividends tax-free
  • Post-2020: DDT abolished. Dividends taxed in hands of investors at their slab rate.
  • High-income investors (30% slab) now prefer share buybacks over dividends for tax efficiency
  • This has driven the surge in Indian company buybacks post-2020 (TCS, Infosys, HCL Tech)
  • Understanding this context is important for Indian corporate finance analysis
Tax Context
🏗️
Indian Infrastructure Finance
  • Government-driven investment in roads, railways, ports, power — ₹10+ lakh crore annual capex
  • Special purpose vehicles (SPVs) used for project finance — ring-fenced debt
  • InvITs (Infrastructure Investment Trusts) — listed vehicles that hold infrastructure assets and distribute income to unitholders
  • NaBFID (National Bank for Financing Infrastructure and Development) — new DFI for infrastructure lending
  • High leverage in infrastructure — projects generate stable, long-term cash flows to service debt

Common Mistakes, Flashcards & Interview Q&A

Part A — Common Mistakes (Don't Repeat These)

❌ MISTAKE 1
Using Book Values Instead of Market Values in WACC
WACC weights must use the MARKET value of equity (shares outstanding × current share price) and market value of debt — never book values from the Balance Sheet. Book value of equity reflects historical cost, not what the market currently values the company at.
❌ MISTAKE 2
Forgetting to Tax-Adjust the Cost of Debt
Interest is tax-deductible, so Kd must always be multiplied by (1 − Tax Rate) before being used in the WACC formula. Using the pre-tax cost of debt overstates WACC and understates the true benefit of debt financing.
❌ MISTAKE 3
Using a Company-Wide WACC for a Project With a Different Risk Profile
WACC reflects the risk of the company's EXISTING operations. Using it to appraise a project in a completely different industry/risk category (e.g. a stable FMCG company evaluating a high-risk tech venture) is a classic ACCA FM trap — a project-specific discount rate should be used instead.
❌ MISTAKE 4
Choosing IRR Over NPV for Mutually Exclusive Projects
When choosing between mutually exclusive projects (only one can be selected), NPV and IRR can rank them differently — always follow NPV, since IRR ignores project scale and assumes unrealistic reinvestment at the IRR rate itself.
❌ MISTAKE 5
Including Depreciation as a Cash Flow in NPV Calculations
NPV appraisal uses CASH flows, not accounting profit — depreciation is a non-cash accounting allocation and must be excluded from the cash flow figures used in NPV. It's only relevant for calculating the tax cash flow (tax savings from the depreciation tax shield) or for ARR, which uses accounting profit deliberately.
❌ MISTAKE 6
Confusing Modigliani-Miller's "No-Tax World" Conclusion With Reality
MM's original theorem (no taxes) states capital structure is irrelevant to firm value. But MM's theorem WITH taxes concludes the opposite — debt increases firm value due to the tax shield, up to the point where financial distress costs offset the benefit. Quoting the no-tax conclusion in a real-world (taxed) context is a common exam error.

"What is WACC and why does it matter for investment decisions?"Foundational corporate finance concept — near-universal in finance interviewsWACC is the blended cost of a company's debt and equity financing, weighted by their proportion in the capital structure.
