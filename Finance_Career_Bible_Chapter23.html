23.2
Legal Systems — Common Law vs Civil Law

The world's legal systems fall broadly into two families. Understanding which one governs a jurisdiction changes how contracts, disputes, and company law actually work in practice.

⚖️
Common Law System
  • Originated in England — used in UK, India, USA, Australia, most Commonwealth countries
  • Based on case law (precedent) — judges' past decisions bind future similar cases
  • Doctrine of "stare decisis" — courts follow precedent set by higher courts
  • Legislation (statute) works alongside case law, but judges actively interpret and develop law through rulings
Precedent-Based
📜
Civil Law System
  • Originated in continental Europe (Roman law tradition) — used in France, Germany, most of Europe, Japan, Latin America
  • Based on comprehensive written codes — a civil code sets out rules systematically in advance
  • Judges apply the code to facts — prior case decisions are persuasive, not strictly binding
  • Legislature (not judges) is the primary source of law-making
Code-Based
🔑 Why This Distinction Matters for Finance
India follows a common law system (inherited from British colonial rule), which is why Indian contract law, company law, and case citations often reference English legal principles. When working with international contracts or cross-border deals, knowing whether the counterparty's jurisdiction is common law or civil law changes how contracts should be drafted and how disputes will likely be resolved.

23.4
Formation of Contract — Offer, Acceptance, Consideration
The Foundation of Business Law

A contract is a legally binding agreement. For a contract to exist, four essential elements must all be present — miss any one and there is no valid contract, regardless of what the parties believe they've agreed.

The 4 Essential Elements of a Valid Contract
1. Offer
A clear, definite statement of terms one party is willing to be bound by if accepted. Must be distinguished from an "invitation to treat" (e.g. goods displayed in a shop window are an invitation to treat, not an offer — the customer makes the offer at the till).
2. Acceptance
Unqualified agreement to all terms of the offer, communicated to the offeror. A "counter-offer" (changing any term) destroys the original offer and becomes a new offer itself.
3. Consideration
Something of value given by each party — the "price" of the promise. Must have SOME value (need not be adequate/fair value) and must NOT be past (a promise to pay for something already done is generally not valid consideration).
4. Intention to Create Legal Relations
Both parties must intend the agreement to be legally binding. Commercial agreements are PRESUMED to have this intention; social/domestic agreements are PRESUMED NOT to — either presumption can be rebutted with evidence.

Offer vs Invitation to Treat — The Classic Exam Distinction

Is an OfferIs an Invitation to Treat (NOT an offer)
A specific quotation sent to a named customer with clear termsGoods displayed in a shop window or on a shelf, with price tags
A tender submitted in response to a specific requestAn advertisement in a newspaper or catalogue
A reward offer to the public ("₹10,000 for return of lost item")A request for tenders/quotations
—An auction — the auctioneer's call for bids is an invitation to treat; the bidder makes the offer
💡 Why the Offer vs Invitation to Treat Distinction Matters
If a shop mis-prices an item on the shelf, it is NOT contractually bound to sell at that price — because the display was only an invitation to treat, and the shop can reject the customer's "offer" to buy at the till. This principle protects businesses from being bound by pricing errors, and is heavily tested in ACCA LW exams.

Worked Example — Offer, Acceptance & Revocation

📋 Scenario: Timing of Contract Formation

Facts: On Monday, A offers to sell B a machine for ₹5,00,000, giving B until Friday to respond. On Wednesday, A sells the machine to someone else and does not tell B. On Thursday, B (unaware of the sale) posts a letter of acceptance.

Analysis: An offer can be revoked (withdrawn) any time before acceptance — but the revocation must be COMMUNICATED to the offeree to be effective. A never told B the offer was revoked. Under the "postal rule," acceptance by post is effective the moment the letter is posted (Thursday) — not when received.

Conclusion: A valid contract was formed on Thursday when B posted the acceptance, because A's revocation was never communicated. A is in breach of contract by selling to someone else.
23.5
Contract Terms — Express, Implied, Conditions & Warranties

Once a contract exists, its exact obligations are defined by its "terms." Not all terms carry equal weight — and knowing the difference determines what remedy is available if a term is broken.

✍️
Express Terms
  • Terms actually stated by the parties — written or oral
  • The starting point for interpreting any contract
  • Written contracts generally take priority over informal oral statements made earlier
Explicitly Agreed
🔍
Implied Terms
  • Terms not stated but read into the contract — by statute, by custom, or by courts to give the contract "business efficacy"
  • Example: sale of goods legislation implies terms that goods must be of satisfactory quality and fit for purpose
  • Cannot usually be excluded if implied by consumer protection statute
Read Into Contract

Conditions vs Warranties — The Remedy Determines the Classification

Term TypeDefinitionRemedy If Breached
ConditionA fundamental term — goes to the root of the contractInnocent party can terminate the contract AND claim damages
WarrantyA minor, subsidiary term — does not go to the root of the contractInnocent party can only claim damages — CANNOT terminate the contract
Innominate TermA term that could be either, depending on the SERIOUSNESS of the actual breach that occurredCourts look at the consequences of the breach to decide the remedy available
🔑 ACCA LW Exam Tip — Classify Before You Answer
When a scenario describes a breach, always classify the term FIRST (condition, warranty, or innominate) before deciding what remedy applies. Getting this wrong — for example, allowing termination for breach of a mere warranty — is one of the most common LW exam errors.
23.6
Vitiating Factors — Misrepresentation, Duress, Mistake
What Can Make a Contract Invalid

Even a contract with all four essential elements present can be challenged if a "vitiating factor" is present — something that undermines genuine consent or fairness in how the agreement was reached.

🎭
Misrepresentation
  • A false statement of FACT (not opinion) made by one party that induces the other to enter the contract
  • Fraudulent: made knowingly false — remedy: rescission + damages
  • Negligent: made carelessly without reasonable grounds for belief — remedy: rescission + damages
  • Innocent: made honestly believing it true — remedy: rescission only (damages at court's discretion)
False Statement
😤
Duress & Undue Influence
  • Duress: illegitimate pressure (threats of violence, unlawful action) forcing agreement
  • Undue Influence: abuse of a position of trust/power to secure agreement (e.g. between a company and a dominated director)
  • Contract is voidable at the option of the pressured party
Improper Pressure
❓
Mistake
  • Common Mistake: both parties share the same fundamental mistaken belief (e.g. the subject matter no longer exists)
  • Mutual Mistake: parties are at cross-purposes, each with a different understanding
  • Unilateral Mistake: only one party is mistaken, and the other knows or should have known
  • Genuine, operative mistake can render a contract void from the start
Genuine Error
23.7
Discharge of Contract & Remedies for Breach

A contract can come to an end ("be discharged") in several ways — and when it ends through breach, the law provides specific remedies to the innocent party.

Ways a Contract Is Discharged

MethodHow It Happens
PerformanceBoth parties fully and precisely perform their obligations — the normal, intended end of a contract
AgreementBoth parties mutually agree to end or vary the contract
FrustrationAn unforeseen event (outside either party's control) makes performance impossible, illegal, or radically different from what was agreed
BreachOne party fails to perform their obligations as agreed — gives rise to remedies for the innocent party

Remedies for Breach of Contract

Available Remedies
Damages
Monetary compensation to put the innocent party in the position they would have been in had the contract been performed. Must be caused by the breach and not too "remote" (unforeseeable). The innocent party has a duty to "mitigate" loss — cannot recover losses that reasonable steps could have avoided.
Specific Performance
A court order requiring the breaching party to actually perform the contract. Discretionary — granted mainly where damages are inadequate (e.g. sale of unique goods or land), not for personal service contracts.
Injunction
A court order preventing a party from doing something that would breach the contract (e.g. preventing breach of a non-compete clause).
Rescission
Cancels the contract, putting parties back in their pre-contract position — typically the remedy for misrepresentation rather than breach.
💡 Duty to Mitigate — A Key Exam Point
An innocent party cannot simply let losses pile up and claim everything back — they must take REASONABLE steps to minimise their loss. For example, if a supplier fails to deliver, the buyer should reasonably attempt to source from an alternative supplier rather than doing nothing and claiming the full extended loss.
23.8
The Law of Agency

Agency law governs situations where one person (the agent) acts on behalf of another (the principal) — creating legal relations between the principal and third parties. This is fundamental to how companies operate, since a company (a legal but not physical person) can only act through human agents — its directors and employees.

🤝
How Agency Is Created
  • Express agreement: explicitly appointed (written or oral)
  • Implied agreement: conduct of the parties implies an agency relationship
  • Ratification: principal later approves an act done without prior authority
  • Necessity: emergency circumstances justify one party acting for another without prior authority
Formation
📋
Types of Authority
  • Actual Authority: authority genuinely given by the principal (express or implied from the role)
  • Apparent (Ostensible) Authority: authority the principal's conduct represents the agent as having, even if not actually granted — third parties can rely on this
Scope of Power
🔑 Why This Matters for Company Directors
A director acting within their apparent authority (e.g. a Managing Director signing routine contracts) binds the company to third parties — even if the board secretly restricted that authority internally, as long as the third party wasn't aware of the restriction. This principle protects third parties dealing with companies in good faith, and is why company boards must carefully manage and communicate who has authority to sign what.
23.9
Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR)

Not every dispute needs to go to court. Alternative Dispute Resolution covers a range of methods for resolving disputes outside traditional litigation — often faster, cheaper, more confidential, and better at preserving business relationships than a public court battle.

MethodHow It WorksOutcome
NegotiationParties discuss directly (often through lawyers) to reach a mutually acceptable resolutionNon-binding until formalised in a settlement agreement — most flexible, least formal
MediationA neutral third party (mediator) facilitates discussion and helps parties find common ground — the mediator does NOT decide the outcomeNon-binding unless parties agree to a settlement — preserves relationships well
ArbitrationA neutral arbitrator (or panel) hears both sides and makes a decision — similar to a private judgeBINDING — the arbitrator's award is legally enforceable, similar to a court judgment
ConciliationSimilar to mediation, but the conciliator may actively propose solutions (more directive than a mediator)Non-binding unless parties agree — a middle ground between mediation and arbitration
🔑 Why Businesses Prefer ADR — Especially Arbitration — Over Litigation
Arbitration is particularly common in COMMERCIAL contracts (especially cross-border ones) because it offers: confidentiality (court proceedings are public; arbitration typically isn't), speed (court backlogs can take years; arbitration is often faster), expertise (parties can choose an arbitrator with specific technical/industry knowledge, unlike a randomly-assigned judge), and enforceability across borders (arbitral awards are widely enforceable internationally under the New York Convention, often more reliably than a foreign court judgment).

Arbitration Clauses in Commercial Contracts

STANDARD
Most significant commercial contracts (supply agreements, JV agreements, cross-border deals) include a dedicated ARBITRATION CLAUSE — specifying the arbitration rules to apply, the seat/location of arbitration, the number of arbitrators, and the governing law
EFFECT
Where a valid arbitration clause exists, courts will typically REFER the dispute to arbitration rather than hearing it themselves — parties are generally bound by their agreement to arbitrate
FINANCE RELEVANCE
Finance professionals reviewing or negotiating commercial contracts should always check the dispute resolution clause — the choice of arbitration seat and rules can significantly affect cost, speed, and predictability if a dispute later arises
23.10
Company Formation & Types of Business Organisation

Businesses can be structured in several legal forms — each with different implications for liability, taxation, control, and ability to raise finance.

StructureLegal StatusOwner LiabilityKey Feature
Sole ProprietorshipNo separate legal identity from ownerUnlimited — owner personally liable for all debtsSimplest, full control, but highest personal risk
PartnershipNo separate legal identity (in most jurisdictions)Unlimited, and JOINT & SEVERAL — each partner liable for all partnership debtsShared decision-making and profits per partnership agreement
Limited Liability Partnership (LLP)Separate legal entityLimited to capital contributedCombines partnership flexibility with limited liability protection
Private Limited CompanySeparate legal entity ("corporate personality")Limited to unpaid share capitalCannot offer shares to the public; restricted share transferability
Public Limited CompanySeparate legal entityLimited to unpaid share capitalCan offer shares to the public, list on a stock exchange
🔑 Separate Legal Personality — The Most Important Company Law Concept
Once incorporated, a company becomes its own legal "person" — distinct from its shareholders and directors. It can own property, enter contracts, sue and be sued in its own name, and its debts belong to IT, not its owners. This is the foundation of limited liability: shareholders can generally only lose what they invested, not their personal assets — a principle established in the landmark case Salomon v Salomon & Co Ltd (1897), which remains foundational to company law across common law jurisdictions.

"Piercing/Lifting the Corporate Veil" — The Exceptions

RARE
Courts VERY rarely disregard separate legal personality — but will do so to prevent fraud, where a company is used as a "mere façade" to evade existing legal obligations, or in specific statutory situations (e.g. certain insolvency wrongdoing provisions). This is the exception, not the rule — the starting presumption is always that the corporate veil stays intact.
23.11
Partnership Law in Depth

Chapter 23.10 introduced partnerships briefly as one of several business structures. Given how common partnerships remain — especially in professional services (law firms, accounting firms, consulting) — the specific legal rules governing them deserve deeper treatment.

🤝
How a Partnership Is Formed
  • Can be formed WITHOUT any written agreement — simply by two or more people carrying on a business together with a view to profit
  • A written PARTNERSHIP AGREEMENT is strongly advisable (though not legally required) to define profit-sharing, decision-making authority, and exit terms
  • In the ABSENCE of an agreement, default statutory rules apply (e.g. equal profit-sharing regardless of capital contributed) — often not what partners actually intended, making a written agreement important
Formation
⚖️
Joint and Several Liability
  • In a general (traditional) partnership, EACH partner is personally liable for the FULL amount of partnership debts — not just their proportional share
  • A creditor can pursue ANY ONE partner for the entire debt, leaving that partner to seek contribution from the others — significant personal risk exposure
  • This is precisely why many professional partnerships have shifted to LLP structures (Chapter 23.10) for liability protection
Personal Risk
👋
Partner as Agent of the Firm
  • Each partner is generally an AGENT of the partnership (and of the other partners) for business carried out in the ordinary course of the partnership's business (linking directly to the agency principles in Chapter 23.8)
  • This means one partner's actions within the ordinary scope of business can bind the WHOLE partnership — even without the other partners' explicit knowledge or consent
Mutual Agency
🚪
Dissolution
  • A general partnership can be dissolved by mutual agreement, by a partner giving notice (if the agreement permits), by the death/bankruptcy of a partner, or by court order
  • Without a well-drafted partnership agreement addressing exit/continuation, the departure of even ONE partner can technically dissolve the entire partnership under default rules — a significant practical risk many partnerships address explicitly in their agreement
Ending the Partnership
🔑 Why This Matters for Finance Professionals
Many ACCA members eventually become PARTNERS themselves (in accounting, audit, or advisory firms) — understanding joint and several liability, mutual agency, and dissolution risk isn't just exam theory, it's directly relevant to a genuinely common senior career outcome. It's also why so many professional partnerships have restructured as LLPs over the past two decades — the liability protection is a major, deliberate legal choice, not an accident of history.
23.12
Share Capital & Company Financing

Companies raise finance in two broad ways — equity (shares) and debt (loans/debentures) — each carrying different legal rights and obligations.

📈
Ordinary Shares
  • Carry voting rights at general meetings
  • Entitled to dividends only after preference shareholders, and only if declared
  • Bear the most risk — last to be paid on winding up, but full upside if the company succeeds
Equity — Voting
💎
Preference Shares
  • Fixed dividend rate, paid before ordinary shareholders
  • Usually no voting rights (unless dividends are in arrears, depending on terms)
  • May be "cumulative" (unpaid dividends carry forward) or "redeemable" (company can buy back)
Equity — Fixed Return
📃
Debentures / Loan Capital
  • Debt, not equity — company is legally obligated to pay interest regardless of profit
  • Debenture holders are CREDITORS, not owners — no voting rights
  • May be secured (backed by a charge over company assets) or unsecured
  • Paid BEFORE any shareholders on winding up
Debt Financing
💡 Fixed vs Floating Charges — A Key LW Distinction
A fixed charge attaches to a specific identifiable asset (e.g. a building) which the company cannot deal with freely without lender consent. A floating charge hovers over a class of changing assets (e.g. inventory, receivables) which the company can freely trade — until it "crystallises" into a fixed charge upon a defined triggering event (e.g. insolvency). On winding up, fixed charge holders are paid ahead of floating charge holders.
23.13
Directors' Duties & Corporate Governance

Directors run the company on behalf of shareholders — and the law imposes specific duties to ensure they act properly, since they control assets that don't belong to them personally.

Core Statutory Directors' Duties

DutyWhat It Requires
Duty to Act Within PowersAct in accordance with the company's constitution and only exercise powers for their proper purpose
Duty to Promote Success of the CompanyAct in good faith to promote the company's success for the benefit of its members as a whole, considering long-term consequences, employees, suppliers, community, and environment
Duty to Exercise Independent JudgmentNot simply defer to others — form genuinely independent views (subject to authorised delegation)
Duty of Reasonable Care, Skill & DiligenceAct with the care, skill and diligence a reasonably diligent person with the director's knowledge/experience would show
Duty to Avoid Conflicts of InterestAvoid situations where personal interests conflict (or may conflict) with the company's interests
Duty Not to Accept Benefits From Third PartiesCannot accept bribes, kickbacks, or benefits from third parties by reason of being a director
Duty to Declare Interest in Proposed TransactionsMust disclose any direct or indirect interest in a proposed transaction to the board before it proceeds
🔑 Consequences of Breaching Director Duties
A director who breaches these duties can be held personally liable — required to compensate the company for losses caused, account for any profit improperly made, or have a contract voided. In serious cases (e.g. fraudulent trading, wrongful trading near insolvency), directors can face disqualification from acting as a director for a period, and in extreme cases, personal criminal liability.

Corporate Governance — Beyond Legal Minimums

👥
Board Structure
  • Balance of executive directors (day-to-day management) and non-executive directors (independent oversight)
  • Independent Chairman separate from CEO is widely recommended good governance practice
  • Board committees (Audit, Remuneration, Nomination) provide specialised oversight
🔍
Accountability Mechanisms
  • Annual General Meetings give shareholders a voice and vote
  • External audit provides independent assurance over financial statements
  • Disclosure requirements ensure transparency to the market
23.14
Corporate Insolvency — When Companies Fail

When a company cannot pay its debts, insolvency law determines how the situation is managed, and — critically for finance professionals doing credit analysis — the ORDER in which creditors get paid.

Key Insolvency Procedures

ProcedurePurpose
Liquidation (Winding Up)Company's assets are sold and distributed to creditors in priority order, and the company ceases to exist
Administration / Resolution ProcessAn independent professional takes control to try to rescue the company as a going concern, or achieve a better outcome for creditors than immediate liquidation (in India: the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process under the IBC, managed via NCLT)
ReceivershipA secured creditor (holding a charge) appoints a receiver to recover what's owed from specific charged assets
Voluntary Arrangement / RestructuringCompany negotiates a formal agreement with creditors to pay a reduced amount or over an extended period, avoiding liquidation

Order of Priority on Liquidation — Critical for Credit Analysis

1st
Fixed Charge Holders — paid from the specific asset secured against them, before anyone else touches that asset
2nd
Insolvency/Liquidation Expenses — costs of running the insolvency process itself
3rd
Preferential Creditors — typically includes certain employee claims (unpaid wages up to a limit) and specific statutory dues
4th
Floating Charge Holders — paid from remaining assets once fixed charges and preferential claims are settled
5th
Unsecured Creditors — trade creditors, unsecured lenders — paid pro-rata from whatever remains, often receiving only a fraction of what's owed
LAST
Shareholders — preference shareholders first (if any capital remains), then ordinary shareholders — usually receive nothing in an insolvent liquidation
💡 Why This Order Matters for Finance Professionals
This priority waterfall is exactly why unsecured trade credit is riskier than secured lending — and why credit analysts price risk, structure security, and set credit limits based on where they'd sit in this queue if the counterparty failed. Understanding this order is directly relevant to credit risk assessment, treasury decisions, and structuring lending facilities.

Employment Law Essentials

Every finance professional either manages people or works within an organisation shaped by employment law. Understanding the basics helps in hiring decisions, performance management, and cost planning.

📝
Employment Contract
  • Formed like any contract — offer, acceptance, consideration (salary for work), intention to create legal relations
  • Contains express terms (salary, hours, role) and implied terms (mutual trust and confidence, duty of care)
  • Distinguished from a "contract for services" (independent contractor) — this distinction affects tax and statutory protections significantly
Formation
🚪
Termination of Employment
  • Dismissal: employer ends the contract — must generally have a fair reason and follow fair procedure to avoid being "unfair"
  • Redundancy: role genuinely no longer needed — a specific, legally distinct type of dismissal, often with statutory compensation
  • Resignation: employee ends the contract, generally with a notice period
Ending Employment
⚖️
Discrimination Protection
  • Employment decisions cannot be based on protected characteristics (varies by jurisdiction — typically includes gender, age, disability, religion, etc.)
  • Applies across recruitment, pay, promotion, and termination decisions
  • Finance teams should understand this when involved in workforce cost decisions or restructuring
Equality

Corporate & Business Law in India — Real World Context

While ACCA LW principles are largely UK/common-law based, India has its own detailed statutory framework that finance professionals working in India must know.

AreaKey Indian LegislationRegulator/Body
Contract LawIndian Contract Act, 1872Civil courts
Company LawCompanies Act, 2013Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA), Registrar of Companies (RoC)
Securities & Capital MarketsSEBI Act, 1992 + SEBI RegulationsSecurities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI)
InsolvencyInsolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT), IBBI

🔑 IBC — India's Modern Insolvency Framework
The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (2016) dramatically modernised Indian insolvency law, introducing a time-bound Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) managed through the NCLT. Before the IBC, insolvency resolution in India could take years; the IBC framework aims for resolution within a defined timeline, significantly improving creditor recovery rates and India's ease-of-doing-business standing.
23.17
ACCA LW Exam Technique

LW is a Computer-Based Exam (CBE) — 2 hours, combining objective test questions (MCQs) with longer multi-task questions requiring application to scenarios.

📋 The LW Scenario-Answer Method

1. IDENTIFY: What area of law does this scenario involve? (Contract? Company? Employment? Insolvency?)
2. STATE THE RULE: What is the relevant legal principle or statutory rule?
3. APPLY TO FACTS: Apply that rule specifically to the facts given — don't just restate the rule generically.
4. CONCLUDE: State a clear conclusion — who wins, what remedy applies, or what the legal position is.
💡 Common LW Exam Trap
Many candidates state the correct legal rule but fail to APPLY it specifically to the facts given — simply repeating "a contract needs offer, acceptance, and consideration" without explaining whether THIS specific scenario has all three earns minimal marks. Always connect the rule directly to the facts in front of you.

Common Mistakes, Flashcards & Interview Q&A

Part A — Common Mistakes (Don't Repeat These)

❌ MISTAKE 1
Confusing an Invitation to Treat With an Offer
Shop displays, advertisements, and requests for tenders are invitations to treat, not offers — the customer/responder makes the actual offer. Treating a shop's mis-priced shelf tag as a binding offer is a classic exam error.
❌ MISTAKE 2
Allowing Termination for a Warranty Breach
Only breach of a CONDITION allows the innocent party to terminate the contract. Breach of a warranty (a minor term) only gives a right to damages — never a right to terminate. Confusing these two is one of the most tested LW errors.
❌ MISTAKE 3
Ignoring Separate Legal Personality
Treating a company's debts as automatically the shareholders' personal debts misunderstands the entire foundation of limited liability. The corporate veil is only pierced in genuinely exceptional circumstances (fraud, façade) — not simply because a company has failed.
❌ MISTAKE 4
Getting the Insolvency Priority Order Wrong
Assuming unsecured creditors or shareholders are paid before secured (fixed charge) creditors is a fundamental misunderstanding. Fixed charge holders are paid first from their specific secured asset; shareholders are paid last, if anything remains at all.
❌ MISTAKE 5
Stating the Legal Rule Without Applying It to the Facts
LW exam answers require applying the rule specifically to the given scenario — merely reciting "a contract requires offer, acceptance, and consideration" without analysing whether THIS scenario actually has all three earns minimal application marks.
❌ MISTAKE 6
Assuming All Company Financing Carries the Same Legal Rights
Ordinary shares, preference shares, and debentures carry fundamentally different legal rights (voting, dividend priority, and creditor vs owner status). Treating "capital raised" as a single undifferentiated pool ignores these critical legal distinctions.
❌ MISTAKE 7
Confusing Mediation and Arbitration
Mediation is NON-BINDING — the mediator only facilitates discussion and cannot impose a decision. Arbitration IS binding — the arbitrator's award is legally enforceable, similar to a court judgment. Treating these as interchangeable, or assuming a mediation outcome is automatically enforceable, is a common error.
❌ MISTAKE 8
Assuming Partners Are Only Liable for Their Own Share of Partnership Debts
In a general partnership, liability is JOINT AND SEVERAL — each partner can be pursued for the FULL amount of a partnership debt, not just their proportional share. This is precisely why many professional partnerships have restructured as LLPs for liability protection.
