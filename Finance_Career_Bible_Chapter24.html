Basic standard costing and simple variance analysis (covered in Chapter 6) work well for straightforward, single-product manufacturing. But real businesses are messier — they face production bottlenecks, workforce learning curves on new products, multiple products competing for the same limited materials, and complex overhead structures that simple costing can't accurately capture. This chapter covers the advanced PM toolkit ACCA expects at Applied Skills level and beyond — the tools that let a management accountant give genuinely sophisticated advice to operations and strategy teams.

"Basic costing tells you what happened. Advanced management accounting tells you what to do about it."

24.2
Throughput Accounting — The Theory of Constraints

Every production process has at least one bottleneck — the resource with the least capacity relative to demand, which limits how much the entire system can produce. The Theory of Constraints (TOC), developed by Eliyahu Goldratt, says the only way to increase overall output is to manage and maximise the bottleneck — improving efficiency anywhere else in the process achieves nothing if the bottleneck itself isn't addressed.

🔗
The Core TOC Insight
  • A chain is only as strong as its weakest link
  • Speeding up a non-bottleneck process just creates a bigger pile of unfinished work-in-progress in front of the bottleneck
  • Total factory output is capped by the bottleneck's capacity — period
Foundational Principle
💰
Throughput Accounting's Key Assumption
  • In the SHORT TERM, only direct materials are treated as a truly variable cost
  • Labour and overheads are treated as largely fixed in the short run — "factory costs," not variable per unit
  • This is a deliberately extreme, short-term simplification — useful for bottleneck decisions, not for long-term pricing
TA Philosophy

The 5 Steps of Theory of Constraints

STEP 1
Identify the Bottleneck — find the resource/process with the least capacity relative to demand placed on it
STEP 2
Exploit the Bottleneck — get maximum output from the bottleneck resource without any further investment (eliminate downtime, prioritise the most profitable products through it)
STEP 3
Subordinate Everything Else — pace all other processes to match the bottleneck's rate; don't let non-bottleneck stations overproduce and pile up inventory
STEP 4
Elevate the Bottleneck — if still insufficient, invest in increasing the bottleneck's actual capacity (new machine, extra shift, outsourcing)
STEP 5
Repeat — once one bottleneck is resolved, another constraint will emerge elsewhere in the system — the cycle continues
24.3
Throughput Accounting Ratio & Product Ranking

When a bottleneck constrains total output, the key question becomes: which products should get priority access to the scarce bottleneck resource? Throughput Accounting provides a ranking method specifically for this.

Throughput Accounting Formulas
Throughput per Unit
Selling Price − Direct Material Cost per Unit
Throughput per Bottleneck Minute
Throughput per Unit ÷ Bottleneck Minutes Required per Unit
Total Factory Cost per Bottleneck Minute
Total Factory Costs (Labour + Overheads) ÷ Total Bottleneck Minutes Available
Throughput Accounting Ratio (TPAR)
Throughput per Bottleneck Minute ÷ Factory Cost per Bottleneck Minute
🔑 Interpreting the TPAR
A TPAR above 1.0 means the product generates more throughput per bottleneck minute than it costs the factory to run that minute — it's profitable to prioritise. A TPAR below 1.0 means the product is actually losing money once the true cost of tying up the scarce bottleneck resource is considered — even if it looks profitable on a traditional costing basis.

Worked Example — Ranking Products Through a Bottleneck

📋 Scenario: Two Products, One Bottleneck Machine

ItemProduct XProduct Y
Selling Price₹800₹1,200
Direct Material Cost₹300₹500
Throughput per Unit₹500₹700
Bottleneck Minutes Required10 mins20 mins
Throughput per Bottleneck Minute₹50/min₹35/min
Factory cost per bottleneck minute (given): ₹30/min
TPAR — Product X: ₹50 ÷ ₹30 = 1.67
TPAR — Product Y: ₹35 ÷ ₹30 = 1.17

Conclusion: Although Product Y has a higher selling price and higher throughput per unit, Product X generates significantly MORE throughput per scarce bottleneck minute (₹50 vs ₹35). With limited bottleneck capacity, Product X should be prioritised first — it's the more efficient user of the constrained resource, even though both products have TPAR above 1.0 and are worth producing.
24.4
Learning Curve Theory

When workers repeat a task, they get faster — this is a well-documented, measurable phenomenon, especially for complex, labour-intensive, repetitive tasks (like assembling a new aircraft model or building custom machinery). The Learning Curve quantifies this improvement, letting management accountants forecast labour costs more accurately for new products.

📉
The Core Principle
  • Every time CUMULATIVE OUTPUT doubles, the average time per unit falls to a fixed percentage of the previous average time
  • This fixed percentage is called the "learning rate" (e.g. 80% learning curve)
  • Applies most strongly to new, complex, labour-intensive production — not to automated or simple repetitive tasks
Core Concept
🎯
When Learning Curves Apply
  • Products/processes are NEW (learning has not yet plateaued)
  • Tasks are complex and predominantly manual/labour-driven
  • No significant breaks in production (learning effect can reset after a long stoppage)
  • The same workforce continues the task (learning is embodied in the people doing it)
Conditions for Use
24.5
Learning Curve — Worked Calculations

The learning curve is calculated using the formula below, then applied to forecast total and incremental labour time/cost for production batches.

Learning Curve Formula
Cumulative Average Time Formula
Y = a × X^b, where Y = cumulative average time per unit; a = time for the first unit; X = cumulative number of units produced; b = log(learning rate) ÷ log(2)
For an 80% Learning Curve
b = log(0.8) ÷ log(2) = −0.3219
Total Time for X Units
Cumulative Average Time (Y) × X (total units)
Incremental Time for a New Batch
Total Time for New Cumulative Total − Total Time for Previous Cumulative Total

Worked Example — 80% Learning Curve

📋 Scenario: New Product, First Unit Takes 100 Hours

Given: First unit = 100 hours. Learning rate = 80% (b = −0.3219). Find the average time per unit after 4 units, and the total time for 4 units.

Step 1 — Using the doubling shortcut (no logarithms needed for exact doublings):
Cumulative average after 1 unit = 100 hours
Cumulative average after 2 units = 100 × 80% = 80 hours (doubling from 1→2)
Cumulative average after 4 units = 80 × 80% = 64 hours (doubling from 2→4)

Step 2 — Total time for 4 units:
Total time = Cumulative Average × Units = 64 hours × 4 = 256 hours

Step 3 — Incremental time for units 3 and 4 (if units 1-2 already made):
Total time for 2 units = 80 × 2 = 160 hours
Incremental time for units 3-4 = 256 − 160 = 96 hours
💡 Exam Shortcut — The Doubling Method
When the required output level is an exact doubling of a known point (2, 4, 8, 16, 32 units...), you can skip the logarithm formula entirely and simply multiply the cumulative average time by the learning rate each time output doubles. The full Y = aX^b formula (using logs) is only needed when the output level ISN'T an exact power-of-2 multiple.
24.6
Applications & Limitations of the Learning Curve
✅
Practical Applications
  • Pricing new products — early units cost more to produce, informing early-stage pricing strategy
  • Budgeting and standard-setting for new production lines
  • Make-or-buy decisions — accounting for the fact that in-house production costs will fall as the workforce learns
  • Workforce planning and negotiating labour contracts for new product launches
Where It Helps
⚠️
Limitations
  • Assumes conditions remain stable — a strike, break in production, or major process change can reset or distort the learning curve
  • Learning eventually plateaus — the curve doesn't continue indefinitely; a "steady state" labour time is eventually reached
  • Assumes a constant learning rate throughout — reality may show a more variable rate
  • Not applicable to short runs, highly automated processes, or established/mature products
Real-World Caveats
24.7
Mix & Yield Variances

When a single production process uses MULTIPLE materials (or multiple labour grades) that can be substituted for one another to some degree, the standard material usage variance from Chapter 6 can be split further into two more insightful components: mix variance (did we use the right proportion of inputs?) and yield variance (did the process convert inputs to output efficiently?).

Mix & Yield Variance Formulas
Material Mix Variance
(Actual Total Quantity in Standard Mix Proportion − Actual Quantity Used) × Standard Price, for each material — summed
Material Yield Variance
(Standard Output-Based Input Quantity − Actual Total Input Quantity Used, in standard mix proportions) × Standard Weighted Average Cost per unit of input
Check
Mix Variance + Yield Variance = Total Material Usage Variance
24.8
Mix & Yield — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: Chemical Mixing Process — Two Inputs

Standard mix for 100kg of output: Material A: 60kg @ ₹40/kg; Material B: 40kg @ ₹25/kg. Standard total input = 100kg for 100kg output (1:1 — no expected loss for simplicity).

Actual results: Material A used: 66kg; Material B used: 44kg. Total actual input = 110kg, producing 105kg of actual output.

Step 1 — Standard mix of actual total input (110kg):
Material A in standard mix: 110kg × 60% = 66kg
Material B in standard mix: 110kg × 40% = 44kg

Step 2 — Mix Variance:
Material A: (66kg − 66kg) × ₹40 = ₹0
Material B: (44kg − 44kg) × ₹25 = ₹0
Total Mix Variance = ₹0 (the actual mix used matched the standard proportion exactly — 60:40 ratio was maintained even though more total material was used)

Step 3 — Yield Variance:
Standard input for 105kg actual output = 105kg (since standard is 1:1 input:output)
Actual total input used = 110kg
Standard weighted average cost per kg of input = (60×40 + 40×25)/100 = (2400+1000)/100 = ₹34/kg
Yield Variance = (105kg − 110kg) × ₹34 = ₹170 Adverse

Interpretation: The mix was perfectly on-standard (₹0 mix variance) — proportions were correct. But the process used 5kg MORE total input than the output achieved should have required (110kg in for only 105kg out), indicating inefficient conversion — an adverse yield variance of ₹170, pointing to process inefficiency, not a materials-mixing problem.
🔑 Why Splitting Mix & Yield Matters Operationally
A single "material usage variance" doesn't tell operations WHERE to focus improvement efforts. Splitting it reveals: an adverse MIX variance points to a materials-proportioning problem (wrong recipe followed); an adverse YIELD variance points to a process efficiency problem (waste, spoilage, poor conversion) — completely different root causes requiring completely different corrective actions.
24.9
Activity-Based Costing (ABC) Deep Dive

Traditional absorption costing spreads overheads using a single volume-based measure (e.g. labour hours) — which distorts costs when products consume overhead resources very differently. ABC assigns overhead costs based on the actual activities that DRIVE those costs.

STEP 1
Identify Major Activities — group overhead costs by the activity that causes them (e.g. machine setups, quality inspections, purchase ordering)
STEP 2
Identify Cost Drivers — find the factor that causes each activity's cost to change (e.g. number of setups, number of inspections, number of purchase orders)
STEP 3
Calculate Cost Driver Rates — Total Activity Cost ÷ Total Cost Driver Volume
STEP 4
Assign Costs to Products — multiply each product's actual consumption of each cost driver by the driver rate
💡 When ABC Matters Most
ABC provides the most value when a business has high overhead costs relative to direct costs, produces a diverse range of products consuming resources very differently (some high-volume/simple, others low-volume/complex), and where accurate product-level costing genuinely drives pricing and strategic decisions. For a simple, single-product factory, ABC's added complexity may not be worth the effort over traditional absorption costing.
24.10
ABC — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: Comparing Traditional Costing vs ABC for Two Products

Given: A factory produces two products — Product A (high volume, 8,000 units, simple, few setups) and Product B (low volume, 2,000 units, complex, many setups). Total overheads = ₹10,00,000, of which ₹6,00,000 relates to machine setups and ₹4,00,000 relates to general/volume-based overheads. Total labour hours = 20,000 (Product A: 16,000 hrs; Product B: 4,000 hrs). Total setups = 100 (Product A: 20 setups; Product B: 80 setups, since it's complex and made in many small batches).

Traditional Costing (absorbed on labour hours only):
Overhead rate = ₹10,00,000 ÷ 20,000 hrs = ₹50/labour hour
Product A overhead: 16,000 hrs × ₹50 = ₹8,00,000 → ₹100/unit (8,000 units)
Product B overhead: 4,000 hrs × ₹50 = ₹2,00,000 → ₹100/unit (2,000 units)
Both products absorb the SAME overhead per unit — despite Product B causing 4x more setups per unit produced.

ABC Costing (two cost pools, two drivers):
Setup cost driver rate = ₹6,00,000 ÷ 100 setups = ₹6,000/setup
General overhead rate = ₹4,00,000 ÷ 20,000 hrs = ₹20/labour hour

Product A: (20 setups × ₹6,000) + (16,000 hrs × ₹20) = ₹1,20,000 + ₹3,20,000 = ₹4,40,000 → ₹55/unit
Product B: (80 setups × ₹6,000) + (4,000 hrs × ₹20) = ₹4,80,000 + ₹80,000 = ₹5,60,000 → ₹280/unit

Interpretation: Traditional costing significantly UNDERSTATES Product B's true cost (₹100/unit vs the ABC-derived ₹280/unit) because it ignores that Product B drives disproportionately more setup activity. Traditional costing simultaneously OVERSTATES Product A's cost. This is "cost cross-subsidisation" — Product A's high volume was effectively subsidising Product B's true complexity cost, which could lead to Product B being underpriced and Product A being overpriced relative to their true costs.
24.11
Cost of Quality Framework

Quality-related costs are often scattered across many accounts and never visible as a single figure to management. The Cost of Quality (COQ) framework groups them into four categories, revealing the true cost of quality (and the cost of poor quality) explicitly.

CategoryDefinitionExample
Prevention CostsCosts incurred to PREVENT defects from occurring in the first placeQuality training, supplier quality audits, process design improvements
Appraisal CostsCosts of CHECKING/inspecting to identify defects before they reach the customerInspection, testing, quality audits of finished goods
Internal Failure CostsCosts when a defect is caught BEFORE reaching the customerScrap, rework, re-inspection, downtime from quality issues
External Failure CostsCosts when a defect reaches the CUSTOMERWarranty claims, product recalls, customer complaints handling, lost future sales/reputation damage
🔑 The Core Insight of Cost of Quality
Spending MORE on prevention and appraisal (getting it right the first time) typically REDUCES the much larger, more damaging costs of internal and especially external failure. External failure costs are usually the most expensive category by far — a single product recall or major warranty crisis can dwarf years of accumulated prevention spending — which is why quality-focused organisations deliberately over-invest in prevention relative to what might seem "necessary" on a narrow cost basis.
24.12
Target Costing & Life Cycle Costing
🎯
Target Costing
  • Starts with the market price the product must sell at, works BACKWARD to the maximum allowable cost
  • Formula: Target Cost = Target Selling Price − Target (Required) Profit Margin
  • If current estimated cost exceeds target cost, a "cost gap" exists — must be closed through design changes, value engineering, or supplier negotiation BEFORE launch
Price-Led Costing
🔄
Life Cycle Costing
  • Tracks and accumulates ALL costs across a product's entire life — from R&D and design, through production, to eventual withdrawal/disposal
  • Counters the tendency to focus only on production-phase costs, ignoring large upfront R&D/design costs or end-of-life decommissioning costs
  • Reveals the TRUE total profitability of a product over its full life, not just a single accounting period
Whole-of-Life View
24.13
Environmental Management Accounting

As ESG considerations become central to business strategy, management accounting has evolved to specifically track environmental costs — which are traditionally buried in general overheads and hidden from decision-makers.

🔑 Key Environmental Cost Categories
Conventional costs (energy, water, raw materials with environmental impact) are often already tracked but not separately visible. Contingent costs (future clean-up, potential fines) are often unrecognised until they occur. Image/relationship costs (reputational impact of poor environmental performance) are the hardest to quantify but increasingly material to brand value and access to capital — investors and lenders now often price these into ESG-linked financing terms.

24.16
Common Mistakes, Flashcards & Interview Q&A

Part A — Common Mistakes (Don't Repeat These)

❌ MISTAKE 1
Including Labour as Variable in Throughput Accounting
Throughput Accounting deliberately treats only direct materials as variable in the short run — labour and overheads are "factory cost," treated as fixed. Calculating throughput per unit by deducting labour cost (in addition to materials) contradicts the entire TA framework.
❌ MISTAKE 2
Ranking Products by Contribution Instead of TPAR When a Bottleneck Exists
When a scarce bottleneck resource exists, ranking products by absolute contribution or contribution margin % ignores how much of the SCARCE resource each product consumes. Always rank by contribution/throughput PER UNIT OF THE SCARCE RESOURCE (or TPAR) once a binding constraint is identified.
❌ MISTAKE 3
Applying the Learning Curve Formula When Output Isn't an Exact Doubling
The simple doubling shortcut only works for exact powers of 2 (2, 4, 8, 16 units). For any other output level (e.g. 5 units, 10 units), the full logarithmic formula Y = aX^b must be used — applying the doubling shortcut incorrectly to non-doubling output levels gives a wrong answer.
❌ MISTAKE 4
Forgetting the Learning Curve Eventually Plateaus
Applying the learning curve formula indefinitely, assuming costs keep falling forever, ignores that a "steady state" is eventually reached where further learning gains become negligible. Using the formula far beyond its valid range overstates efficiency gains.
❌ MISTAKE 5
Confusing Mix Variance Direction With Yield Variance Direction
A favourable mix variance (using a cheaper-than-standard combination of materials) can sometimes cause an ADVERSE yield variance (a cheaper mix might produce lower-quality output or more waste) — the two can move in opposite directions, and both should be analysed together, not assumed to always align.
❌ MISTAKE 6
Using a Single Volume-Based Overhead Rate for Genuinely Diverse Products
As the ABC worked example (24.10) shows, using one blanket labour-hour-based overhead rate for products with very different complexity/setup profiles systematically miscosts them — high-complexity, low-volume products get undercosted, and simple high-volume products get overcosted, potentially leading to bad pricing decisions on both.
❌ MISTAKE 7
Under-Investing in Prevention Because It's "Not an Emergency"
Since prevention and appraisal costs are visible and controllable NOW, while failure costs (especially external failure) are often deferred and less visible until a crisis hits, organisations under pressure to cut costs frequently cut prevention spending first — exactly the opposite of what the Cost of Quality framework recommends, since this typically increases far larger failure costs later.
❌ MISTAKE 8
Treating Target Costing as a One-Time Exercise
Target costing is meant to be an ITERATIVE process throughout design and development — continuously value-engineering the product until the cost gap closes — not a single calculation done once at the start and then ignored as the design evolves.
❌ MISTAKE 9
Ignoring Life Cycle Costs When Evaluating a New Product's Profitability
Evaluating a new product's profitability using only its production-phase costs and revenue ignores potentially significant upfront R&D/design costs and end-of-life decommissioning costs — a product that looks profitable period-by-period during production may be unprofitable across its FULL life cycle once these are included.

Part C — Interview Questions & Model Answers

QuestionWhat They Are TestingModel Answer
"How would you decide which products to prioritise if a factory has a production bottleneck?"Applied understanding of throughput accounting and constraint managementI'd first confirm which specific resource is actually the binding constraint, then calculate the Throughput Accounting Ratio for each product — throughput per bottleneck minute divided by factory cost per bottleneck minute — rather than simply ranking by overall profit margin. Products with the highest TPAR generate the most value per unit of the scarce resource and should be prioritised, since maximizing output through the bottleneck maximizes overall factory profit.
"Why might a company use the learning curve when pricing a brand-new, complex product?"Practical application of learning curve theory to pricing strategyEarly production units of a complex new product typically take significantly longer (and cost more) than later units, as the workforce becomes more efficient. If pricing is based only on the first unit's cost, the product may be priced too high to be competitive; the learning curve lets a company forecast average costs across a full production run, supporting more realistic and competitive pricing from launch.
"What's the difference between mix variance and yield variance, and why split them?"Depth of understanding beyond basic variance analysisMix variance isolates whether the actual proportion of different materials used matched the standard recipe; yield variance isolates whether the overall production process converted inputs into output efficiently. Splitting them matters because they point to entirely different root causes and corrective actions — a mix problem means the wrong ratio of ingredients was used, while a yield problem means the process itself is wasteful or inefficient, regardless of whether the mix was correct.
"Why might traditional absorption costing lead a company to make bad pricing decisions?"Applied understanding of ABC's practical business impactTraditional costing spreads overhead using a single volume-based measure like labour hours, which understates the true cost of complex, low-volume products (that consume disproportionate setup/inspection resources) and overstates the cost of simple, high-volume products. This can lead a company to underprice its complex products (thinking they're cheaper to make than they really are) while overpricing its simple products — potentially losing competitiveness on the simple products while unknowingly selling complex ones at a loss.
"How would you convince a cost-conscious management team to invest more in quality prevention?"Communication and business-case-building skillI'd use the Cost of Quality framework to show that failure costs — especially external failure costs like recalls and warranty claims — are typically far larger than prevention costs, even though prevention spending is more visible and immediate. I'd try to quantify the organisation's own historical failure costs to make the case concrete, showing that a relatively modest increase in prevention spending could plausibly avoid a much larger, though less predictable, failure cost down the line.
