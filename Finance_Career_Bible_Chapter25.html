Working capital management (covered at foundation level in earlier chapters) becomes genuinely powerful when paired with quantitative optimisation models. How much inventory should you actually order, and when? How much cash should a treasury team hold, and when should it move cash into short-term investments? How does a company protect itself from currency movements on a cross-border deal? This chapter answers all three with the specific mathematical models ACCA FM tests — and that real treasury departments actually use.

"Working capital management isn't about avoiding risk — it's about holding exactly the right amount of it, no more, no less."
25.1
Why Working Capital Needs Quantitative Models

Holding too much inventory or cash ties up capital that could be earning a return elsewhere. Holding too little risks stockouts, missed sales, or an inability to pay bills. The models in this chapter find the mathematically optimal balance point.

ResourceToo Much = Too Little = Optimisation Model
InventoryExcess holding cost, obsolescence risk, tied-up cashStockouts, lost sales, production stoppagesEconomic Order Quantity (EOQ)
CashOpportunity cost of idle cash (foregone investment return)Inability to pay bills, forced borrowing at unfavourable ratesMiller-Orr Model
Currency ExposureOver-hedging costs money and removes upsideUnder-hedging exposes profit to adverse currency swingsForward/Option Hedging
25.2
Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) — The Model

EOQ answers a precise question: what order quantity minimises the total cost of ordering and holding inventory? It balances two opposing costs — order LARGE quantities infrequently, and you save on ordering costs but pay more to hold inventory; order SMALL quantities frequently, and holding costs fall but ordering costs rise.

EOQ Formula
Economic Order Quantity
EOQ = √[(2 × D × Co) ÷ Ch]
D = Annual Demand (units); Co = Cost per Order; Ch = Holding Cost per unit per year
Total Annual Ordering Cost
(D ÷ EOQ) × Co
Total Annual Holding Cost
(EOQ ÷ 2) × Ch
Key Insight at EOQ
At the EOQ point, Total Ordering Cost EXACTLY EQUALS Total Holding Cost — this is the mathematical minimum of total inventory cost
25.3
EOQ — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: A Retailer's Annual Inventory Order

Given: Annual demand (D) = 10,000 units. Cost per order (Co) = ₹500. Holding cost per unit per year (Ch) = ₹8.

Step 1 — Calculate EOQ:
EOQ = √[(2 × 10,000 × 500) ÷ 8] = √[10,000,000 ÷ 8] = √1,250,000 = 1,118 units (rounded)

Step 2 — Number of orders per year:
10,000 ÷ 1,118 = 8.94 ≈ 9 orders per year

Step 3 — Total annual ordering cost:
(10,000 ÷ 1,118) × ₹500 = 8.94 × 500 = ₹4,472

Step 4 — Total annual holding cost:
(1,118 ÷ 2) × ₹8 = 559 × 8 = ₹4,472

Verification: Ordering cost (₹4,472) = Holding cost (₹4,472) — confirming this IS the EOQ minimum point, exactly as the theory predicts.
Total Annual Cost: ₹4,472 + ₹4,472 = ₹8,944
25.4
EOQ With Quantity Discounts

Suppliers often offer discounts for ordering in larger quantities. The basic EOQ model must be adjusted to check whether taking the discount (even at a higher holding cost) reduces TOTAL cost overall.

STEP 1
Calculate the basic EOQ ignoring the discount
STEP 2
Calculate total cost (purchase + ordering + holding) at the basic EOQ order quantity
STEP 3
Calculate total cost (purchase + ordering + holding) at the MINIMUM quantity needed to qualify for the discount
STEP 4
Compare total costs — choose whichever order quantity gives the LOWEST overall total cost, even if it means ordering above the mathematical EOQ
🔑 Key Point
Purchase cost must be included in the comparison once discounts are involved — the discount saves money on the PURCHASE cost even though holding cost rises with a larger order quantity. The basic EOQ formula alone doesn't capture this trade-off.
25.5
Reorder Levels & Safety Stock
Reorder & Safety Stock Formulas
Reorder Level (no uncertainty)
Average Daily Usage × Lead Time (days)
Reorder Level (with safety stock)
(Average Daily Usage × Lead Time) + Safety Stock
Maximum Stock Level
Reorder Level + Reorder Quantity − (Minimum Usage × Minimum Lead Time)
Minimum Stock Level
Reorder Level − (Average Usage × Average Lead Time)

Safety stock provides a buffer against uncertainty in demand or supplier lead time — but holding it is not free, so the amount held should reflect a genuine trade-off between stockout risk/cost and additional holding cost.

25.6
The Miller-Orr Cash Management Model

While EOQ optimises inventory, the Miller-Orr model answers the equivalent question for CASH: given that daily cash flows are unpredictable (unlike steady, predictable inventory usage), how should a treasury team decide when to move cash into/out of short-term investments?

📊
The Core Idea
  • Sets THREE cash balance levels: a lower limit, an upper limit, and a "return point"
  • Cash balance is left alone to fluctuate randomly (day to day) between the lower and upper limits
  • When cash hits the UPPER limit → invest the excess to bring balance back down to the return point
  • When cash hits the LOWER limit → sell investments to bring balance back up to the return point
3-Limit System
⚖️
Why It's Better Than a Simple Rule
  • Unlike EOQ (which assumes steady, predictable demand), cash flows are genuinely random day-to-day
  • Miller-Orr is specifically designed for this randomness — it doesn't try to predict cash flows, just manages the RANGE they're allowed to fluctuate within
  • Minimises the total cost of transferring cash to/from investments, balanced against the opportunity cost of holding idle cash
Handles Uncertainty
Miller-Orr Model Formulas
Spread (Upper − Lower Limit)
3 × [(0.75 × Transaction Cost × Variance of Daily Cash Flows) ÷ Interest Rate]^(1/3)
Return Point
Lower Limit + (Spread ÷ 3)
Upper Limit
Lower Limit + Spread
25.7
Miller-Orr — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: Setting Cash Control Limits

Given: Lower limit (set by management, minimum safety cash) = ₹10,000. Transaction cost per transfer = ₹50. Daily interest rate = 0.025% (0.00025). Standard deviation of daily cash flows = ₹2,000 (so variance = 2,000² = 4,000,000).

Step 1 — Calculate the Spread:
Spread = 3 × [(0.75 × 50 × 4,000,000) ÷ 0.00025]^(1/3)
= 3 × [150,000,000 ÷ 0.00025]^(1/3)
= 3 × [600,000,000,000]^(1/3)
= 3 × 8,434 (approx cube root)
= ₹25,302 (rounded)

Step 2 — Upper Limit:
Upper Limit = ₹10,000 + ₹25,302 = ₹35,302

Step 3 — Return Point:
Return Point = ₹10,000 + (₹25,302 ÷ 3) = ₹10,000 + ₹8,434 = ₹18,434

Interpretation: Cash is allowed to fluctuate freely between ₹10,000 and ₹35,302. If it hits ₹35,302, the treasury team invests ₹16,868 (35,302 − 18,434) to bring it back to the ₹18,434 return point. If it falls to ₹10,000, they liquidate ₹8,434 of investments to bring it back up to ₹18,434.
25.8
Receivables Management — Credit Policy & Factoring
📋
Credit Policy Decisions
  • Trade-off: looser credit terms boost sales volume but increase receivables financing cost and bad debt risk
  • Evaluate proposed policy changes by comparing the INCREMENTAL contribution from extra sales against the incremental costs (financing + bad debts)
  • Early settlement discounts trade a small margin reduction for faster cash collection — evaluate using the effective annual cost of the discount
Credit Terms
💵
Factoring & Invoice Discounting
  • Factoring: a third party (factor) takes over receivables management and collection, advancing a % of invoice value immediately, for a fee
  • Invoice Discounting: similar cash-advance mechanism but the company retains control of collection and the customer relationship — often confidential
  • Both improve cash flow immediately at the cost of a financing fee — useful for rapidly growing businesses with cash tied up in receivables
Accelerating Cash
25.9
Credit Policy — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: Should the Company Relax Its Credit Terms?

Given: Current annual sales = ₹5 Crore, all on 30-day credit terms, contribution margin = 40%, current bad debts = 1% of sales, current receivables financing cost = 10% p.a.
Proposed policy: extend credit terms to 60 days, expected to increase sales by 20% (to ₹6 Crore), but bad debts would rise to 2% of sales.

Step 1 — Incremental contribution from extra sales:
Incremental sales = ₹6 Cr − ₹5 Cr = ₹1 Cr
Incremental contribution = ₹1 Cr × 40% = ₹40,00,000 (benefit)

Step 2 — Incremental bad debts:
Old bad debts = ₹5 Cr × 1% = ₹5,00,000
New bad debts = ₹6 Cr × 2% = ₹12,00,000
Incremental bad debts = ₹12,00,000 − ₹5,00,000 = ₹7,00,000 (cost)

Step 3 — Incremental receivables financing cost:
Old average receivables = ₹5 Cr × (30/365) = ₹41,09,589
New average receivables = ₹6 Cr × (60/365) = ₹98,63,014
Incremental receivables investment = ₹98,63,014 − ₹41,09,589 = ₹57,53,425
Incremental financing cost = ₹57,53,425 × 10% = ₹5,75,343 (cost)

Step 4 — Net effect of the policy change:
Benefit: ₹40,00,000
Costs: ₹7,00,000 + ₹5,75,343 = ₹12,75,343
Net benefit = ₹40,00,000 − ₹12,75,343 = ₹27,24,657 — POLICY SHOULD BE ADOPTED

Interpretation: Even though bad debts more than double in percentage terms and financing costs rise, the incremental contribution from higher sales volume comfortably outweighs both incremental costs — this incremental (marginal) analysis approach, comparing ALL costs and benefits of a proposed change, is the standard ACCA FM/AFM technique for any credit policy decision.
25.10
Foreign Exchange Risk — Types & Exposure

Any company with international revenue, costs, or financing faces currency risk. ACCA FM identifies three distinct types of exposure, each requiring different management approaches.

Exposure TypeWhat It IsExample
Transaction ExposureRisk on a SPECIFIC, already-agreed future transaction in a foreign currencyAn Indian exporter has agreed to receive $100,000 in 90 days for goods already shipped
Translation ExposureRisk from consolidating foreign subsidiary financial statements into the parent's reporting currencyAn Indian parent company's UK subsidiary's Balance Sheet, translated from GBP to INR, changes in value purely due to exchange rate movement
Economic ExposureThe broader, long-term impact of exchange rate movements on a company's competitive position and future cash flowsA sustained rupee appreciation makes an Indian exporter's products less price-competitive globally over time
25.11
Forex Hedging Techniques
📝
Forward Contracts
  • Locks in an exchange rate today for a transaction settling at a specific future date
  • Fully eliminates uncertainty — but also removes any upside if the rate moves favourably
  • Simplest, most commonly used hedge — offered by virtually all banks
Lock the Rate
🎯
Currency Options
  • Gives the RIGHT (not obligation) to exchange at a set rate — buyer pays a premium for this flexibility
  • Protects against adverse movement while still allowing the company to benefit if the rate moves favourably
  • More expensive than a forward (due to the premium) but more flexible
Flexible Protection
🔄
Money Market Hedge
  • Uses borrowing/lending/deposits in the two currencies to synthetically fix the exchange rate outcome, without a formal forward or option contract
  • Achieves an economically equivalent result to a forward contract, using the money markets instead
Synthetic Hedge
🤝
Natural Hedging
  • Matching foreign currency inflows and outflows internally (e.g. borrowing in the same currency as foreign revenue)
  • No transaction cost, but requires the right natural business structure to work
  • Multinationals actively structure operations to maximise natural hedging opportunities
No-Cost Hedge
25.12
Currency Option — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: Comparing a Currency Option to a Forward Contract

Facts: The same Indian importer from the forward contract example (25.13) needs to pay $500,000 in 3 months. Instead of a forward, they buy a CALL option on USD (the right to buy USD) at a strike price of ₹83.60/$, paying a premium of ₹1.50/$ (total premium = $500,000 × ₹1.50 = ₹7,50,000, paid upfront).

Scenario A — Rupee weakens to ₹86/$ at settlement:
The option is "in the money" — company EXERCISES the option, buying USD at the strike rate ₹83.60 instead of the market rate ₹86.
Cost: $500,000 × ₹83.60 = ₹4,18,00,000 + premium ₹7,50,000 = ₹4,25,50,000 total
(Without any hedge, cost would have been $500,000 × ₹86 = ₹4,30,00,000 — the option saved money AND capped the downside)

Scenario B — Rupee strengthens to ₹81/$ at settlement:
The option is "out of the money" — company LETS THE OPTION LAPSE, buying USD at the better market rate ₹81 instead.
Cost: $500,000 × ₹81 = ₹4,05,00,000 + premium ₹7,50,000 = ₹4,12,50,000 total
(Under a forward hedge locked at ₹83.60, the cost would have been a FIXED ₹4,18,00,000 regardless — the option holder benefits from the favourable rate movement, something a forward contract holder cannot do)

Key Comparison: The option costs an extra ₹7,50,000 premium upfront compared to a "free" forward contract — but in exchange, it preserves the ability to benefit if the rupee strengthens (Scenario B), while a forward locks in a single fixed rate regardless of which way the market moves. The option is the more expensive but more flexible hedge; the forward is free but rigid.
25.13
Forward Contract — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: Indian Importer Hedging a USD Payable

Facts: An Indian company must pay a US supplier $500,000 in 3 months. Current spot rate: ₹83.00/$. 3-month forward rate quoted by the bank: ₹83.60/$.

Without hedging: The company is exposed to whatever the spot rate is in 3 months — if the rupee weakens to ₹85/$, the payment would cost ₹4,25,00,000 instead of the ₹4,15,00,000 it would cost today.

With a forward hedge: The company locks in ₹83.60/$ today, so regardless of what the spot rate actually is in 3 months, the payment will cost exactly:
$500,000 × ₹83.60 = ₹4,18,00,000

Trade-off: If the rupee actually strengthens to ₹81/$ by the payment date, the company would have paid LESS (₹4,05,00,000) without the hedge — but it has certainty and protection against the downside scenario, which is the entire point of hedging: removing uncertainty, not maximising every possible outcome.

Treasury Function in Indian Companies — Real World Context

Indian corporate treasury operations are shaped by RBI regulations on foreign exchange, which differ meaningfully from fully open capital account jurisdictions.

AreaIndian Context
Forex RegulationGoverned by FEMA (Foreign Exchange Management Act) — RBI regulates permissible hedging instruments and documentation requirements
Hedging InstrumentsForwards, options, and currency swaps are available through Authorised Dealer banks; must generally relate to genuine underlying exposure (not pure speculation)
Cash ManagementLarge Indian corporates increasingly use cash pooling and centralised treasury structures to optimise group-wide cash and reduce idle balances across subsidiaries
Working Capital FinancingCash credit and overdraft facilities remain the dominant short-term financing tools for Indian businesses, alongside commercial paper for larger corporates

Common Mistakes, Flashcards & Interview Q&A

Part A — Common Mistakes (Don't Repeat These)

❌ MISTAKE 1
Forgetting to Include Purchase Cost in EOQ Discount Comparisons
When evaluating whether to take a quantity discount, the total cost comparison must include the PURCHASE cost (which falls with the discount), not just ordering and holding costs — comparing only ordering + holding costs misses the entire point of the discount decision.
❌ MISTAKE 2
Using Standard Deviation Instead of Variance in the Miller-Orr Formula
The Miller-Orr spread formula requires the VARIANCE of daily cash flows, not the standard deviation. Since variance = standard deviation squared, plugging in standard deviation directly (without squaring it) gives a significantly wrong spread.
❌ MISTAKE 3
Confusing Transaction and Translation Exposure
Transaction exposure relates to a SPECIFIC, already-committed cash flow (hedgeable with forwards/options). Translation exposure arises purely from consolidation accounting and doesn't involve actual cash flows — hedging it (if done at all) uses different techniques and is often considered lower priority than transaction exposure.
❌ MISTAKE 4
Assuming Hedging Always Improves the Financial Outcome
Hedging REMOVES uncertainty — it doesn't guarantee a better outcome than staying unhedged. If the exchange rate happens to move favourably, an unhedged position would have been cheaper. The value of hedging lies in certainty and risk reduction, not in guaranteed profit.
❌ MISTAKE 5
Applying EOQ to Situations With Highly Variable Demand
The basic EOQ model assumes constant, predictable demand and lead time. Applying it directly to volatile, unpredictable demand situations (without incorporating safety stock or a more sophisticated model) can lead to frequent stockouts despite "optimal" ordering.
❌ MISTAKE 6
Only Considering Bad Debts When Evaluating a Credit Policy Change
As the worked example in 25.9 shows, a full credit policy evaluation must include incremental contribution from extra sales, incremental bad debts, AND incremental financing cost from higher average receivables — evaluating only the bad debt impact in isolation, without weighing it against the sales benefit, leads to systematically rejecting policies that are actually value-creating.
❌ MISTAKE 7
Forgetting the Option Premium Is Paid Regardless of Whether the Option Is Exercised
Unlike a forward contract (no upfront cost), an option's premium is paid whether or not the option ends up being exercised — it's the cost of the flexibility itself. Comparing only the "exercise cost" of an option to a forward's locked rate, while ignoring the premium already paid, understates the option's true total cost.
❌ MISTAKE 8
Choosing a Forward Over an Option Purely Because It's "Free"
A forward contract has no upfront premium, but it eliminates ALL upside if the exchange rate moves favourably — while an option costs a premium but preserves that upside. The right choice depends on the company's risk appetite and view on likely currency movement, not simply which hedge appears cheaper at first glance.

Part C — Interview Questions & Model Answers

QuestionWhat They Are TestingModel Answer
"How would you decide how much cash a company should hold?"Applied understanding of cash management trade-offsToo much idle cash has an opportunity cost — it could be earning a return elsewhere; too little risks being unable to meet obligations. Models like Miller-Orr formalise this by setting a range within which cash is allowed to fluctuate, only actively moving cash to/from short-term investments when it hits the boundaries — balancing transaction costs of moving cash against the opportunity cost of holding it idle.
"An Indian exporter is worried about the rupee strengthening before they get paid in dollars. What are their options?"Practical forex risk management knowledgeThey could use a forward contract to lock in today's exchange rate for the future receipt, eliminating uncertainty entirely. Alternatively, a currency option would let them benefit if the rupee actually weakens (keeping upside) while still protecting against it strengthening, at the cost of an upfront premium. A money market hedge could achieve a similar locked-in outcome using borrowing/lending in the two currencies instead of a formal derivative contract.
"Walk me through the trade-off in the EOQ model."Core understanding of a fundamental working capital modelOrdering large quantities infrequently reduces total ordering costs (fewer orders means fewer fixed order costs) but increases holding costs (more average inventory sitting in the warehouse). Ordering small quantities frequently does the opposite. EOQ finds the exact order quantity where these two opposing costs are minimized in total — and mathematically, at that exact point, total ordering cost equals total holding cost.
"When would you recommend an option over a forward contract for hedging currency risk?"Applied judgment on hedge selectionAn option makes sense when the company wants protection against adverse currency movement but still wants to benefit if the rate moves favourably, and is willing to pay a premium for that flexibility — useful when there's a genuine view that the rate could move either way, or the exposure itself is uncertain (e.g. a bid for a contract that may not be won). A forward is more appropriate when the exposure is certain and the priority is complete cost certainty rather than preserving upside potential.
"How would you evaluate whether to offer a customer more generous credit terms?"Practical working capital decision-makingI'd run an incremental analysis comparing the additional contribution from any resulting sales increase against the additional costs — higher bad debt risk and the financing cost of carrying larger average receivables balances for longer. If the incremental benefit exceeds the incremental costs, the policy change creates value; if I'm uncertain about the sales response assumption, I'd sensitivity-test it to see how much the sales increase estimate would need to fall before the decision reverses.
