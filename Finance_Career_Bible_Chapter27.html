A large organisation with multiple divisions faces a fundamental performance management challenge: how do you measure and reward divisional managers fairly, when their decisions affect (and are affected by) other divisions? Advanced Performance Management builds on PM's foundational tools (Chapter 6 and 24) to tackle the genuinely hard problems of large, complex, multi-divisional organisations — where measurement systems shape BEHAVIOUR, not just report on it.

"What gets measured gets managed — which means a badly designed performance metric doesn't just fail to help. It actively drives the wrong behaviour."

27.2
Divisional Performance — ROI vs RI

When a company is organised into divisions treated as "investment centres" (responsible for profit AND the capital invested to generate it), two competing measures are used to judge divisional performance — each with very different behavioural consequences.

Divisional Performance Formulas
Return on Investment (ROI)
Divisional Profit ÷ Divisional Capital Employed × 100
Residual Income (RI)
Divisional Profit − (Divisional Capital Employed × Company's Required/Cost of Capital Rate)
📊
ROI's Behavioural Problem
  • A % measure — divisional managers may REJECT a genuinely good new investment if its ROI is below their division's CURRENT (high) ROI, even if it's above the company's cost of capital
  • This is a classic case of measurement systems causing dysfunctional decision-making — "goal incongruence"
Percentage Measure
💰
RI's Advantage
  • An absolute ₹ measure — any investment earning ABOVE the required cost of capital rate increases RI
  • Encourages managers to accept ALL value-creating investments, regardless of the division's current average return
  • Better aligns divisional manager incentives with overall company (shareholder) value creation
Absolute Measure — Better Aligned
27.3
ROI vs RI — Worked Example & Goal Congruence

📋 Scenario: Should Division X Accept a New Project?

Given: Division X currently has Profit = ₹40 Lakh, Capital Employed = ₹200 Lakh → Current ROI = 20%. Company's required return (cost of capital) = 12%.
A new project requires ₹50 Lakh capital and generates ₹8 Lakh profit → Project ROI = 16%.

Under ROI (the problem):
New combined ROI = (₹40L + ₹8L) ÷ (₹200L + ₹50L) = ₹48L ÷ ₹250L = 19.2%
This is LOWER than the division's current 20% ROI — the manager, evaluated on ROI, would likely REJECT this project to protect their reported ROI, even though the project's 16% return is well above the company's 12% required rate.

Under RI (the fix):
Current RI = ₹40L − (₹200L × 12%) = ₹40L − ₹24L = ₹16L
Project's own RI = ₹8L − (₹50L × 12%) = ₹8L − ₹6L = ₹2L (positive)
New combined RI = ₹16L + ₹2L = ₹18L (an INCREASE from ₹16L)

Conclusion: RI correctly signals this project SHOULD be accepted (it increases residual income), while ROI incorrectly signals it should be REJECTED (it dilutes the division's percentage return) — this is the textbook example of ROI causing goal incongruence, and exactly why RI (and later, EVA) is generally considered the superior divisional performance measure.
27.4
Economic Value Added (EVA)

EVA is a refinement of RI, developed to more accurately reflect ECONOMIC (rather than purely accounting) profit and capital, by making specific adjustments to standard accounting figures.

EVA Formula
EVA
NOPAT (Net Operating Profit After Tax, Adjusted) − (WACC × Adjusted Capital Employed)
Common Adjustments
Add back R&D expenditure (capitalise it, since it creates future economic value like an asset); adjust for operating leases; remove non-cash provisions' distorting effects
🔑 Why EVA Adjustments Matter
Standard accounting expenses R&D immediately, which penalises innovative divisions in a standard RI calculation (heavy R&D spend reduces current profit, even though it builds future value). EVA capitalises R&D and other value-building "investments" that accounting treats as expenses — better reflecting genuine long-term economic performance, though at the cost of more complex calculation.
27.5
EVA — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: Calculating EVA With R&D Adjustment

Given: Division reports accounting Net Operating Profit After Tax (NOPAT) = ₹8 Crore. During the year, ₹2 Crore was spent on R&D and fully expensed under normal accounting rules. Capital Employed (accounting basis) = ₹40 Crore. WACC = 12%. For EVA purposes, R&D is capitalised and amortised over 4 years (i.e., added back to profit less one year's amortisation, and added to capital employed).

Step 1 — Adjust NOPAT for EVA purposes:
Add back full R&D expense: +₹2 Crore
Less EVA amortisation of R&D (₹2 Cr ÷ 4 years): −₹0.5 Crore
Adjusted NOPAT = ₹8 Cr + ₹2 Cr − ₹0.5 Cr = ₹9.5 Crore

Step 2 — Adjust Capital Employed for EVA purposes:
Add back the R&D asset now being "capitalised" (unamortised balance, assumed ₹2 Cr in year of spend): +₹2 Crore
Adjusted Capital Employed = ₹40 Cr + ₹2 Cr = ₹42 Crore

Step 3 — Calculate EVA:
EVA = Adjusted NOPAT − (WACC × Adjusted Capital Employed)
= ₹9.5 Cr − (12% × ₹42 Cr) = ₹9.5 Cr − ₹5.04 Cr = ₹4.46 Crore

Compare to standard RI (no adjustments):
RI = ₹8 Cr − (12% × ₹40 Cr) = ₹8 Cr − ₹4.8 Cr = ₹3.2 Crore

Interpretation: EVA (₹4.46 Cr) is HIGHER than standard RI (₹3.2 Cr) here, because capitalising R&D instead of fully expensing it in the current year better reflects that R&D builds long-term value rather than being a pure current-period cost — this prevents penalising an innovative division for its R&D investment in the same period it's spent, giving a truer picture of genuine economic value creation.
27.6
The Balanced Scorecard — Strategic Application

The Balanced Scorecard (introduced at foundation level in Chapter 6) becomes a genuinely strategic tool at APM level — used to translate high-level strategy into specific, measurable, cascaded objectives across the organisation.

PerspectiveStrategic QuestionExample Measures
FinancialHow do we look to shareholders?ROI, RI, revenue growth, cost efficiency
CustomerHow do customers see us?Market share, customer satisfaction, retention rate
Internal Business ProcessWhat must we excel at internally?Cycle time, defect rate, process innovation
Learning & GrowthCan we continue to improve and create value?Employee skills/training, IT capability, innovation pipeline
🔑 The Strategic Insight — Cause and Effect Linkages
At APM level, the Balanced Scorecard is understood as a chain of CAUSE AND EFFECT: investment in Learning & Growth (e.g. staff training) improves Internal Processes (e.g. fewer errors), which improves the Customer perspective (e.g. satisfaction), which ultimately drives the Financial perspective (e.g. revenue growth). This "strategy map" logic — rather than treating the four perspectives as a simple checklist — is what distinguishes sophisticated Balanced Scorecard application from a superficial one, and is specifically what APM exams test.

Building Block Model for Service Businesses

Fitzgerald and Moon's Building Block Model was developed specifically to address performance measurement challenges unique to SERVICE businesses (as opposed to manufacturing), where output is intangible, simultaneous with consumption, and harder to standardise.

🧱
Dimensions (What to Measure)
  • Competitive: market share, sales growth
  • Financial: profitability, liquidity, capital structure
  • Quality of Service: reliability, responsiveness, competence, courtesy
  • Flexibility: ability to adapt to changing customer needs
  • Resource Utilisation: efficiency of resource use
  • Innovation: ability to develop new services/processes
6 Dimensions
🎯
Standards (How to Set Targets)
  • Standards must be: OWNED by those being measured (buy-in), ACHIEVABLE (realistic), and EQUITABLE (fair across different units/individuals)
Target-Setting Principles
🏆
Rewards (How to Motivate)
  • Reward systems must be CLEAR (understood), MOTIVATING (staff actually care about them), and CONTROLLABLE (staff can genuinely influence the outcome being rewarded)
Motivation Principles
💡 Why Service Businesses Need a Different Model
Manufacturing performance frameworks often over-emphasise easily quantifiable output measures (units produced, defect rates), which don't translate well to services where quality is often subjective and experienced differently by each customer (e.g. a consulting engagement, a hospital visit, a legal service). The Building Block Model explicitly incorporates service-specific dimensions like flexibility and quality of service that a purely manufacturing-oriented framework would miss entirely.
27.8
Transfer Pricing — Why It Matters

When one division of a company sells goods/services to ANOTHER division of the same company, a "transfer price" must be set for that internal transaction. This single price determines the SELLING division's revenue and the BUYING division's cost — meaning it directly reallocates reported profit between divisions, even though no external money changes hands.

🔑 The Core Tension
A transfer price that's fair and motivating for the selling division may be unfair and demotivating for the buying division, and vice versa. The ideal transfer price should: (1) promote goal congruence — the price that's right for divisions should also be right for the company overall, (2) preserve divisional autonomy where possible, and (3) allow fair performance evaluation of each division independently.
27.9
Transfer Pricing Methods
MethodHow It WorksKey Issue
Market PriceTransfer price = the price the selling division could get selling externallyBest when a genuine external market exists — gives fair, objective pricing signal
Cost-Based (Full Cost)Transfer price = full absorption cost of productionSelling division earns NO profit on internal sales — demotivating, doesn't reflect true value
Cost-PlusTransfer price = Cost + a profit marginFairer than pure cost, but the margin % is often arbitrarily set and can still cause disputes
Marginal CostTransfer price = variable/marginal cost onlyPromotes goal congruence for short-term capacity decisions, but selling division earns zero contribution — poor for performance evaluation
Negotiated PriceDivisions negotiate the price between themselves, like independent businessesPreserves autonomy and can find a fair middle ground, but can be time-consuming and result in disputes needing head office arbitration
27.10
Transfer Pricing — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: Setting a Transfer Price With Spare Capacity

Given: Division A produces a component at variable cost ₹200/unit, fixed cost ₹50/unit (at full capacity). External market price for the component = ₹320/unit. Division B needs 1,000 units and could buy externally at ₹320, or from Division A internally.

Scenario 1 — Division A has NO spare capacity (selling all output externally at ₹320):
Transfer price should be the MARKET PRICE (₹320) — this represents Division A's true opportunity cost of diverting units from external sale to internal transfer. Any lower price makes Division A worse off than selling externally, causing goal incongruence.

Scenario 2 — Division A HAS spare capacity (not selling all output):
Minimum acceptable transfer price = Variable Cost only = ₹200 (no opportunity cost of lost external sales, since the capacity would otherwise sit idle). Any transfer price between ₹200 (A's minimum) and ₹320 (B's maximum, since B could otherwise buy externally at that price) makes BOTH divisions better off than their alternatives — commonly negotiated somewhere in this range, e.g. ₹250-260.
🔑 The General Transfer Pricing Rule
Minimum Transfer Price = Marginal (Variable) Cost + Opportunity Cost of Lost Contribution (₹0 if there's spare capacity, or the lost external contribution if operating at full capacity). This single rule underlies most ACCA APM transfer pricing questions.
27.11
Behavioural Aspects of Budgeting

Budgets aren't neutral, objective plans — they shape how people behave, and poorly designed budgeting processes can actively encourage dysfunctional behaviour.

🎭
Budgetary Slack
  • Managers deliberately understate revenue forecasts or overstate cost forecasts to make targets easier to hit
  • Rooted in the fear of being punished for missing an aggressive, honestly-set target
  • Reduced by tying rewards to improvement/stretch rather than punishing any miss, and using participative budgeting carefully
Gaming the System
🏃
Spend-It-Or-Lose-It Behaviour
  • Managers rush to spend remaining budget near year-end to avoid a reduced budget next year — even on low-value spending
  • A direct, predictable consequence of a rigid "use it or lose it" annual budgeting structure
Year-End Distortion
🎯
Target Setting Difficulty (Top-Down vs Bottom-Up)
  • Top-down targets risk being unrealistic/demotivating if set without operational input
  • Bottom-up targets risk embedding slack if managers control their own target-setting entirely
  • Most effective approach: participative budgeting with senior management review/challenge of submitted figures
Process Design

Non-Financial Performance Indicators

Financial measures alone are inherently backward-looking and can be manipulated short-term (e.g. cutting R&D or maintenance to boost this quarter's profit). Non-financial indicators capture leading, forward-looking performance signals financial measures miss.

CategoryExample MetricsWhy It Matters
CustomerNet Promoter Score, customer retention rate, complaint volumePredicts future revenue before it shows up in financial results
QualityDefect rate, first-pass yield, on-time delivery %Leading indicator of future rework costs, customer satisfaction, and brand reputation
EmployeeStaff turnover, engagement scores, training hoursHigh turnover predicts future recruitment cost, knowledge loss, and quality issues
Innovation% revenue from new products, R&D pipeline metricsSignals whether the business is investing in future competitiveness, not just harvesting current products
27.13
Performance Management in Not-for-Profit & Public Sector

Without a profit motive, performance measurement in NFP/public sector organisations relies heavily on the "3 Es" framework.

ECONOMY
Acquiring resources of the right quality at the lowest cost — "doing things cheaply"
EFFICIENCY
Maximising output from a given level of input resources — "doing things right"
EFFECTIVENESS
Achieving the actual intended outcomes/objectives — "doing the right things"
💡 Why This Matters — VFM (Value For Money)
Together, the 3 Es form the "Value For Money" framework central to public sector and NFP performance evaluation. It's entirely possible to be highly economical and efficient while being completely ineffective (e.g. cheaply and quickly delivering a service nobody actually needed) — good VFM analysis requires all three to be genuinely achieved together.
27.14
Big Data & Performance Management

Modern performance management increasingly incorporates real-time, high-volume data analytics — moving beyond periodic (monthly/quarterly) reporting toward continuous performance monitoring.

🔑 The Shift Big Data Enables
Traditional performance management relies on structured, periodic financial reports. Big data allows continuous, real-time dashboards drawing on operational data (website traffic, machine sensor data, social media sentiment) alongside financial data — enabling much faster identification of emerging performance issues, but also raising new challenges around data quality, integration complexity, and the risk of information overload without clear analytical focus.

Common Mistakes, Flashcards & Interview Q&A

Part A — Common Mistakes (Don't Repeat These)

❌ MISTAKE 1
Assuming Managers Always Accept Value-Creating Projects Under ROI
A project with a positive NPV/return above the cost of capital can still REDUCE a division's current ROI if that division's existing ROI is unusually high — a manager evaluated purely on ROI has a real incentive to reject it, even though it's genuinely good for the company.
❌ MISTAKE 2
Using Full Cost as the Transfer Price When Spare Capacity Exists
When a selling division has spare capacity, the minimum transfer price should be based on VARIABLE cost only (no opportunity cost) — using full absorption cost (including fixed cost) as the floor overstates the true minimum and can cause the buying division to reject internally-beneficial transfers.
❌ MISTAKE 3
Ignoring Opportunity Cost When Setting Transfer Prices at Full Capacity
When a selling division is operating at full capacity and could sell everything externally, the transfer price must reflect the LOST external contribution (opportunity cost) — setting it below market price without this consideration undercompensates the selling division and causes goal incongruence.
❌ MISTAKE 4
Treating Budgetary Slack as Simple Dishonesty Rather Than a System Design Issue
Blaming individual managers for budgetary slack misses that it's usually a predictable, rational RESPONSE to how the budgeting/reward system is designed — fixing it requires redesigning incentives (e.g. rewarding accuracy and improvement, not just target-hitting), not just moral appeals.
❌ MISTAKE 5
Confusing Efficiency With Effectiveness in VFM Analysis
A programme can be highly efficient (low cost per unit of output) while being completely ineffective (the output doesn't achieve the actual intended objective). Both must be assessed — high efficiency alone doesn't demonstrate good Value For Money.
❌ MISTAKE 6
Treating the Balanced Scorecard's Four Perspectives as Independent, Unconnected Measures
The real strategic power of the Balanced Scorecard comes from understanding the CAUSE-AND-EFFECT chain linking Learning & Growth → Internal Process → Customer → Financial. Treating each perspective as a standalone checklist of metrics, without understanding how they drive each other, misses the entire strategic logic behind the framework.
❌ MISTAKE 7
Applying a Manufacturing-Oriented Framework Directly to a Service Business
Service businesses have fundamentally different performance characteristics (intangibility, simultaneity of production and consumption, harder-to-standardise quality) — the Building Block Model's service-specific dimensions (flexibility, quality of service) address this, and applying purely manufacturing-style output/efficiency metrics to a service context misses what actually matters to service customers.
❌ MISTAKE 8
Setting Reward Systems That Reward Outcomes Staff Cannot Control
Per the Building Block Model's "Rewards" component, a reward system must be CONTROLLABLE — rewarding a divisional manager for a company-wide share price movement they have little influence over, for example, demotivates rather than motivates, since it's a poorly designed link between effort and reward.

Part C — Interview Questions & Model Answers

QuestionWhat They Are TestingModel Answer
"Why might a divisional manager reject a good investment opportunity, and how would you fix it?"Understanding of goal congruence and measurement system designIf evaluated on ROI, a manager might reject a project earning above the company's cost of capital but below their division's current average ROI, since it would dilute their reported percentage return. Switching the performance measure to Residual Income fixes this — RI rewards any project earning above the required rate as an absolute value increase, correctly aligning the manager's incentive with genuine value creation.
"How would you set a transfer price between two divisions of the same company?"Practical application of transfer pricing principlesI'd first check whether the selling division has spare capacity. If it does, the minimum acceptable price is the variable cost (no opportunity cost from lost external sales). If it's operating at full capacity, the transfer price should reflect the market price or lost external contribution, since diverting output internally has a genuine opportunity cost. The final negotiated price should sit between the selling division's minimum and the buying division's maximum (what they'd pay externally), and should also consider tax transfer pricing rules if it's a cross-border transaction.
"What is budgetary slack, and how would you reduce it in an organisation?"Awareness of the behavioural/human side of management accountingBudgetary slack is when managers deliberately build in a buffer — understating revenue or overstating costs — to make targets easier to achieve, usually driven by fear of punishment for missing an honest target. Reducing it involves redesigning incentives to reward accuracy and improvement over time rather than simple target-hitting, involving managers genuinely in setting targets (participative budgeting) while having senior management critically review and challenge submissions, and fostering a culture where honest forecasting isn't punished.
"How would you use the Balanced Scorecard to translate a company's strategy into day-to-day operations?"Strategic application ability beyond rote definitionI'd start from the Financial perspective's ultimate goal, then work backward asking what Customer outcomes drive that goal, what Internal Processes need to excel to deliver those customer outcomes, and what Learning & Growth investments (skills, systems, culture) are needed to enable those processes — building an explicit "strategy map" of cause-and-effect linkages, then setting specific measurable targets at each level so that day-to-day operational metrics genuinely connect back to the top-level strategic objective, rather than existing in isolation.
"Why might standard manufacturing performance metrics be inappropriate for a professional services firm?"Applied understanding of service-specific performance measurementManufacturing metrics like units-produced or defect rate assume a tangible, standardised, inventory-able output — but a service (like a consulting engagement or legal advice) is intangible, produced and consumed simultaneously, and quality is often subjectively experienced by each client differently. The Building Block Model's dimensions — particularly flexibility (adapting to each client's specific needs) and quality of service (reliability, responsiveness, courtesy) — capture what actually drives service business performance in a way that manufacturing-oriented output metrics simply cannot.
