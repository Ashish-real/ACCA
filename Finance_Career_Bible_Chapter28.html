28.2
Personal Income Tax Computation — Full Worked Example

Basic TX covers personal income tax, but a genuinely complete worked example — combining salary income, exemptions, deductions, and the choice between tax regimes — is worth walking through in full, since this is exactly the kind of computation ACCA TX/ATX candidates (and any finance professional advising individuals) need to execute confidently.

Part A — The Two-Regime Choice (Illustrative Structure)

Indian personal taxpayers can choose between two tax regimes each year: the Old Regime (higher slab rates, but allows numerous deductions and exemptions like HRA, Section 80C investments, and home loan interest) and the New Regime (lower slab rates, but most deductions/exemptions are NOT available). The illustrative slab structure below shows the general SHAPE of how this works — exact rates and thresholds are revised periodically in the Union Budget, so always verify current rates before real-world application.

Income Slab (Illustrative)Old Regime RateNew Regime Rate
Up to ₹2.5L / ₹3L (basic exemption)NilNil
Next slab (~₹2.5L-5L / ₹3L-7L)5%5%
Middle slabs20%10-15% (more slabs, gentler progression)
Top slab (above ~₹10L / ₹15L)30%30%
🔑 The Core Trade-Off
The Old Regime generally benefits taxpayers with SIGNIFICANT deductions available (large HRA claims, substantial Section 80C investments, home loan interest) — the deductions can outweigh the higher slab rates. The New Regime generally benefits taxpayers with FEW deductions to claim, since the lower rates apply directly without needing to itemise. The right choice is genuinely taxpayer-specific — exactly the kind of computation-then-compare exercise this section walks through.

Part B — Full Worked Example: Salaried Individual

📋 Scenario: Comparing Old vs New Regime for a Salaried Employee

Given (Annual figures): Basic Salary ₹9,00,000. HRA received ₹3,60,000. Actual rent paid ₹4,20,000 (metro city). Other taxable allowances ₹1,20,000. Section 80C investments (PF, ELSS, life insurance) ₹1,50,000. Home loan interest paid ₹1,80,000. Standard deduction (both regimes) = ₹50,000.

Step 1 — Calculate HRA Exemption (Old Regime only — New Regime doesn't allow this):
HRA exemption = LOWEST of the following three:
(a) Actual HRA received = ₹3,60,000
(b) Rent paid − 10% of Basic Salary = ₹4,20,000 − ₹90,000 = ₹3,30,000
(c) 50% of Basic Salary (metro city) = ₹4,50,000
HRA Exemption = ₹3,30,000 (the lowest of the three)
Taxable HRA = ₹3,60,000 − ₹3,30,000 = ₹30,000

Step 2 — Gross Salary Income:
Basic Salary: ₹9,00,000
Taxable HRA: ₹30,000
Other Allowances: ₹1,20,000
Gross Salary = ₹10,50,000

Step 3a — OLD REGIME Computation:
Gross Salary: ₹10,50,000
Less: Standard Deduction: (₹50,000)
Less: Section 80C: (₹1,50,000)
Less: Home Loan Interest (Section 24): (₹1,80,000) [capped at ₹2,00,000, fully allowed here]
Taxable Income (Old Regime) = ₹6,70,000

Step 3b — NEW REGIME Computation:
Gross Salary: ₹10,50,000 (HRA exemption NOT available — full ₹3,60,000 HRA is taxable, but our Gross Salary above already only used the OLD regime's HRA treatment — under New Regime, Gross Salary = ₹9,00,000 + ₹3,60,000 + ₹1,20,000 = ₹13,80,000)
Less: Standard Deduction: (₹50,000)
[80C and home loan interest NOT allowed under New Regime]
Taxable Income (New Regime) = ₹13,30,000

Step 4 — Apply illustrative slab rates and compare final tax liability (including 4% cess on both):
Using illustrative progressive rates on the respective taxable incomes above, the Old Regime's much lower taxable income base (₹6,70,000 vs ₹13,30,000) — a direct result of claiming HRA exemption, 80C, and home loan interest — would very likely produce a LOWER final tax liability despite the higher slab rates, because the deductions available here are substantial relative to income.

Conclusion: For THIS taxpayer profile (significant HRA claim + full 80C utilisation + home loan), the Old Regime is very likely more tax-efficient. A taxpayer with minimal deductions (no home loan, minimal 80C investment, living in own house with no HRA claim) would typically find the New Regime more favourable instead — confirming there's no universal "better" regime, only a computation-specific answer.
💡 The Professional Advisory Point
This exact comparison — computing tax liability under BOTH regimes and recommending the lower one — is one of the most common, genuinely valuable pieces of practical tax advice a finance professional gives individual clients or colleagues each year, since most salaried taxpayers don't run this comparison themselves and simply default to whichever regime their employer applies by default.
28.3
Corporate Group Taxation — Reliefs & Structuring

When a company operates through multiple subsidiaries, tax law often provides specific reliefs that allow the group to be treated more favourably than if each company were taxed in complete isolation.

🔄
Group Loss Relief
  • Losses in one group company can often be surrendered to offset profits in another group company (subject to ownership thresholds, typically 75%+ common ownership)
  • Prevents a group from paying tax on overall profit while one subsidiary sits with unrelieved losses
Loss Surrender
🏢
Intra-Group Asset Transfers
  • Many jurisdictions allow assets to be transferred between group companies without triggering an immediate capital gains tax charge
  • Recognises that no real economic disposal has occurred — the asset remains within the same overall economic group
No-Gain-No-Loss Transfers
28.4
Group Relief — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: Surrendering Losses Within a Group

Given: Parent Co has taxable profit of ₹8 Crore. Subsidiary Co (85% owned by Parent) has a tax loss of ₹3 Crore for the same period. Corporate tax rate = 25%.

Without group relief:
Parent Co tax = ₹8 Crore × 25% = ₹2 Crore
Subsidiary Co tax = ₹0 (loss, no tax due), loss carried forward for future use
Total group tax paid NOW = ₹2 Crore

With group relief (loss surrendered to Parent):
Parent Co taxable profit after relief = ₹8 Crore − ₹3 Crore = ₹5 Crore
Parent Co tax = ₹5 Crore × 25% = ₹1.25 Crore
Total group tax paid NOW = ₹1.25 Crore (a saving of ₹75 Lakh in current-year cash tax)

Interpretation: Group relief allows the group to access the tax benefit of the loss IMMEDIATELY, rather than waiting for Subsidiary Co to generate enough future profit to use the loss itself — a significant cash flow timing benefit for the group as a whole.
28.5
International Tax — Residence & Double Taxation

A company or individual with cross-border activity risks being taxed TWICE on the same income — once by the country where the income arises (source country) and again by the country of residence. International tax rules exist specifically to resolve this.

🏠
Tax Residence — Companies
  • Typically determined by place of incorporation AND/OR place of "central management and control" (where key strategic decisions are actually made)
  • A company can potentially be tax resident in more than one country under different countries' domestic rules — creating direct double tax risk
Corporate Residence
🌍
Source vs Residence Taxation
  • Source-based taxation: a country taxes income ARISING within its borders, regardless of who earns it
  • Residence-based taxation: a country taxes its residents on their WORLDWIDE income, regardless of where earned
  • Most countries use a combination, which is exactly why double taxation risk arises
The Root Cause
28.6
Double Taxation Avoidance Agreements (DTAA)

Countries sign bilateral treaties (DTAAs) that allocate taxing rights between the two countries and provide relief mechanisms so the same income isn't fully taxed twice.

Double Tax Relief Methods
Exemption Method
Income taxed in the source country is fully EXEMPTED from tax in the residence country
Credit Method (Most Common)
Residence country taxes worldwide income but gives a CREDIT for tax already paid in the source country, up to the residence country's own tax rate on that income
Deduction Method (Least Favourable)
Foreign tax paid is simply deducted as an EXPENSE before calculating residence-country tax — provides only partial relief
🔑 Foreign Tax Credit — The Key Limitation
Under the credit method, the credit given can NEVER exceed the residence country's own tax liability on that same income. If foreign tax paid is HIGHER than what the residence country would have charged, the excess is simply lost (not refunded) — this is why understanding DTAA rates and credit limitations matters for genuine international tax planning.
28.7
DTAA Foreign Tax Credit — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: Claiming Foreign Tax Credit Under the Credit Method

Given: An Indian company earns ₹50,00,000 of interest income from a US-based investment. Under the India-US DTAA, US withholding tax of 15% is deducted at source = ₹7,50,000. India's corporate tax rate = 25%.

Step 1 — India's tax liability on this income (before credit):
₹50,00,000 × 25% = ₹12,50,000

Step 2 — Foreign Tax Credit available:
Foreign tax actually paid = ₹7,50,000
Credit is capped at India's own tax on that income (₹12,50,000) — since ₹7,50,000 is LESS than the cap, the FULL ₹7,50,000 is creditable

Step 3 — Net Indian tax payable:
₹12,50,000 − ₹7,50,000 = ₹5,00,000

Total tax paid globally on this income: ₹7,50,000 (US) + ₹5,00,000 (India, net) = ₹12,50,000 — exactly equal to India's domestic tax rate applied once, confirming double taxation has been correctly eliminated.

Now consider a HIGHER foreign withholding rate scenario: If the US withholding tax had instead been 30% (₹15,00,000) due to no treaty benefit being available, the credit would still be CAPPED at ₹12,50,000 (India's own tax on that income) — the excess ₹2,50,000 (₹15,00,000 − ₹12,50,000) would simply be LOST, not refunded, illustrating exactly the credit-cap limitation covered in 28.5.
28.8
Permanent Establishment — The International Tax Trigger

A foreign company doesn't automatically become taxable in another country just by having some connection to it — international tax law uses the concept of a Permanent Establishment (PE) as the trigger point at which a country gains the right to tax a foreign enterprise's business profits.

🏢
Fixed Place of Business PE
  • A physical location through which business is wholly or partly carried on — branch office, factory, workshop, or even a construction site lasting beyond a treaty-specified duration (often 6-12 months)
  • Most DTAAs specifically EXCLUDE purely preparatory or auxiliary activities (e.g. a warehouse used solely for storage) from creating a PE
Physical Presence
🤝
Dependent Agent PE
  • Arises when a person habitually concludes contracts (or plays the principal role leading to contract conclusion) on behalf of a foreign enterprise in that country
  • An INDEPENDENT agent acting in the ordinary course of their own business generally does NOT create a PE for the foreign principal
Agency Presence
🔑 Why PE Matters Enormously in Practice
If a foreign company is found to have a PE in a country, that country gains the right to tax the PROFITS ATTRIBUTABLE to that PE — even if the company never intended to establish a formal local presence. This has become especially significant with digital businesses, where traditional PE concepts (requiring physical presence) struggled to capture value created by companies with significant digital/remote engagement but minimal physical footprint — a major driver behind the global push for digital services taxes and the OECD's BEPS (Base Erosion and Profit Shifting) reforms.
28.9
Controlled Foreign Company (CFC) Rules

Without anti-avoidance rules, a company could shift profits into a subsidiary based in a low-tax or zero-tax jurisdiction and simply defer (or avoid) home-country tax indefinitely by not repatriating those profits. CFC rules prevent this by allowing the parent's home country to tax certain foreign subsidiary profits AS IF they had already been distributed to the parent, even if they haven't been.

TRIGGER
CFC rules typically apply when a domestic parent controls a foreign subsidiary (often >50% ownership/control) located in a low-tax jurisdiction
TARGET
Usually targets PASSIVE income specifically (interest, royalties, dividends) rather than genuine active trading profits — the concern is shifting easily-movable income to low-tax entities, not penalising genuine overseas business operations
EFFECT
The CFC's undistributed passive income is attributed to the parent and taxed in the parent's home jurisdiction NOW, removing the deferral benefit that motivated the low-tax structure in the first place
28.10
Transfer Pricing — Tax Perspective

Chapter 27 covered transfer pricing from a management accounting / performance measurement angle. From a TAX perspective, transfer pricing takes on much higher stakes — multinationals could otherwise shift profit to low-tax jurisdictions by manipulating prices on cross-border intra-group transactions, so tax authorities worldwide mandate the Arm's Length Principle.

🔑 The Arm's Length Principle
Cross-border transactions between related/group entities must be priced as if the parties were UNRELATED, independent third parties negotiating at market terms. Tax authorities can adjust a company's taxable profit if they determine the actual transfer price used was not at arm's length — typically requiring detailed transfer pricing documentation and benchmarking studies to defend the pricing used.
Arm's Length MethodApproach
Comparable Uncontrolled Price (CUP)Compare the price to an identical/similar transaction between unrelated parties
Resale Price MethodWork backward from the resale price to unrelated customers, less an appropriate margin
Cost Plus MethodCost of the supplying entity plus an appropriate arm's length markup
Transactional Net Margin Method (TNMM)Compares net profit margins earned on the controlled transaction to comparable independent companies
Profit Split MethodSplits combined profit between related entities based on their relative contribution/value added
28.11
Transfer Pricing Tax — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: Tax Authority Transfer Pricing Adjustment

Facts: An Indian subsidiary sells goods to its parent company in a low-tax jurisdiction at ₹80/unit. Comparable arm's length price (based on similar transactions between unrelated companies, per a TP benchmarking study) = ₹120/unit. Volume = 1,00,000 units. Indian corporate tax rate = 25%.

Tax Authority's Adjustment:
Price shortfall per unit = ₹120 − ₹80 = ₹40
Total understated revenue = 1,00,000 units × ₹40 = ₹40,00,000
Additional taxable profit assessed = ₹40,00,000
Additional tax due = ₹40,00,000 × 25% = ₹10,00,000

Consequence: Beyond the additional tax itself, transfer pricing adjustments typically also trigger penalties (often a percentage of the adjustment) and require the company to maintain more robust, defensible TP documentation going forward — which is exactly why multinationals invest heavily in transfer pricing studies and documentation BEFORE tax authorities challenge their pricing, not after.
28.12
General Anti-Avoidance Rules (GAAR)

Beyond specific anti-avoidance rules targeting particular known tax-avoidance techniques, many tax systems (including India's) have a broader General Anti-Avoidance Rule that allows tax authorities to disregard or recharacterise an "impermissible avoidance arrangement" — even if it technically complies with the letter of the law.

⚖️
What GAAR Targets
  • Arrangements whose MAIN purpose (or one of the main purposes) is to obtain a tax benefit
  • Arrangements lacking genuine commercial substance beyond the tax advantage
  • Arrangements creating rights/obligations not typically found between parties dealing at arm's length
Substance Over Form
🛡️
Why It Matters for Tax Planning
  • GAAR means TECHNICAL legal compliance alone is no longer sufficient protection against challenge
  • Genuine commercial rationale — beyond just tax savings — must exist and be documented for any significant restructuring
  • Shifts tax planning from purely "can we do this legally" to "would this survive substance-over-form scrutiny"
Planning Implication
28.13
Tax-Efficient Structuring of Transactions

Genuine tax planning (not avoidance schemes) involves structuring GENUINE commercial transactions in the most tax-efficient legally available way.

TIMING
Timing of Transactions — legitimately timing a disposal, investment, or income recognition to fall in a more favourable tax year or period, within normal commercial flexibility
ENTITY CHOICE
Choice of Business Vehicle — selecting between a subsidiary, branch, LLP, or joint venture structure based partly on the differing tax treatment each carries, alongside genuine commercial/legal considerations
FINANCING MIX
Debt vs Equity Financing — since interest is typically tax-deductible while dividends are not, financing structure genuinely affects the group's overall tax efficiency (subject to thin capitalisation and interest limitation rules that cap this benefit)
RELIEF UTILISATION
Maximising Available Reliefs — ensuring all legitimately available reliefs, allowances, and exemptions are correctly claimed and optimally sequenced (e.g. using capital losses against the most appropriate gains)
28.14
Capital Gains Tax Planning
📅
Holding Period Planning
  • Many tax systems (including India) apply lower rates to LONG-TERM capital gains vs short-term — timing a disposal to cross the long-term threshold can be a legitimate, significant tax saving
  • Requires balancing tax timing against genuine commercial/investment considerations — never purely tax-driven
Timing
🔄
Loss Harvesting & Matching
  • Realising capital losses to offset against capital gains in the same period, reducing net taxable gains
  • Must be a genuine economic transaction, not an artificial round-trip designed purely to manufacture a tax loss (which GAAR/specific anti-avoidance rules would likely challenge)
Loss Utilisation
28.15
Capital Gains Planning — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: The Tax Value of Waiting for Long-Term Treatment

Given: An investor is considering selling listed equity shares currently valued at ₹50,00,000, held for 11 months, with a cost base of ₹30,00,000 (unrealised gain = ₹20,00,000). Short-term capital gains (held <12 months) on listed equity are taxed at 20%; long-term capital gains (held ≥12 months) are taxed at 12.5% (illustrative rates for this example, reflecting the general short-term vs long-term structure).

Scenario A — Sell now (short-term):
Tax = ₹20,00,000 × 20% = ₹4,00,000
Net proceeds after tax = ₹50,00,000 − ₹4,00,000 = ₹46,00,000

Scenario B — Wait 1 more month, then sell (long-term):
Tax = ₹20,00,000 × 12.5% = ₹2,50,000
Net proceeds after tax = ₹50,00,000 − ₹2,50,000 = ₹47,50,000 (assuming share price unchanged)

Value of waiting: ₹47,50,000 − ₹46,00,000 = ₹1,50,000 additional after-tax value, purely from crossing the long-term holding threshold — a genuine, legitimate tax planning consideration (assuming the investor has no urgent liquidity need and the share price is expected to remain broadly stable over that single month).

Important caveat: This analysis assumes the share price doesn't move adversely during the waiting period — the tax saving must always be weighed against the MARKET RISK of continuing to hold the investment. Purely tax-driven timing decisions that ignore genuine investment risk are not sound financial planning, even where the tax arithmetic looks attractive in isolation.
28.16
Ethics in Tax Planning — Avoidance vs Evasion

This is the single most important conceptual distinction in advanced tax practice — and one every finance professional must understand precisely.

ConceptDefinitionLegal Status
Tax AvoidanceLegally arranging one's affairs to minimise tax, using legitimate reliefs, structures, and timing within the law's actual intentLegal, but AGGRESSIVE avoidance risks GAAR challenge if lacking genuine commercial substance
Tax EvasionIllegally concealing income, falsifying records, or deliberately misreporting to reduce tax owedILLEGAL — a criminal offence in virtually every jurisdiction
Tax Mitigation / PlanningUsing reliefs and structures exactly as intended by the legislature (e.g. claiming a genuinely available deduction)Fully legal and uncontroversial
🔑 The Professional Ethics Line
A professional accountant's duty of Professional Competence and Due Care, alongside Integrity (Chapter 16), means advising on aggressive avoidance schemes that lack genuine commercial substance carries real professional risk — even if technically "legal" at the time, GAAR and evolving anti-avoidance rules mean such schemes are increasingly challenged retrospectively, and professional bodies increasingly expect members to decline involvement in artificial, contrived arrangements.

🔑 Key India-Specific Point for ACCA Students
Unlike the UK-based group relief concept covered in ATX's core syllabus, India taxes each company in a group SEPARATELY — there's no formal mechanism to surrender losses between Indian group companies for tax purposes (unlike the worked example in 28.3, which reflects UK-style group relief). This is an important distinction to note when applying ATX concepts to Indian corporate structures in practice.

Common Mistakes, Flashcards & Interview Q&A

Part A — Common Mistakes (Don't Repeat These)

❌ MISTAKE 1
Assuming Group Relief Applies Universally
Group loss relief mechanisms vary significantly by jurisdiction — India, for example, does not have a UK-style formal group relief regime; each Indian company is generally taxed separately. Assuming ATX's UK-based group relief concepts apply identically everywhere is a critical error in cross-border tax analysis.
❌ MISTAKE 2
Believing the Foreign Tax Credit Is Always Fully Refundable
Under the credit method, the credit is CAPPED at the residence country's own tax on that income — if foreign tax paid exceeds this cap, the excess is simply lost, not refunded or carried forward (in most systems). This limitation is frequently tested and frequently missed.
❌ MISTAKE 3
Treating Technical Legal Compliance as Sufficient Protection
Under GAAR, an arrangement can be technically legal under specific rules yet still be challenged and unwound if it lacks genuine commercial substance and its main purpose is tax avoidance. "It's technically legal" is no longer a complete answer in jurisdictions with GAAR.
❌ MISTAKE 4
Confusing Tax Avoidance With Tax Evasion
These are fundamentally different — avoidance is legal (though aggressive avoidance can be challenged under GAAR), evasion is a criminal offence involving concealment or falsification. Using these terms interchangeably, especially in an exam or professional context, is a serious conceptual error.
❌ MISTAKE 5
Ignoring Transfer Pricing Documentation Requirements
Even if a transfer price is genuinely arm's length, failing to maintain proper contemporaneous documentation and benchmarking studies exposes a company to penalty risk during a tax audit — the burden of proof is typically on the taxpayer to demonstrate arm's length pricing, not on the tax authority to disprove it.
❌ MISTAKE 6
Assuming a Company Needs a Formal Branch to Have a Permanent Establishment
A PE can arise through a dependent agent habitually concluding contracts on a company's behalf, even without any formal branch or office — assuming PE risk only exists with a physical branch presence misses this equally important "agency PE" trigger, especially relevant for companies using local sales agents or representatives.
❌ MISTAKE 7
Assuming CFC Rules Target All Foreign Subsidiary Income
CFC rules typically target PASSIVE income (interest, royalties, dividends) parked in low-tax jurisdictions — genuine active trading profits from a real overseas business operation are generally NOT the target, though the exact scope varies by jurisdiction. Assuming CFC rules automatically tax all foreign subsidiary profits overstates their reach.
❌ MISTAKE 8
Making Purely Tax-Driven Timing Decisions Without Weighing Market Risk
As the capital gains worked example (28.14) notes, waiting to cross a long-term holding threshold has genuine tax value — but this must always be weighed against the market risk of continuing to hold the investment during the waiting period. A tax saving that's smaller than a plausible adverse price movement isn't a sound reason to delay a sale.

Part C — Interview Questions & Model Answers

QuestionWhat They Are TestingModel Answer
"What's the difference between tax avoidance and tax evasion, and why does the distinction matter professionally?"Core ethical and technical understanding essential for any tax-adjacent roleTax avoidance is legally structuring one's affairs to minimise tax using legitimate reliefs and structures — though aggressive avoidance lacking genuine commercial substance can still be challenged under GAAR. Tax evasion is illegally concealing income or falsifying records — a criminal offence. The distinction matters professionally because a finance professional's ethical duties (integrity, professional competence) mean advising on or facilitating evasion is never acceptable, while legitimate tax planning is a genuine value-adding professional service.
"How would a multinational company approach setting a transfer price between two of its subsidiaries in different countries?"Practical, applied transfer pricing knowledgeThe starting principle is the arm's length standard — pricing the transaction as if the two entities were unrelated. Depending on the nature of the transaction and available data, they'd choose an appropriate method (CUP if comparable market transactions exist, cost-plus or resale price for simpler distribution arrangements, or TNMM/profit split for more complex value chains), then prepare contemporaneous documentation to defend that pricing in case of a tax authority audit.
"Why might a company's transfer pricing be challenged even if it believes the pricing is fair?"Awareness of the practical audit/compliance risk dimensionTax authorities in different countries have competing interests — the country where profit is shifted OUT of wants to see higher local pricing (more local tax base), while the receiving country's authority might see it differently. Even genuinely reasonable pricing can be challenged if the company lacks robust contemporaneous documentation and benchmarking evidence to defend it, since the burden of proof for justifying transfer prices typically sits with the taxpayer.
"What is a Permanent Establishment, and why does it matter for a company expanding internationally?"Practical international tax risk awareness relevant to business expansionA Permanent Establishment is the threshold at which a foreign country gains the right to tax a company's business profits — triggered either by a fixed physical place of business or by a dependent agent habitually concluding contracts on the company's behalf. It matters hugely for expansion planning because unintentionally creating a PE (e.g. through a local sales team with contract-signing authority) can trigger unexpected local tax filing obligations and liabilities the company hadn't planned for or budgeted.
"How would you explain CFC rules to a client planning to set up a subsidiary in a low-tax jurisdiction?"Practical tax advisory communication skillI'd explain that CFC rules exist specifically to prevent using a low-tax subsidiary purely to defer or avoid home-country tax on passive income like interest, royalties, or dividends — if the rules apply, that undistributed passive income gets taxed in the parent's home country now, as if it had already been repatriated, regardless of whether it actually has been. I'd clarify that genuine active trading operations are typically NOT the target, so a low-tax jurisdiction can still make sense for real business activity, but purely passive income-parking structures are unlikely to achieve the intended deferral benefit.
