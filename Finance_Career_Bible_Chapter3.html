In Chapter 2, you learned how every business transaction is recorded through double-entry bookkeeping. At the end of every accounting period, all those entries flow into four powerful documents — the Financial Statements. These are the scorecards of business. They tell the story of what a company earned, what it owns, what it owes, and how much cash it actually generated.

"Anyone can tell you their business is doing well. Financial statements prove it — or expose the lie."

Every Big 4 audit, every MNC CFO role, every investment decision in the world starts with reading these four documents. Master them and you can evaluate any company on earth.

3.1
What Are Financial Statements?
The Scorecards of Business

Financial Statements are formal records of a business's financial activities prepared at the end of an accounting period. Under IFRS (which ACCA follows), every company must prepare a complete set of financial statements.

📊
Income Statement (P&L)
  • Shows revenues earned and expenses incurred
  • Tells you: did the business make a profit or loss?
  • Covers a period of time (e.g. year ended 31 March)
  • Also called: Profit & Loss Account, P&L
Performance
⚖️
Balance Sheet (SFP)
  • Shows assets, liabilities, and equity
  • Tells you: what does the business own and owe?
  • A snapshot at one point in time
  • IFRS name: Statement of Financial Position
Position
💵
Cash Flow Statement
  • Shows actual cash coming in and going out
  • Tells you: is the business generating real cash?
  • 3 sections: Operating, Investing, Financing
  • Profit ≠ Cash — this statement shows why
Cash
📈
Statement of Changes in Equity
  • Shows how equity changed during the period
  • Tracks: share capital, retained earnings, dividends
  • Connects the P&L to the Balance Sheet
  • Often called SOCE or SOCOE
Equity Movement
🎯 ACCA Exam Tip
Under IAS 1 (Presentation of Financial Statements) — the IFRS standard that governs this — every entity must present all four statements plus notes. IAS 1 is tested in ACCA FA and FR papers. Know this standard by name.
3.2
The Income Statement (P&L)
Measuring Profit

The Income Statement answers the most important question in business: did we make money? It shows all revenues earned and all expenses incurred during the accounting period, resulting in either a profit or a loss.

Revenue
− Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)
= GROSS PROFIT
− Operating Expenses
= OPERATING PROFIT (EBIT)
− Finance Costs (Interest)
+ Other Income
= PROFIT BEFORE TAX (PBT)
− Income Tax Expense
= PROFIT FOR THE YEAR (PAT)
The structure of every Income Statement — from Revenue to Net Profit

Key Terms You Must Know

📦
Gross Profit & Gross Margin
  • Gross Profit = Revenue − COGS
  • Gross Margin % = Gross Profit ÷ Revenue × 100
  • Measures efficiency of core operations
  • Higher = better pricing or lower production cost
Profitability Signal
⚙️
EBIT & EBITDA
  • EBIT = Earnings Before Interest & Tax
  • EBITDA = EBIT + Depreciation + Amortisation
  • Used to compare companies across different tax regimes
  • Common in valuation and investment banking
Valuation Metric
💰
PAT & Net Margin
  • PAT = Profit After Tax = bottom line
  • Net Margin % = PAT ÷ Revenue × 100
  • What actually remains for shareholders
  • Goes to retained earnings or paid as dividend
Bottom Line
3.3
The Balance Sheet (Statement of Financial Position)
Measuring Worth

The Balance Sheet is a snapshot of a company's financial position at a single point in time. It shows everything the company owns (assets), everything it owes (liabilities), and what belongs to the owners (equity). It always balances — because of the accounting equation.

ASSETS = LIABILITIES + EQUITY

Non-Current Assets + Current Assets = Non-Current Liabilities + Current Liabilities + Equity
The Balance Sheet is just the Accounting Equation in full detail

3.4
The Cash Flow Statement
Measuring Real Cash

A company can show a profit on the P&L and still run out of cash. The Cash Flow Statement shows where cash actually came from and where it went. It is divided into three sections — Operating, Investing, and Financing activities.

⚙️
Operating Activities
  • Cash from core business operations
  • Start with Profit Before Tax
  • Adjust for non-cash items (depreciation)
  • Adjust for working capital changes
  • Most important section — shows business health
Day-to-Day Business
🏗️
Investing Activities
  • Cash spent on or received from long-term assets
  • Purchase of equipment, property, vehicles
  • Sale of investments or subsidiary companies
  • Usually negative (outflow) in growing businesses
Long-term Investment
🏦
Financing Activities
  • Cash from or repaid to investors and lenders
  • Issue of shares, repayment of loans
  • Payment of dividends to shareholders
  • Shows how business funds itself
Funding the Business

3.6
How the Four Statements Connect
The Big Picture

The four financial statements are not separate documents — they are deeply interconnected. Understanding how they link is one of the most important skills in financial accounting.

Step 1
📊 Income Statement → produces Net Profit
Revenue − Expenses = Profit for the year (₹2,29,500 in our example)
↓
Step 2
📈 Profit → flows into Statement of Changes in Equity
Net profit increases retained earnings. Dividends reduce it. Closing retained earnings = ₹1,29,500
↓
Step 3
⚖️ Closing Equity → flows into the Balance Sheet
Retained earnings of ₹1,29,500 appears in the equity section of the Balance Sheet
↓
Step 4
💵 Cash Flow Statement → explains the Cash balance on Balance Sheet
The closing cash of ₹2,40,000 on the Cash Flow Statement = Cash & Cash Equivalents on the Balance Sheet
🔗 The Golden Links
P&L → SOCE: Net profit adds to retained earnings
SOCE → Balance Sheet: Closing equity goes into SFP
Cash Flow → Balance Sheet: Closing cash = cash on SFP
All 4 → Notes: Every number is explained in the notes to accounts
3.7
Profit vs Cash — The Critical Difference
Most Misunderstood Concept

This is one of the most important — and most misunderstood — concepts in finance. A profitable company can run out of cash. A loss-making company can have lots of cash. Understanding why this happens will make you a better accountant, analyst, and advisor.

ReasonEffect on ProfitEffect on Cash
DepreciationReduces profit (expense)No cash impact — non-cash charge
Credit Sales (Debtors)Increases revenue/profitNo cash yet — client hasn't paid
Buying EquipmentNo immediate P&L impactLarge cash outflow immediately
Accrued ExpensesReduces profit nowCash paid later (next period)
Prepaid ExpensesNo P&L impact yetCash already paid out
Receiving Advance (Deferred Income)No revenue yetCash received immediately

🔥 Real World Example — Why Companies Go Bankrupt Despite Being "Profitable"

Imagine Ashish's consulting firm has ₹50L in revenue on the P&L — looks profitable. But ₹40L is owed by clients who haven't paid yet (debtors). Meanwhile, Ashish must pay salaries, rent, and suppliers every month. The firm shows a profit but has no cash to pay bills. This is called a liquidity crisis — and it kills profitable businesses every day.

This is exactly why the Cash Flow Statement exists — to show the real cash picture, not just the accounting profit.

3.8
Notes to Financial Statements

The Notes are the fifth and often most important part of a complete set of financial statements. They explain and expand on every number in the four main statements. Under IAS 1, notes are mandatory.

📋
What Notes Include
  • Accounting policies used (depreciation method, revenue recognition)
  • Detailed breakdown of each line item
  • Contingent liabilities and commitments
  • Related party transactions
  • Events after the reporting date
IAS 1 Required
🔍
Why Auditors Love Notes
  • Notes reveal what numbers hide
  • Accounting policy changes can manipulate profit
  • Off-balance sheet items disclosed in notes
  • Big 4 audit teams read notes first
  • Fraud often hidden in note disclosures
Audit Focus
💼
Why MNC Finance Teams Care
  • Notes contain segment information (which division is profitable)
  • Capital commitments show future spending plans
  • Debt covenants and borrowing facilities disclosed
  • Management compensation in notes
Management Info

3.12
Events After the Reporting Period (IAS 10)

Part A — The Core Question IAS 10 Answers

Financial statements aren't finalised the instant the year ends — there's always a gap between the reporting date (e.g. 31 March) and the date the statements are actually authorised for issue (e.g. board approval in May). IAS 10 tells you how to treat things that happen or become known during that gap.

🔧
Adjusting Events
  • Provide EVIDENCE of conditions that ALREADY EXISTED at the reporting date
  • Financial statements MUST be adjusted to reflect this new evidence
  • Example: a customer goes bankrupt in April, confirming a receivable was already uncollectible at 31 March
  • Example: a court case pending at year-end is settled after year-end, confirming the amount of a provision
Update the Numbers
📢
Non-Adjusting Events
  • Indicate conditions that AROSE AFTER the reporting date — didn't exist at year-end
  • Financial statements are NOT adjusted — but must be DISCLOSED in the notes if material
  • Example: a fire destroys a warehouse in April (didn't affect the 31 March position)
  • Example: a major acquisition or share issue announced after year-end
Disclose, Don't Adjust

Part B — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: Classifying 3 Post-Year-End Events

Company's year-end: 31 March. Statements authorised for issue: 20 May.

Event 1: On 10 April, a major customer who owed ₹15,00,000 at 31 March is declared insolvent, with the liquidator confirming only ₹2,00,000 will ever be recovered.
Classification: ADJUSTING. The customer's financial distress existed at year-end (they didn't become insolvent overnight) — this is new evidence about a condition that already existed. Action: Write down the receivable to ₹2,00,000, recognising a ₹13,00,000 bad debt expense in the year ended 31 March.

Event 2: On 5 May, the company's main factory is severely damaged by an unprecedented flood.
Classification: NON-ADJUSTING. The flood is a new event that occurred entirely after year-end — it says nothing about the condition of the factory AT 31 March. Action: Do NOT adjust the financial statements — but disclose the nature and estimated financial impact of the flood in the notes, since it's clearly material.

Event 3: On 15 May, the board announces a decision to permanently close an underperforming division — a decision made and communicated entirely after year-end, with no prior indication before 31 March.
Classification: NON-ADJUSTING. Since the decision was made after year-end, the division's assets/liabilities were correctly stated as a going concern at 31 March. Action: Disclose the closure decision and its expected impact in the notes — but do not restate the division's assets in the 31 March statements.
🔑 The One-Question Test
Ask: "Did this condition already exist at the reporting date, and I'm just learning about it now — or did something genuinely NEW happen after the reporting date?" Existed-but-just-discovered = Adjusting. Genuinely new = Non-Adjusting. This single question resolves the vast majority of IAS 10 exam scenarios.

Part C — The Going Concern Exception

There's one critical exception to the adjusting/non-adjusting split: if an event after the reporting period indicates the going concern basis is no longer appropriate (e.g. the company's only major customer collapses post year-end, making the company itself unable to continue trading), the financial statements must be adjusted FUNDAMENTALLY — potentially requiring a switch from the going concern basis to a break-up basis entirely, regardless of whether the triggering event itself would normally be classified as adjusting or non-adjusting.

3.13
Common Mistakes, Flashcards & Interview Q&A

Part A — Most Common Mistakes (Don't Repeat These)

❌ MISTAKE 1
Confusing Profit with Cash Balance
Students see a healthy Net Profit and assume the company has plenty of cash. A business can be hugely profitable and still run out of cash (rising receivables, heavy capex, inventory build-up). Always check the Cash Flow Statement separately — never infer cash position from the P&L.
❌ MISTAKE 2
Forgetting That Retained Earnings Links P&L to Balance Sheet
Net Profit for the year does not just vanish — it flows into Retained Earnings in the Balance Sheet (via SOCE), after deducting any dividends paid. Forgetting this link means your Balance Sheet won't balance, and you'll miss marks on questions testing the four-statement connection.
❌ MISTAKE 3
Mixing Up Current and Non-Current Classification
An item is current if it will be realised/settled within 12 months (or the operating cycle). A common error is treating a long-term loan's current portion as fully non-current, or classifying a fixed deposit maturing in 3 months as non-current. Always check the actual timeline, not just the account name.
❌ MISTAKE 4
Preparing the Cash Flow Statement Starting From the Wrong Number
The indirect method starts from Net Profit BEFORE tax and interest (or Profit Before Tax), not from Net Profit after tax. Starting from the wrong base number and then adjusting produces a Cash Flow Statement that never reconciles to the actual closing cash balance.
❌ MISTAKE 5
Treating Depreciation as a Cash Outflow
Depreciation reduces profit but involves no cash movement — it must be ADDED BACK in the Cash Flow Statement (indirect method), not subtracted again. Double-counting depreciation as a cash outflow is one of the most frequent errors in ACCA FA/FR cash flow questions.
❌ MISTAKE 6
Ignoring Working Capital Movements
A rise in receivables or inventory is a cash OUTFLOW (cash tied up); a rise in payables is a cash INFLOW (cash retained). Students often get the direction backwards — remember: an increase in an asset uses cash, an increase in a liability provides cash.
❌ MISTAKE 7
Not Reading the Notes to Financial Statements
The notes contain critical information — accounting policies, contingent liabilities, related party transactions, segment results — that changes how the numbers should be interpreted. Skipping the notes and only looking at the primary statements gives an incomplete (sometimes misleading) picture.
❌ MISTAKE 8
Forgetting SOCE Isn't Just About Profit
The Statement of Changes in Equity captures more than retained earnings — share issues, dividends, other comprehensive income (like revaluation surplus), and prior period adjustments all flow through SOCE. Treating it as "just profit minus dividends" misses these other equity movements.
❌ MISTAKE 9
Adjusting Financial Statements for Non-Adjusting Events
A material non-adjusting event (like a fire, flood, or major post-year-end acquisition) should be DISCLOSED in the notes — not used to restate the primary financial statements. Only adjusting events (evidence of conditions that already existed at year-end) trigger an actual change to the reported figures.

Part C — Interview Questions & Model Answers

QuestionWhat They Are TestingModel Answer
"Walk me through the three financial statements and how they connect."Foundational understanding — the #1 most-asked finance interview questionThe Income Statement shows revenue minus expenses over a period, ending in Net Profit. That Net Profit flows into Retained Earnings on the Balance Sheet (via SOCE), which shows what the company owns (assets) and owes (liabilities + equity) at a point in time. The Cash Flow Statement starts from profit and adjusts for non-cash items and working capital changes to show actual cash movement, reconciling to the cash balance on the Balance Sheet.
"Why can a company be profitable but still go bankrupt?"Do you understand the profit-cash distinction?Profit is an accounting measure that includes non-cash items (like depreciation) and recognises revenue when earned, not when cash is received. A company can show strong profit while its cash is tied up in receivables, inventory, or heavy capital expenditure — and if it cannot pay its bills as they fall due, it becomes insolvent regardless of reported profit.
"If depreciation increases, what happens to each of the three statements?"Do you understand how one entry ripples across statements?Income Statement: profit decreases (depreciation is an expense). Balance Sheet: asset's carrying value decreases, and retained earnings (equity) decreases by the same amount as the profit hit. Cash Flow Statement: no direct cash impact — but since profit fell for a non-cash reason, depreciation is added back in the indirect method, so operating cash flow itself doesn't change.
"What is working capital and why does it matter for cash flow?"Practical grasp of short-term liquidity managementWorking capital = Current Assets − Current Liabilities. It represents cash tied up in day-to-day operations (inventory, receivables) net of short-term obligations (payables). A growing business often needs more working capital, which consumes cash even while showing rising profits — this is a critical link between the Income Statement and Cash Flow Statement.
"What would you look at first when analysing a company's financial statements?"Analytical approach and prioritisationI'd start with the Cash Flow Statement to understand the quality of earnings — is operating cash flow tracking profit, or diverging? Then the Balance Sheet for financial position and leverage. Then the Income Statement for revenue trends and margins. Finally the notes, for accounting policies, contingencies, and anything unusual that changes the picture.
