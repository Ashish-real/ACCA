30.1
Why These Topics Deserve Their Own Chapter

Both share-based payments and complex group restructuring share a common thread: they require careful, methodical application of a MULTI-STEP process — get one step wrong, and everything downstream is wrong too. This makes them simultaneously high-value (they're heavily tested) and high-risk (they're the source of the most common calculation errors) for SBR candidates.

30.2
Share-Based Payments — IFRS 2 Fundamentals

When a company compensates employees (or others) with shares or share options instead of (or alongside) cash, IFRS 2 requires this to be recognised as an EXPENSE — reflecting the genuine economic cost of the benefit granted, even though no cash changes hands.

📈
Equity-Settled
  • Company settles the obligation by issuing its OWN shares/options
  • Measured at FAIR VALUE AT GRANT DATE — this value is NOT remeasured for subsequent share price changes
  • Expense spread over the VESTING PERIOD (the period employees must work to earn the award)
Fixed at Grant Date
💵
Cash-Settled
  • Company settles the obligation with CASH, based on the value of shares (e.g. Share Appreciation Rights)
  • Measured at fair value, but RE-MEASURED at each reporting date until settlement
  • Creates a LIABILITY (not equity), since cash will actually be paid out
Remeasured Each Period
30.3
Equity-Settled Share-Based Payments — Worked Example
Equity-Settled SBP Formula
Cumulative Expense to Date
(Number of Options Expected to Vest × Fair Value at Grant Date) × (Years Elapsed ÷ Total Vesting Period)
Expense for the Current Year
Cumulative Expense to Date (this year) − Cumulative Expense Recognised (prior years)

📋 Scenario: 3-Year Vesting Employee Share Option Scheme

Given: On 1 Jan Year 1, a company grants 1,000 employees 100 options each (100,000 options total), vesting after 3 years of continued employment. Fair value at grant date = ₹15/option. At the end of Year 1, the company estimates 10% of employees will leave before vesting and revises this to 15% at the end of Year 2. By the actual vesting date (end of Year 3), 880 employees remain (12% left).

Year 1 — Cumulative expense:
(1,000 × 90%) × 100 options × ₹15 × (1/3) = 90,000 options × ₹15 × 1/3 = ₹4,50,000
Year 1 P&L expense = ₹4,50,000 (first year, nothing to deduct)

Year 2 — Cumulative expense:
(1,000 × 85%) × 100 options × ₹15 × (2/3) = 85,000 options × ₹15 × 2/3 = ₹8,50,000
Year 2 P&L expense = ₹8,50,000 − ₹4,50,000 = ₹4,00,000

Year 3 — Cumulative expense (using ACTUAL vesting, not estimate):
(880 employees × 100 options) × ₹15 × (3/3) = 88,000 options × ₹15 = ₹13,20,000
Year 3 P&L expense = ₹13,20,000 − ₹8,50,000 = ₹4,70,000

Key Point: Each year's expense is based on the BEST CURRENT ESTIMATE of the number of options expected to vest — Year 3 uses the ACTUAL final number (880), since by vesting date there's no more estimation uncertainty. The fair value per option (₹15) never changes throughout — only the estimated/actual QUANTITY of vesting options changes.
30.4
Cash-Settled Share-Based Payments

The mechanics parallel equity-settled schemes (spreading over vesting period, adjusting for expected forfeitures) — but with one critical difference: the fair value used is REMEASURED at every reporting date, not fixed at grant date.

🔑 Why Cash-Settled Schemes Create More Volatility
Since the liability is remeasured to current fair value every period (reflecting the current share price), P&L expense for cash-settled schemes can swing significantly period to period as the share price moves — unlike equity-settled schemes, where the expense is smooth and predictable once granted, since the grant-date fair value never changes.
30.5
Cash-Settled SBP — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: Share Appreciation Rights (SARs) With Remeasurement

Given: On 1 Jan Year 1, a company grants 500 employees 200 Share Appreciation Rights each (1,00,000 SARs total), vesting after 2 years. SARs entitle employees to a CASH payment equal to the increase in share price above ₹100 (the grant-date price). No employees are expected to leave. Fair value of each SAR: Year 1 end = ₹18; Year 2 end (vesting date) = ₹25.

Year 1 — Cumulative liability & expense:
Cumulative liability = 1,00,000 SARs × ₹18 × (1/2) = ₹9,00,000
Year 1 expense = ₹9,00,000 (first year)

Year 2 — Cumulative liability & expense (using vesting-date fair value):
Cumulative liability = 1,00,000 SARs × ₹25 × (2/2) = ₹25,00,000
Year 2 expense = ₹25,00,000 − ₹9,00,000 = ₹16,00,000

Key Point — Compare to Equity-Settled: Notice the fair value PER SAR changed from ₹18 to ₹25 between Year 1 and Year 2 — this REMEASUREMENT at each reporting date is exactly what distinguishes cash-settled from equity-settled treatment (where the ₹15/option grant-date fair value in the 30.3 example never changed). This remeasurement continues even AFTER vesting, right up until the SARs are actually exercised/settled in cash, creating ongoing P&L volatility tied to the company's share price.
30.6
Market vs Non-Market Vesting Conditions
Condition TypeExampleAccounting Treatment
Service ConditionEmployee must remain employed for 3 yearsStandard treatment — adjust the NUMBER of options expected to vest as estimates change (as in 30.3)
Non-Market Performance ConditionCompany's revenue must grow by 20% over 3 yearsSame as service conditions — adjust the number expected to vest based on the likelihood of the target being met
Market ConditionCompany's share price must reach ₹500, or outperform a peer indexBuilt INTO the grant-date fair value calculation itself (via option-pricing models) — NEVER adjusted afterward, even if the market condition is ultimately not met
🔑 The Critical Distinction
This is one of the most commonly tested SBR distinctions: for a MARKET condition (like a share price target), the expense is recognised regardless of whether the condition is actually achieved — because the probability of achieving it was already factored into the grant-date fair value using an option-pricing model. For non-market/service conditions, failure to meet the condition means NO expense is ultimately recognised for those forfeited options (adjusted through the "number expected to vest").
30.7
Modifications, Cancellations & Employee Choice Awards
🔧
Modifications
  • If a modification INCREASES fair value (e.g. reducing the exercise price), the incremental value is recognised as ADDITIONAL expense over the remaining vesting period
  • If a modification DECREASES fair value, the original grant-date fair value continues to be used — companies cannot reduce expense already committed to
Asymmetric Treatment
❌
Cancellations
  • If the COMPANY cancels an award (not the employee forfeiting it through leaving), any remaining unrecognised expense is accelerated and recognised IMMEDIATELY
  • Treated as if vesting occurred at the cancellation date — the full remaining cost hits P&L at once
Immediate Recognition
30.8
Step Acquisitions (Business Combinations Achieved in Stages)

Sometimes a company builds up control gradually — first acquiring a minority stake (e.g. as an associate), then later acquiring enough additional shares to gain CONTROL. IFRS 3 has specific rules for this "step acquisition" scenario.

STEP 1
Derecognise the Previous Investment — remove the carrying value of the previously-held investment (e.g. the associate, under equity method) from the books
STEP 2
Remeasure to Fair Value — the previously-held stake is REMEASURED to its fair value AT THE DATE CONTROL IS OBTAINED — any gain/loss versus its previous carrying value goes to P&L
STEP 3
Calculate Goodwill on the FULL Combination — goodwill is calculated using the fair value of the ENTIRE consideration transferred (both the fair value of the previously-held interest PLUS the fair value of new consideration paid), compared against 100% of identifiable net assets acquired
30.9
Step Acquisition — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: Associate Becomes Subsidiary

Given: Parent Co previously held 25% of Target Co as an associate, carried at ₹3 Crore under the equity method. Parent now acquires an additional 50% (total 75%, gaining control) for ₹9 Crore cash. At the acquisition date, the fair value of the previously-held 25% stake is ₹3.5 Crore. Fair value of Target's identifiable net assets = ₹10 Crore. NCI is measured at its proportionate share of net assets (25% × ₹10 Crore = ₹2.5 Crore).

Step 1 — Gain on remeasurement of previously-held interest:
Fair value at acquisition date (₹3.5 Cr) − Previous carrying value (₹3 Cr) = ₹0.5 Crore gain recognised in P&L

Step 2 — Goodwill calculation:
Fair value of previously-held interest: ₹3.5 Crore
+ Fair value of new consideration paid: ₹9 Crore
+ NCI at acquisition: ₹2.5 Crore
= Total: ₹15 Crore
− Fair value of identifiable net assets: ₹10 Crore
= Goodwill = ₹5 Crore

Key Point: The goodwill calculation uses the FAIR VALUE of the previously-held stake (₹3.5 Cr) — not its original cost or previous carrying value (₹3 Cr) — because control is deemed to be obtained through a single, fresh transaction at fair value, even though economically it was built up over two separate purchases.
30.10
Disposals — Full and Partial
Disposal TypeAccounting Treatment
Full Disposal (loses control entirely)Derecognise ALL subsidiary assets/liabilities/goodwill/NCI; recognise gain/loss on disposal; any RETAINED interest is remeasured to fair value at the date control is lost
Partial Disposal — STILL Retains ControlTreated as an EQUITY transaction between owners (parent and NCI) — NO gain/loss recognised in P&L; the difference is adjusted directly through equity (adjusting NCI and parent's retained earnings)
Partial Disposal — LOSES ControlTreated like a full disposal (derecognise everything), with any retained stake remeasured to fair value — same principle as step acquisitions, applied in reverse
30.11
Disposal Retaining Control — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: Parent Sells Down From 90% to 70%, Control Retained

Given: Parent sells a 20% stake in Subsidiary Co (reducing its holding from 90% to 70% — still control, since 70% > 50%) for ₹8 Crore cash. At disposal date, Subsidiary's net assets = ₹30 Crore.

Step 1 — Confirm this is an equity transaction (control retained):
Since Parent still controls Subsidiary after the sale (70% > 50%), this is NOT treated as a disposal generating a P&L gain/loss — it's an equity transaction between owners.

Step 2 — Calculate the adjustment to NCI:
NCI's share of net assets increases from 10% to 30% (as NCI's ownership grows from the sale)
NCI adjustment = (30% − 10%) × ₹30 Crore = 20% × ₹30 Cr = ₹6 Crore (increase to NCI)

Step 3 — Calculate the adjustment to Parent's equity (Retained Earnings):
Cash received: ₹8 Crore
Less: increase in NCI: ₹6 Crore
Adjustment to Parent's equity = ₹8 Cr − ₹6 Cr = ₹2 Crore (increase to Parent's Retained Earnings, directly through equity — NOT P&L)

Interpretation: No gain or loss appears anywhere in the Income Statement — the entire ₹8 Crore cash receipt is simply reallocated within EQUITY: ₹6 Crore increases NCI's recorded share (reflecting their larger 30% stake) and the remaining ₹2 Crore increases the Parent's own retained earnings. This is fundamentally different from the losing-control example in 30.12, which DOES generate a P&L gain/loss — the presence or absence of continuing control is the single factor that determines which treatment applies.
30.12
Disposal Losing Control — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: Parent Sells Down From 80% to 20%, Losing Control

Given: Parent sells 60% of its 80% stake in Subsidiary Co for ₹12 Crore cash, retaining 20% (no longer control — now just an investment). At disposal date: Subsidiary's net assets = ₹15 Crore, goodwill = ₹2 Crore, NCI (20% before disposal) = ₹3 Crore. Fair value of the retained 20% stake = ₹4 Crore.

Step 1 — Derecognise everything related to the subsidiary:
Net assets: −₹15 Crore
Goodwill: −₹2 Crore
NCI: +₹3 Crore (NCI derecognised — a credit, since it's removed as a liability-like balance)

Step 2 — Recognise consideration received AND retained interest at fair value:
Cash received: +₹12 Crore
Fair value of retained 20% stake: +₹4 Crore

Step 3 — Calculate the gain/loss on disposal:
(₹12 Cr cash + ₹4 Cr retained stake FV + ₹3 Cr NCI derecognised) − (₹15 Cr net assets + ₹2 Cr goodwill)
= ₹19 Crore − ₹17 Crore = ₹2 Crore gain on disposal, recognised in P&L

Key Point: The retained 20% stake is now accounted for as a simple investment (likely FVTPL or FVOCI under IFRS 9, or as an associate if significant influence remains) at its FAIR VALUE of ₹4 Crore going forward — not at any previous carrying amount.
30.13
Foreign Subsidiary Consolidation & Translation

When a foreign subsidiary's financial statements are in a different currency, they must be translated into the group's presentation currency BEFORE consolidation.

Translation Rules (IAS 21)
Assets & Liabilities (Balance Sheet)
Translated at the CLOSING rate (rate at the reporting date)
Income & Expenses (P&L)
Translated at the rate ruling AT THE DATE OF THE TRANSACTION — in practice, often an average rate for the period is used as a reasonable approximation
Resulting Exchange Difference
Goes to OTHER COMPREHENSIVE INCOME (a separate "Foreign Currency Translation Reserve" within equity) — NOT through profit or loss
🔑 Why the Translation Difference Bypasses P&L
This exchange difference is purely a TRANSLATION effect — it doesn't reflect an actual realised gain or loss from a transaction, just the mechanical effect of using different exchange rates for the balance sheet (closing rate) versus the P&L (average rate). Recognising it in OCI rather than P&L avoids injecting artificial volatility into reported profit from a purely accounting/translation mechanic.
30.14
Foreign Subsidiary Translation — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: Translating a US Subsidiary's Results Into INR

Given: A US subsidiary reports Net Assets at year-end of $10,00,000 and Profit for the year of $1,50,000. Exchange rates: Opening rate (1 Jan) = ₹82/$; Closing rate (31 Dec) = ₹86/$; Average rate for the year = ₹84/$. Opening net assets = $9,00,000, translated at last year's closing rate of ₹82/$.

Step 1 — Translate closing net assets (Balance Sheet — closing rate):
$10,00,000 × ₹86 = ₹8,60,00,000

Step 2 — Translate profit for the year (P&L — average rate):
$1,50,000 × ₹84 = ₹1,26,00,000 (this flows into the consolidated Income Statement)

Step 3 — Calculate the exchange difference (balancing figure to OCI):
Opening net assets (translated at opening rate): $9,00,000 × ₹82 = ₹7,38,00,000
+ Profit for the year (translated at average rate): ₹1,26,00,000
= Expected closing net assets: ₹8,64,00,000
Actual closing net assets (translated at closing rate): ₹8,60,00,000
Exchange difference = ₹8,60,00,000 − ₹8,64,00,000 = −₹4,00,000 (a translation LOSS, taken to OCI, not P&L)

Interpretation: Even though the subsidiary was PROFITABLE in local currency ($1,50,000 profit) and the rupee weakened against the dollar over the year (₹82→₹86), the specific combination of opening, average, and closing rates used in translation produced a small net translation LOSS of ₹4,00,000 — this is purely a mechanical consequence of using different rates for different components (and the net asset base growing through the year via new capital or retained profit not captured in this simplified example), not a real economic loss, which is exactly why it's routed through OCI rather than distorting reported operating profit.

Key Takeaways & Action Steps

  • Grant-date fair value never changes
  • Number expected to vest DOES change
2️⃣

  • Never adjusted afterward, even if not achieved
  • Non-market conditions DO adjust vesting estimates

  • Gain/loss to P&L on remeasurement

  • Retaining control on partial disposal = equity transaction, no P&L

Common Mistakes, Flashcards & Interview Q&A

Part A — Common Mistakes (Don't Repeat These)

❌ MISTAKE 1
Remeasuring Grant-Date Fair Value for Equity-Settled Schemes
The fair value at grant date for equity-settled schemes is FIXED and never remeasured, even if the share price moves dramatically afterward. Only the estimated NUMBER of options expected to vest changes — recalculating fair value each period is a fundamental error confusing equity-settled with cash-settled treatment.
❌ MISTAKE 2
Adjusting for a Failed Market Condition
If a share price target (market condition) isn't achieved, the expense already recognised is NOT reversed — the probability of achieving it was already built into the original fair value calculation via an option-pricing model, and failure to meet it doesn't change that.
❌ MISTAKE 3
Using Original Cost Instead of Fair Value for the Previously-Held Stake in a Step Acquisition
When control is obtained through a step acquisition, the previously-held interest must be remeasured to FAIR VALUE at the date control is obtained, with the gain/loss going to P&L — using its original cost or previous carrying value in the goodwill calculation is a common and significant error.
❌ MISTAKE 4
Recognising a P&L Gain/Loss on a Partial Disposal That Retains Control
If control is RETAINED after a partial disposal, this is treated purely as an equity transaction between owners (parent and NCI) — no gain/loss goes through P&L. A P&L gain/loss only arises when control is actually LOST.
❌ MISTAKE 5
Translating P&L Items at the Closing Rate
Balance sheet items use the closing rate; P&L (income/expense) items use the rate at transaction date (commonly approximated by an average rate for the period) — using the closing rate for P&L items is a frequently tested error in foreign subsidiary consolidation.
❌ MISTAKE 6
Applying the Same Fair Value Treatment to Cash-Settled and Equity-Settled Schemes
As the worked examples in 30.3 and 30.5 show, equity-settled schemes use a FIXED grant-date fair value while cash-settled schemes are REMEASURED every period — treating a Share Appreciation Rights scheme (cash-settled) with fixed grant-date valuation, or vice versa, produces a materially wrong expense pattern.
❌ MISTAKE 7
Recognising a Gain/Loss on a Disposal That Retains Control
As the worked example in 30.11 shows, a partial disposal where the parent RETAINS control is purely an equity transaction — the entire consideration is reallocated between NCI and Parent's equity, with absolutely nothing recognised in the Income Statement, unlike a disposal that loses control.
❌ MISTAKE 8
Assuming a Weakening Home Currency Against a Foreign Subsidiary's Currency Always Produces a Translation Gain
As the worked example in 30.14 shows, the net translation difference depends on the specific interplay of opening, closing, and average rates alongside the actual movements in net assets during the year — a weakening rupee doesn't automatically guarantee a translation gain; the mechanical calculation must always be performed rather than assumed from the rate direction alone.

Part C — Interview Questions & Model Answers

QuestionWhat They Are TestingModel Answer
"Why does IFRS 2 require companies to expense employee share options, even though no cash is paid?"Conceptual understanding of the economic substance principleShare options represent a genuine economic cost to existing shareholders — issuing new shares dilutes their ownership, and the option itself has real value the employee receives as compensation for services. Not expensing it would understate the true cost of employing staff and overstate profit, which is why IFRS 2 requires recognising this non-cash cost, matched against the vesting period over which the service is provided.
"Walk me through how you'd account for a company gradually acquiring control of another company over two separate purchases."Applied understanding of step acquisition mechanicsThe previously-held stake — say an associate — is derecognised from its equity-method carrying value and remeasured to fair value at the date control is actually obtained, with any gain or loss going to profit or loss. Goodwill is then calculated using the FULL fair value of everything given up to obtain control — the fair value of the previously-held interest plus the fair value of any new consideration paid plus NCI — compared against the fair value of the target's identifiable net assets.
"Why does a foreign subsidiary's currency translation adjustment go to OCI instead of the income statement?"Depth of understanding of IAS 21's underlying rationaleThe translation adjustment arises purely from using different exchange rates for the balance sheet (closing rate) and income statement (average/transaction rate) — it's a mechanical translation effect, not a real economic gain or loss from an actual transaction. Routing it through OCI rather than P&L avoids injecting artificial currency-driven volatility into reported operating profit, which would misrepresent the underlying performance of the business.
"How would you explain the difference between equity-settled and cash-settled share-based payments to a client?"Communication skill combined with technical accuracyEquity-settled schemes — like standard employee stock options — are valued once at grant date, and that value never changes even if the share price moves dramatically afterward; only the estimated number of awards expected to actually vest gets adjusted. Cash-settled schemes — like Share Appreciation Rights — are revalued every reporting period based on the current share price, since the company will ultimately pay cash tied to that value, which means the expense can be much more volatile from year to year as the share price moves.
"What's the accounting difference between a partial disposal that retains control and one that loses control?"Core SBR technical distinction with real M&A relevanceIf control is retained after a partial disposal, it's treated purely as an equity transaction between the parent and NCI — the consideration received is simply reallocated between NCI's equity and the parent's retained earnings, with nothing hitting the income statement. If control is lost, the entire subsidiary is derecognised, a gain or loss is recognised in profit or loss, and any retained stake is remeasured to fair value — a fundamentally different accounting outcome driven entirely by whether the parent still controls the entity afterward.
