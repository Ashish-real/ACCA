Every strategy in Chapter 31 ultimately gets executed — or fails to be executed — by real people, organised into real structures, led by real managers, going through real change. A financially brilliant strategy that ignores how people actually behave, how organisations are actually structured, and how change actually gets resisted or adopted, is a strategy destined to underperform its own paper potential. This chapter covers the Organisational Behaviour and HR frameworks that complete the strategic picture — genuinely useful for any finance professional stepping into people-leadership responsibility.

"Culture eats strategy for breakfast" — a reminder that even the best strategic plan fails if the organisation's people, structure, and culture aren't aligned to deliver it.

32.2
Organisational Structures — Types & Trade-offs
Structure TypeDescriptionKey Trade-off
FunctionalOrganised by specialism (Finance, Marketing, Operations, HR)Deep specialist expertise BUT risk of departmental silos and slow cross-functional coordination
DivisionalOrganised by product line, geography, or customer segment, each with its own functionsFast, focused decision-making per division BUT duplication of functions increases overall cost
MatrixEmployees report to both a functional manager AND a project/product manager simultaneouslyFlexible resource sharing across projects BUT dual reporting lines can create conflict and confusion
Network / VirtualA core organisation coordinates a network of external partners/contractors rather than employing all functions internallyHighly flexible and asset-light BUT less direct control over quality and coordination
🔑 There's No Universally "Best" Structure
Structure choice should follow STRATEGY, not the other way around (a core principle from Alfred Chandler's "structure follows strategy"). A company pursuing rapid diversification into new markets likely needs a divisional structure for focused accountability; a company pursuing deep technical excellence in one core business likely benefits from a functional structure's specialist depth.
32.3
Choosing a Structure — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: Restructuring After Rapid Geographic Expansion

Situation: A company that has operated with a functional structure (single Finance, Marketing, Operations heads) for its India business is now expanding into 4 new countries, each with different regulatory environments, customer preferences, and competitive dynamics.

Problem with retaining the functional structure: A single central Marketing function trying to serve 5 very different countries would struggle to develop genuinely locally-relevant marketing strategies, and decision-making would be slow as every country-specific decision routes through centralised functional heads unfamiliar with local nuances.

Recommended structure: Shift to a DIVISIONAL structure organised by GEOGRAPHY — each country gets its own general manager with dedicated (even if smaller) functional support, enabling fast, locally-relevant decisions.

Trade-off to manage: This will increase overall cost (duplicated functional roles across 5 countries) and risk inconsistent standards/branding across markets — the company should retain some CENTRALISED functions (e.g. treasury, group financial reporting, core brand guidelines) even within an overall divisional structure, which is effectively a hybrid approach many real multinational organisations adopt in practice.
32.4
Leadership Theories — From Trait to Situational

Leadership theory has evolved significantly — from early theories assuming leaders are simply "born" with fixed traits, to modern theories recognising that effective leadership depends heavily on the SITUATION.

TheoryCore Idea
Trait Theory (early)Effective leaders share identifiable inherent personal traits (confidence, intelligence, decisiveness) — now considered overly simplistic, since many effective leaders don't share a common trait profile
Behavioural TheoryFocuses on what leaders actually DO (task-oriented vs people-oriented behaviours) rather than who they inherently are
Situational Leadership (Hersey-Blanchard)The MOST EFFECTIVE leadership style depends on the follower's readiness/maturity level for the specific task — no single "best" style works universally
Contingency Theory (Fiedler)Leadership effectiveness depends on the FIT between leadership style and the favourableness of the situation (leader-member relations, task structure, positional power)
🔑 The Situational Leadership Model in Practice
Hersey-Blanchard's model suggests: a highly DIRECTIVE style suits followers with low competence/confidence on a task; a COACHING style suits followers with some competence but still developing confidence; a SUPPORTING style suits competent followers who need encouragement rather than direction; and a DELEGATING style suits highly competent, confident followers who need minimal oversight. Effective leaders flex their style based on the specific follower and specific task — not a single fixed personal style.
32.5
Transformational vs Transactional Leadership
🔁
Transactional Leadership
  • Based on a clear exchange — rewards for meeting targets, correction/consequences for falling short
  • Effective for stable environments and routine, clearly-defined tasks
  • Limited ability to inspire genuine commitment beyond the immediate transactional reward
Exchange-Based
✨
Transformational Leadership
  • Inspires followers through a compelling VISION, genuine role-modelling, and individual development/mentorship
  • More effective for driving genuine CHANGE and innovation, since it builds intrinsic commitment rather than just compliance
  • Requires more time/relationship investment; not always necessary for routine operational management
Vision-Based
💡 When Each Style Fits Best
Transactional leadership works well for maintaining stable, well-understood operations (e.g. a well-established finance function running routine month-end close). Transformational leadership is generally more effective when driving significant CHANGE (e.g. leading a finance transformation programme, or a major strategic pivot) — most effective senior leaders draw on elements of both depending on the specific situation, rather than exclusively using one style.
32.6
Leadership Style — Worked Scenario

📋 Scenario: Choosing a Leadership Approach for a New Finance Hire

Situation: A finance manager has two direct reports — Person A, a newly-qualified analyst with strong technical skills but low confidence handling ambiguous, judgment-based tasks; Person B, a 6-year veteran with deep expertise and a strong track record of independent, high-quality work.

Applying Situational Leadership:
For Person A (competent but low confidence on ambiguous tasks) → a COACHING approach is appropriate: provide clear guidance and direction, but actively explain the reasoning, invite questions, and build confidence through supportive feedback — over time, transitioning toward more autonomy as confidence grows.

For Person B (highly competent, proven track record) → a DELEGATING approach is appropriate: assign the outcome/objective clearly, but grant significant autonomy over HOW to achieve it, checking in periodically rather than closely directing — over-managing a highly competent team member with a directive style would likely be demotivating and signal a lack of trust.

Key Insight: The SAME manager should genuinely use two DIFFERENT leadership styles with these two team members simultaneously — this is exactly the practical, situational application Situational Leadership theory recommends, and exactly the kind of nuanced people-management judgment expected of senior finance professionals managing a team.
32.7
Motivation Theories — Content Theories

Content theories focus on WHAT specifically motivates people — identifying the underlying needs that drive behaviour.

TheoryCore Idea
Maslow's Hierarchy of NeedsNeeds exist in a hierarchy (Physiological → Safety → Social → Esteem → Self-Actualisation) — lower needs must be broadly satisfied before higher needs become significant motivators
Herzberg's Two-Factor TheoryDistinguishes HYGIENE factors (salary, working conditions — their ABSENCE causes dissatisfaction, but their presence doesn't actively motivate) from MOTIVATORS (achievement, recognition, growth — these actively drive genuine motivation)
McClelland's Acquired Needs TheoryPeople are driven by varying combinations of 3 acquired needs: Achievement (desire to excel), Affiliation (desire for relationships), Power (desire for influence/control)
🔑 Herzberg's Critical Insight for Finance Managers
Simply paying people MORE (a hygiene factor) will remove dissatisfaction but won't necessarily create genuine, sustained motivation — that requires investing in MOTIVATORS like meaningful work, recognition, and growth opportunities. A finance team with excellent pay but no sense of achievement or growth potential may still show high turnover and low engagement — a frequently tested, practically important insight.
32.8
Motivation Theories — Process Theories

Process theories focus on HOW motivation actually happens — the cognitive process by which people decide how much effort to invest.

⚖️
Vroom's Expectancy Theory
  • Motivation = Expectancy (will effort lead to performance?) × Instrumentality (will performance lead to reward?) × Valence (do I actually value that reward?)
  • If ANY of these three is low/zero, overall motivation collapses — even a highly valued reward won't motivate if the person doesn't believe effort will actually lead to it
Effort-Reward Link
⚖️
Adams' Equity Theory
  • People assess motivation by comparing their own input/output ratio to a relevant comparator (colleague, industry peer)
  • PERCEIVED inequity (feeling under-rewarded relative to others' effort/reward ratio) reduces motivation, even if absolute pay is objectively reasonable
Relative Fairness
32.9
Motivation — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: Diagnosing a Motivation Problem Using Multiple Theories

Situation: A well-paid finance analyst has become visibly disengaged, doing only the minimum required work despite no change in salary or working conditions.

Applying Herzberg: Since HYGIENE factors (pay, conditions) are unchanged and presumably adequate, the disengagement likely stems from missing MOTIVATORS — perhaps the role has become repetitive with no recent recognition, growth opportunity, or sense of meaningful achievement.

Applying Equity Theory: Investigate whether the analyst perceives INEQUITY — perhaps a peer with a similar role and lower perceived effort recently received a promotion or bonus, creating a sense of unfair input/output ratio relative to that comparator, even if the analyst's own absolute pay hasn't changed.

Applying Expectancy Theory: Check whether the analyst still believes extra effort will lead to meaningful reward (Instrumentality) — if recent promotions/rewards have seemed disconnected from actual performance, the analyst may have rationally concluded extra effort isn't worth investing, regardless of how much they'd value the reward itself (Valence).

Integrated Diagnosis: A thorough manager wouldn't rely on just ONE theory — combining these lenses suggests investigating BOTH whether meaningful growth/recognition opportunities have genuinely dried up (Herzberg) AND whether a specific perceived unfairness event has occurred (Equity) AND whether the performance-reward link feels broken (Expectancy) — a genuinely diagnostic conversation with the analyst, informed by all three frameworks, is more likely to identify the real root cause than assuming any single explanation.
32.10
Change Management — Lewin's Three-Step Model

Kurt Lewin's classic model remains a foundational way to think about how organisational change actually happens.

UNFREEZE
Create the motivation for change by helping people understand WHY the current state is no longer acceptable/sustainable — reducing resistance by building a genuine case for change before attempting to implement it
CHANGE (MOVE)
Actually implement the new processes, structures, or behaviours — this is the transition period itself, often the most disruptive and uncertain phase
REFREEZE
Embed the change as the new normal — reinforcing it through updated policies, reward systems, and culture, so the organisation doesn't drift back to old habits
🔑 The Most Commonly Skipped Step
Organisations frequently rush straight to "Change" without adequately "Unfreezing" first (building genuine buy-in and understanding of WHY change is needed) — and just as commonly neglect "Refreezing" (assuming the change is complete once implemented, without reinforcing it), leading to the organisation gradually reverting to old behaviours once initial attention/pressure fades.
32.11
Change Management — Kotter's 8-Step Model

John Kotter's more detailed model provides a specific, actionable sequence for leading larger-scale organisational change.

Kotter's 8 Steps
1-2: Build Urgency & Coalition
Create a genuine sense of urgency for change; assemble a powerful enough guiding coalition of influential supporters
3-4: Vision & Communication
Develop a clear change vision and strategy; communicate that vision relentlessly through every available channel
5-6: Empower & Early Wins
Remove obstacles and empower broad-based action; generate visible SHORT-TERM WINS to build momentum and credibility
7-8: Consolidate & Anchor
Consolidate gains and produce more change, rather than declaring victory too early; anchor new approaches firmly in the organisational culture
💡 Kotter's Most Frequently Cited Failure Point
Kotter specifically identifies "declaring victory too soon" (skipping step 7) as one of the most common reasons change initiatives ultimately fail — celebrating an early win as if the change is complete allows resistance and old habits to creep back before the new approach is genuinely anchored in the culture (step 8).
32.12
Managing Resistance to Change — Worked Scenario

📋 Scenario: Finance Team Resisting a New ERP System

Situation: A finance team is resisting a new ERP system rollout — some team members are skeptical it will actually improve efficiency, others are anxious about their ability to learn the new system, and a few senior team members feel their years of expertise in the old system is being devalued.

Applying Lewin — Unfreeze phase gap: The resistance suggests insufficient "Unfreezing" occurred — the case for WHY change is needed wasn't convincingly made before implementation began, leaving skepticism unaddressed.

Root cause diagnosis by resistance TYPE:
• Skepticism about benefits → needs clear, credible EVIDENCE and communication (addressing a rational, evidence-based objection)
• Anxiety about learning the new system → needs TRAINING and support, addressing a genuine capability concern
• Feeling devalued expertise → needs explicit RECOGNITION of past contribution and a clear narrative of how existing expertise transfers/remains valuable in the new system, addressing an emotional/identity concern

Recommended approach (applying Kotter): Go back and strengthen the "Unfreeze"/urgency-building work with clear evidence of the current system's limitations, identify and empower influential team members as change champions (guiding coalition), invest specifically in training (addressing the capability-based resistance), and explicitly recognise the team's existing expertise while framing the new system as an evolution, not a replacement of that expertise — then generate an early visible win (e.g. one clearly improved process) to build momentum before pushing the full rollout.

Key Insight: Different sources of resistance require DIFFERENT interventions — treating all resistance as simple stubbornness and pushing through with authority alone typically increases resentment without resolving the underlying rational, capability, or emotional concerns driving that resistance.
32.13
Team Dynamics — Tuckman's Stages

Bruce Tuckman's model describes the predictable stages teams typically go through as they develop.

StageWhat Happens
FormingTeam members are polite, cautious, and dependent on the leader for direction — genuine trust hasn't yet developed
StormingConflict emerges as personalities, working styles, and views on approach clash — an uncomfortable but NORMAL and often necessary stage
NormingThe team establishes agreed working norms, resolves earlier conflicts, and begins to develop genuine cohesion and trust
PerformingThe team operates at high effectiveness with minimal need for direct oversight — genuine collaboration and mutual trust are established
Adjourning(Added later) The team disbands upon completing its purpose — relevant for project-based or temporary teams
🔑 Why "Storming" Shouldn't Be Feared
Teams (and managers) sometimes try to avoid or suppress the Storming stage, treating any conflict as a bad sign — but Tuckman's model suggests Storming is a NORMAL, often NECESSARY stage for a team to work through genuine differences before reaching true cohesion. Suppressing conflict rather than working through it can prevent a team from ever reaching genuine Norming and Performing stages.
32.14
Team Roles — Belbin's Model

Meredith Belbin's model identifies 9 distinct team roles people naturally gravitate toward — a well-balanced team benefits from a genuine MIX of these roles, not everyone playing the same type.

🧠
Thinking Roles
  • Plant: creative, generates original ideas
  • Monitor Evaluator: analyses options critically and objectively
  • Specialist: brings deep technical expertise
Idea Generation
🤝
People Roles
  • Coordinator: clarifies goals, delegates effectively
  • Teamworker: builds cohesion, resolves friction
  • Resource Investigator: explores external opportunities, networks
Relationship Building
⚙️
Action Roles
  • Shaper: drives the team forward, challenges the team to improve
  • Implementer: turns ideas into practical, disciplined action
  • Completer Finisher: ensures thoroughness, catches errors, meets deadlines
Execution
💡 Practical Application for Finance Teams
A finance team composed ENTIRELY of Completer Finishers might be extremely accurate and deadline-driven but lack the Plant's creative problem-solving or the Shaper's drive to challenge outdated processes. Understanding Belbin roles helps a manager deliberately build a BALANCED team, or at least recognise and consciously compensate for gaps in an existing team's natural role composition.

Organisational Culture — Handy's Model

Charles Handy's model identifies four distinct organisational culture "types," each with different implications for how decisions get made and how people are expected to behave.

Culture TypeCharacteristicsBest Suited To
Power CultureCentred on a single powerful individual/small group; fast decisions but heavily dependent on that personSmall, entrepreneurial organisations
Role CultureHighly structured, rule-based, hierarchical — clear job descriptions and defined authorityStable, predictable environments (e.g. regulated industries, government bodies)
Task CultureOrganised flexibly around specific projects/problems, expertise-driven rather than hierarchy-drivenInnovative, project-based organisations (e.g. consulting, tech)
Person CultureIndividuals are the central focus, with minimal organisational hierarchy — the "organisation" exists mainly to support the individuals within itProfessional partnerships (e.g. law firms, some consulting/medical practices)

Common Mistakes, Flashcards & Interview Q&A

Part A — Common Mistakes (Don't Repeat These)

❌ MISTAKE 1
Assuming One Leadership Style Works for Everyone
Situational Leadership specifically rejects a single "best" style — the same manager should genuinely flex between directive, coaching, supporting, and delegating approaches depending on each individual follower's competence and confidence for the specific task at hand.
❌ MISTAKE 2
Believing Higher Pay Alone Will Fix a Motivation Problem
Per Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory, pay is a HYGIENE factor — insufficient pay causes dissatisfaction, but MORE pay beyond an adequate level doesn't reliably create sustained motivation. Genuine motivation requires investing in MOTIVATORS like achievement, recognition, and growth.
❌ MISTAKE 3
Skipping Lewin's "Unfreeze" Stage and Jumping Straight to Implementing Change
Implementing change without first building genuine understanding of WHY the current state is unsustainable (Unfreeze) typically generates unnecessary resistance — people need to understand and accept the case for change before they'll genuinely embrace it.
❌ MISTAKE 4
Declaring a Change Initiative Complete After an Early Win
Kotter specifically warns against declaring victory too soon — an early visible win should be used to build momentum for FURTHER change and to anchor the new approach in culture (steps 7-8), not treated as the finish line, or the organisation risks reverting to old habits.
❌ MISTAKE 5
Treating Team Conflict (Storming) as Something to Avoid at All Costs
Tuckman's model treats Storming as a NORMAL, often NECESSARY stage of team development — suppressing legitimate disagreement to avoid discomfort can prevent a team from ever reaching genuine Norming and Performing stages built on real, tested trust.
❌ MISTAKE 6
Treating All Resistance to Change as Simple Stubbornness
As the worked scenario in 32.12 shows, resistance can stem from rational skepticism, genuine capability/confidence concerns, or emotional/identity concerns — each requiring a fundamentally different response (evidence, training, or recognition respectively). Applying a single "push through with authority" response to all resistance types is a common and ineffective error.
❌ MISTAKE 7
Assuming Equity Theory Is About Absolute Pay Levels
Equity Theory is about PERCEIVED RELATIVE fairness compared to a chosen comparator, not absolute pay adequacy — an employee can be well-paid in absolute terms yet still demotivated if they perceive their input/output ratio as unfair relative to a specific peer or reference group.
❌ MISTAKE 8
Building a Team With Only One Belbin Role Type
A team composed entirely of similar personality/role types (e.g. all Completer Finishers, or all Shapers) tends to be unbalanced — missing either the creative idea generation, the relationship-building, or the disciplined execution that a genuinely effective team needs across all three role categories.
