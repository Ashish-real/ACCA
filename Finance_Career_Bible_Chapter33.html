33.15Key Takeaways & Action Steps

Valuation is arguably the most important skill in all of finance. Every financial decision — whether to acquire a company, whether to invest in a stock, whether to launch a project, whether to take a company public — requires an answer to one fundamental question: What is this thing actually worth? This chapter teaches you the major methods professionals use to answer that question, with the same rigour used by investment bankers, PE analysts, and CFOs at the world's top firms.

"Price is what you pay. Value is what you get. The entire discipline of valuation exists to bridge the gap between the two — and exploit it." — Warren Buffett (adapted)
33.1
Why Valuation Matters — The Core Question in Finance

Every major financial event requires valuation. An IPO needs a price. An M&A deal needs a negotiated value. A private equity fund needs to know what to pay for a target. An equity research analyst needs to decide if a stock is cheap or expensive. A CFO needs to know if their own company is undervalued or overvalued before raising capital. Valuation is the common language of all these decisions.

🏦
Investment Banking
  • Value M&A targets — advise buyers on what to pay and sellers on what to accept
  • Price IPOs — set an offer price that is fair to both issuer and investors
  • Fairness opinions — tell a board whether a deal price is fair
  • Leveraged buyout (LBO) models — determine maximum price PE can pay and still hit return targets
  • Valuation is the #1 technical skill tested in IB interviews
M&A / IPO
📈
Equity Research
  • Analysts build DCF models to estimate intrinsic value of listed stocks
  • Compare to current market price → Buy, Hold, or Sell recommendation
  • Publish price targets — what the stock should be worth in 12 months
  • Update models after every earnings release, guidance change, macro event
  • Institutional investors use this research to allocate billions
Stock Analysis
💼
Private Equity & VC
  • PE: buy companies at one value, improve them, sell at higher value — entry and exit valuation critical
  • VC: value early-stage startups with no earnings — use revenue multiples, market size, comparables
  • LBO modelling: reverse-engineer what price yields target IRR (typically 20-25%+)
  • Portfolio company valuation for fund reporting — mark-to-market quarterly
PE / VC
🏢
Corporate Finance (CFO Role)
  • Value acquisition targets — should we buy this company? What is it worth to us?
  • Assess our own valuation — are we cheap? Should we buy back shares?
  • Value new projects / capex — NPV is a form of project valuation (Chapter 22)
  • Impairment testing — IFRS requires valuing goodwill and assets for impairment annually
  • Fairness opinions for transactions — CFO must defend the price paid to shareholders
CFO / Corp Dev
⚖️
Accounting & Audit (ACCA Context)
  • IFRS 13 — Fair Value Measurement: assets and liabilities must be valued at fair value under many IFRS standards
  • Goodwill impairment testing: recoverable amount (higher of fair value less costs to sell and value in use)
  • Financial instruments at fair value — equity investments, derivatives
  • Purchase price allocation (PPA) in business combinations — IFRS 3
  • ACCA AFM and SBR both test valuation extensively
ACCA / IFRS
🌐
Valuation in India — Regulatory Context
  • SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) mandates independent valuation for open offers, delistings, related party transactions
  • ICAI has published Valuation Standards (2018) — framework for registered valuers
  • Companies Act 2013 — certain transactions require valuation by registered valuer (under IBBI)
  • IBBI (Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India) — registered valuers for insolvency resolution
  • Tax (Section 56 of Income Tax Act) — share valuation for unlisted companies to prevent tax avoidance

33.2
The Three Pillars of Valuation — An Overview

All valuation methods fall into three broad categories. Each answers the same question — what is this company worth — but from a different angle. No single method is perfect. Professionals always use multiple methods together and triangulate a range. Knowing all three, and when to use each, is what separates a competent analyst from an outstanding one.

🔮
Pillar 1 — Intrinsic Valuation (DCF)
  • What is this company worth based on its own future cash flows?
  • Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) — project future free cash flows and discount to present value
  • Theoretically the most rigorous — value comes from fundamentals, not market sentiment
  • Limitations: highly sensitive to assumptions (growth rate, discount rate) — small changes in inputs = large changes in output
  • Best for: mature, predictable businesses with stable cash flows
  • Used heavily by: equity analysts, PE investors, corporate acquirers
DCF Method
📊
Pillar 2 — Relative Valuation (Multiples)
  • What do similar companies trade at in the market? Apply those multiples to our company.
  • Trading Comps: P/E ratio, EV/EBITDA, EV/Revenue, P/B — compared to listed peers
  • Faster to compute than DCF, anchored to real market prices
  • Limitation: if the whole market is overvalued (bubble), comparables are also overvalued — you can be "relatively cheap" but still absolutely expensive
  • Best for: businesses with clear public comparables; quick sanity checks
  • Used by: virtually everyone — investment bankers, analysts, fund managers
Multiples / Comps
🏗️
Pillar 3 — Asset-Based Valuation
  • What are the net assets of this company worth if we liquidated or replaced them?
  • Net Asset Value (NAV) — total assets minus total liabilities at fair value
  • Replacement Cost — what would it cost to rebuild this business from scratch?
  • Limitation: ignores going-concern value (the value of future earnings above and beyond asset value)
  • Best for: asset-heavy businesses (real estate, banks, holding companies), distressed companies, liquidation scenarios
  • Used by: banks valuing collateral, restructuring advisors, real estate analysts
NAV / Asset
🔑 The Golden Rule of Valuation — Never Use Just One Method
Every professional valuation uses at least two or three methods and presents a range of values — not a single number. A DCF gives you intrinsic value. Trading comps give you market context. Precedent transactions give you deal-level pricing. The truth usually lies somewhere in the overlap. The skill is not just knowing each method — it is knowing how to weight them given the specific situation, asset type, and purpose of the valuation.

33.3
Intrinsic Value vs Market Value — Key Distinction

Before diving into the methods, you must understand the fundamental distinction between Intrinsic Value and Market Value. These are often different — and exploiting that difference is how investors make money.

🎯
Intrinsic Value
  • The "true" value of a company based on its fundamentals — cash flows, growth, risk
  • Calculated through analysis — DCF is the primary method
  • Does NOT depend on what the market currently thinks or what other people are paying
  • A company generating ₹100Cr of free cash flow per year, growing at 12%, with low risk has a specific intrinsic value regardless of its current share price
  • Warren Buffett's entire investment philosophy: buy companies whose intrinsic value is far above market price
  • Problem: intrinsic value requires assumptions about the future — two analysts can calculate very different intrinsic values for the same company
Fundamental Value
📉
Market Value
  • The price the market is currently willing to pay — share price × shares outstanding = Market Cap
  • Reflects collective opinion of all investors at this moment in time
  • Driven by: current earnings, sentiment, macro conditions, news flow, technical factors, momentum
  • Can deviate significantly from intrinsic value — especially during bubbles (overvalued) or crashes (undervalued)
  • In the short run, market price is driven by supply and demand. In the long run, it tends to converge to intrinsic value.
  • Benjamin Graham: "In the short run, the market is a voting machine. In the long run, it is a weighing machine."
Market Price
⚖️
The Margin of Safety
  • The gap between intrinsic value and the price you actually pay
  • If intrinsic value = ₹200 per share and you buy at ₹120 → margin of safety = 40%
  • Protects you against errors in your own analysis (you might be wrong about growth rate)
  • Protects you against bad luck (unexpected events hit the business)
  • The larger the margin of safety, the better the investment — core principle of value investing
  • ACCA AFM: understand this concept even though it is not always explicitly tested — it underpins all valuation
Buffett / Graham
💡 Why Valuation Is Hard — The Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH)
The Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH) says that market prices already reflect all available information — so it is impossible to consistently find undervalued stocks. If EMH is true, intrinsic value = market value at all times, and valuation analysis is pointless.

Three forms: Weak form (prices reflect all past price data — technical analysis useless), Semi-strong form (prices reflect all public information — fundamental analysis useless), Strong form (prices reflect all information including insider information — no one can beat the market).

ACCA AFM: Know all three forms of EMH. The exam often asks you to discuss implications for analysts, investors, and corporate decisions (e.g. timing of share issues). Most practitioners believe markets are semi-strong at best — there are still opportunities for rigorous valuation analysis.
33.4
DCF Valuation — Discounted Cash Flow Method (Deep Dive)
Most Important Valuation Method

DCF is the gold standard of valuation. The core idea is simple: a company is worth the present value of all the future cash flows it will ever generate. In practice, this means projecting free cash flows for a forecast period (typically 5–10 years), then calculating a terminal value for everything beyond that, and discounting everything back to today using an appropriate discount rate (WACC).

"The value of any investment is, and always must be, the present value of all the cash it will generate over its lifetime." — John Burr Williams, The Theory of Investment Value (1938)

DCF Step-by-Step Process

STEP 01
Understand the Business & Gather Historical Data
Read annual reports, understand the business model, revenue drivers, cost structure, capex intensity. Gather 3–5 years of historical financials. Calculate historical revenue growth, EBITDA margins, Free Cash Flow conversion. This tells you what the business has delivered — the foundation for your forecast.
STEP 02
Project Free Cash Flow to Firm (FCFF) for 5–10 Years
Build a financial model forecasting Revenue → EBIT → NOPAT (Net Operating Profit After Tax) → Add back: Depreciation & Amortisation → Subtract: Capex, Changes in Working Capital → = Free Cash Flow to Firm (FCFF). Assumptions should be justified by industry dynamics, management guidance, competitive position, and macro outlook.
STEP 03
Calculate the Discount Rate (WACC)
WACC = Cost of Equity (from CAPM — covered in Chapter 22) weighted by equity proportion + Cost of Debt (post-tax) weighted by debt proportion. WACC reflects the risk of the entire business — the higher the risk, the higher the WACC, the lower the present value. (Full WACC detail in Section 33.5.)
STEP 04
Calculate Terminal Value
After your forecast period ends, the company still has value — this is the terminal value. Two methods: Gordon Growth Model (GGM) — assume FCF grows at a constant rate forever; or Exit Multiple — apply a multiple (e.g. EV/EBITDA) to the final year's metric. Terminal value often accounts for 60–80% of total DCF value — so getting this right is critical. (Full detail in Section 33.6.)
STEP 05
Discount Everything Back to Present Value
PV of each year's FCFF = FCFF ÷ (1 + WACC)^n. PV of Terminal Value = Terminal Value ÷ (1 + WACC)^n (where n = end of forecast period). Sum of all PVs = Enterprise Value (EV). This is the total value of the business including both equity and debt.
STEP 06
Bridge from Enterprise Value to Equity Value (Share Price)
Equity Value = Enterprise Value − Net Debt (Total Debt − Cash). This gives you the value attributable to equity shareholders. Equity Value ÷ Shares Outstanding = Intrinsic Value Per Share. Compare to current market price to determine if the stock is undervalued (buy) or overvalued (sell).

Free Cash Flow to Firm — The Full Build

FCFF Build — From P&L and Balance Sheet
EBIT (Earnings Before Interest & Tax)
Revenue − COGS − Operating Expenses − D&A
This is operating profit — the return generated by the business before financing costs and tax.
NOPAT (Net Operating Profit After Tax)
EBIT × (1 − Tax Rate)
Tax-adjusted operating profit. Removes the tax benefit of debt interest — treats firm as if all equity financed (tax effect captured in WACC separately).
+ Depreciation & Amortisation
Add back D&A (non-cash charge)
D&A reduced EBIT but did not consume cash — we add it back to get to cash earnings.
− Capital Expenditure (Capex)
Subtract investment in PP&E and other long-term assets
Cash actually spent on maintaining and growing the asset base. From cash flow statement or capex schedule.
− / + Change in Working Capital
−ΔInventory − ΔReceivables + ΔPayables
Growing businesses often need more working capital — this consumes cash. Shrinking WC (e.g. reducing receivables) releases cash.
= Free Cash Flow to Firm (FCFF)
NOPAT + D&A − Capex − ΔNWC
The cash the business generates after all operating investments. Available to be distributed to both debt and equity holders — hence "to firm."

DCF Worked Example — XYZ Ltd

📊 Full DCF Valuation — XYZ Manufacturing Ltd

Company: XYZ Manufacturing Ltd. Revenue FY25 = ₹500Cr. EBIT Margin = 18%. Tax Rate = 25%. D&A = ₹30Cr/yr. Capex = ₹40Cr/yr. Change in NWC = ₹10Cr/yr (growing). WACC = 12%. Revenue grows at 10%/yr for 5 years. Terminal Growth Rate = 5%. Net Debt = ₹200Cr. Shares Outstanding = 10Cr.

Step 1 — Calculate Base Year FCFF (FY25):
EBIT = ₹500Cr × 18% = ₹90Cr
NOPAT = ₹90Cr × (1 − 25%) = ₹67.5Cr
FCFF = ₹67.5Cr + ₹30Cr − ₹40Cr − ₹10Cr = ₹47.5Cr

Step 2 — Project FCFF (assume FCFF grows in line with revenue at 10%/yr):
FY26: ₹47.5Cr × 1.10 = ₹52.25Cr → PV = 52.25 ÷ 1.12 = ₹46.65Cr
FY27: ₹57.48Cr → PV = 57.48 ÷ 1.12² = ₹45.84Cr
FY28: ₹63.22Cr → PV = ₹44.95Cr
FY29: ₹69.55Cr → PV = ₹44.18Cr
FY30: ₹76.50Cr → PV = ₹43.40Cr
Sum of PV of FCFF (FY26–FY30) = ₹225.02Cr

Step 3 — Terminal Value (Gordon Growth Model, at end of FY30):
Terminal Value = FCFF_FY30 × (1 + g) ÷ (WACC − g)
= ₹76.50Cr × 1.05 ÷ (12% − 5%)
= ₹80.33Cr ÷ 7%
= ₹1,147.5Cr
PV of Terminal Value = ₹1,147.5Cr ÷ 1.12⁵ = ₹651.0Cr

Step 4 — Enterprise Value:
EV = ₹225.02Cr + ₹651.0Cr = ₹876.0Cr

Step 5 — Equity Value & Intrinsic Share Price:
Equity Value = EV − Net Debt = ₹876Cr − ₹200Cr = ₹676Cr
Intrinsic Share Price = ₹676Cr ÷ 10Cr shares = ₹67.6 per share

Decision: If the stock currently trades at ₹50 → it is undervalued by 35% → potential BUY. If it trades at ₹90 → it is overvalued → potential SELL or HOLD.
⚠️ Key DCF Limitations — Always Discuss in Exams
1. Garbage In, Garbage Out: The DCF is only as good as its assumptions. Change WACC from 12% to 10% and the value jumps dramatically. Change terminal growth from 5% to 6% and the value can increase by 30–40%.

2. Terminal Value Dominates: In most DCF models, 60–80% of the total value comes from the terminal value — which is itself based on a perpetuity assumption. This is highly uncertain for any business beyond 5 years.

3. No Capture of Optionality: DCF values the business as it is, not what it could become. High-growth startups, biotech companies with pipeline drugs, or companies with real options (expansion rights, patents) are hard to value with DCF alone.

ACCA exam tip: When asked to evaluate a DCF, always acknowledge its limitations AND explain what information you would need to improve the analysis.
33.5
WACC as the Discount Rate — Linking Chapter 22 to 23

The discount rate in a DCF should reflect the risk of the cash flows being discounted. Since FCFF is the cash available to all providers of capital (both debt and equity), the correct discount rate is WACC — Weighted Average Cost of Capital (covered fully in Chapter 22). Here we focus on how WACC connects to and impacts the DCF valuation.

WACC Formula — Recap
WACC
WACC = Ke × [E/(E+D)] + Kd(1−t) × [D/(E+D)]

Ke = Cost of Equity (from CAPM: Rf + β(Rm−Rf))
Kd = Pre-tax cost of debt (interest rate or YTM on debt)
t = Corporate tax rate
E = Market value of equity | D = Market value of debt
Always use MARKET values for weights, never book values.

WACC Sensitivity — Small Changes Have Huge Impact

📊 WACC vs Valuation — Why 1% Change Matters Enormously

Using XYZ Ltd example from 23.4: Terminal Year FCFF = ₹76.5Cr, Terminal Growth = 5%

At WACC = 10%: Terminal Value = 76.5 × 1.05 ÷ (10%−5%) = ₹1,606Cr
At WACC = 12%: Terminal Value = 76.5 × 1.05 ÷ (12%−5%) = ₹1,148Cr
At WACC = 14%: Terminal Value = 76.5 × 1.05 ÷ (14%−5%) = ₹893Cr

A 2% change in WACC changes the terminal value by 40% in either direction. This is why sensitivity analysis (Section 33.12) is non-negotiable in any serious valuation.
📉
Higher WACC = Lower Valuation
  • Higher risk businesses demand higher returns from investors
  • Future cash flows are discounted more heavily → lower present value
  • Triggered by: higher beta, more debt (financial risk), rising interest rates
  • Impact: destroys company valuations when rates rise (seen in 2022 rate hike cycle globally)
  • High-growth tech stocks most impacted — their value is mostly in far-future cash flows, which discount heavily at high rates
Risk Up = Value Down
📈
Lower WACC = Higher Valuation
  • Lower risk, or lower interest rate environment → lower WACC
  • Future cash flows discounted less → higher present value
  • Why low-rate environments (2009–2021) drove asset price booms globally
  • Companies with stable, predictable cash flows (utilities, FMCG) always trade at premium — their WACC is low (low beta, low credit risk)
  • Debt tax shield reduces WACC (up to the point where financial distress costs rise)
Rate Down = Value Up
🎯
Adjusted WACC for Risk — Advanced Concept
  • If a company is valuing a division in a different industry (e.g. FMCG company valuing a pharma acquisition), it should use a WACC appropriate for the pharma industry — not its own WACC
  • Method: find a proxy company in the target industry → ungear its beta → regear to your capital structure → use in CAPM → get project-specific Ke → calculate adjusted WACC
  • This is the beta ungearing/regearing process — core ACCA AFM technique
ACCA AFM — Advanced
33.6
Terminal Value — The Biggest Number in Valuation
60–80% of Total DCF Value

After your explicit forecast period (typically 5–10 years), the company continues to generate cash flows into perpetuity. The Terminal Value (TV) captures this. Because TV often accounts for the majority of a DCF's total value, it deserves deep attention. There are two main methods for calculating it.

Terminal Value — Two Methods
Method 1 — Gordon Growth Model (GGM)
TV = FCFFn × (1 + g) ÷ (WACC − g)

FCFFn = Free Cash Flow in final forecast year
g = Long-term sustainable growth rate (usually GDP growth or inflation rate — 3–5% typically)
WACC − g = the "spread" — must be positive (WACC must exceed g or formula breaks)
Key rule: g should never exceed long-run GDP growth — no company can grow faster than the economy forever
Method 2 — Exit Multiple
TV = Final Year EBITDA × EV/EBITDA Multiple

Multiple = current trading multiple of comparable companies (EV/EBITDA, EV/EBIT, P/E)
More pragmatic — anchored to real market pricing
But introduces circular logic: if multiples compress (as in a bear market), TV falls dramatically
Commonly used by investment bankers and PE analysts in LBO models
⚠️
Common Terminal Value Mistakes
  • Using a growth rate higher than WACC — makes the formula produce negative or nonsensical value
  • Using unrealistically high terminal growth (e.g. 8–10% forever) — flatters valuation wildly
  • Forgetting to discount the TV back to present value — it exists at end of forecast period, not today
  • Not checking: does the implied EV/EBITDA of your GGM-derived TV make sense vs market multiples?
  • Allowing Capex < D&A in terminal year — implies the business is shrinking while growing
Watch Out
✅
Cross-Check Your Terminal Value
  • Always compute implied EV/EBITDA from your GGM terminal value and check vs comparable company multiples
  • If your implied multiple is 35x but peers trade at 15x — your growth/margin assumptions are too aggressive
  • Check: what % of your EV is the terminal value? If it is 90%+, you have very little visibility — acknowledge this limitation
  • Run sensitivity on g (terminal growth) — typically test 3%, 4%, 5% — and show the range this creates
Sanity Check
33.7
Relative Valuation — Trading Multiples (EV/EBITDA, P/E, P/B)

Relative valuation asks: What are similar companies worth, and what does that imply for our company? Instead of projecting cash flows, we look at what the market is currently paying for comparable businesses and apply those same pricing ratios to the company we are valuing. This is fast, practical, and market-anchored — which is why it is the most widely used valuation tool in day-to-day finance.

How to Run a Trading Comps Analysis — Step by Step

STEP 01
Select Comparable Companies
Choose 5–10 publicly listed companies that are similar to your target in: industry/sector, business model, geographies, size (revenue, market cap), growth profile, margin profile. Perfect comps rarely exist — you must use judgement. Disclose any differences and adjust for them.
STEP 02
Gather Financial Data for Each Comparable
Collect: Market Cap, Share Price, Shares Outstanding, Total Debt, Cash and Equivalents, EBITDA, EBIT, Net Profit (EPS), Revenue, Book Value. Sources: Bloomberg, Screener.in, Trendlyne, Moneycontrol, company filings (BSE/NSE). Use LTM (Last Twelve Months) data — or NTM (Next Twelve Months forecasts) for forward multiples.
STEP 03
Calculate Enterprise Value for Each Comparable
EV = Market Cap + Total Debt − Cash. Note: EV is the total value of the business independent of capital structure. This is why EV-based multiples (EV/EBITDA, EV/Revenue) are preferred for cross-company comparisons — they are not distorted by whether a company is financed with more debt or more equity.
STEP 04
Calculate Each Multiple for Each Comparable
Build a table: for each comparable company, calculate EV/EBITDA, P/E, EV/Revenue, P/B. Note the range — minimum, 25th percentile, median, 75th percentile, maximum. Exclude outliers if there is a clear reason (exceptional item, distressed company). The median is usually the best reference point.
STEP 05
Apply the Range of Multiples to Your Target
If comps trade at EV/EBITDA of 12x–18x (median 15x) and your target's LTM EBITDA is ₹100Cr:
→ Low case: 12 × ₹100Cr = ₹1,200Cr EV
→ Base case: 15 × ₹100Cr = ₹1,500Cr EV
→ High case: 18 × ₹100Cr = ₹1,800Cr EV
Then deduct net debt to get equity value range. This gives you a valuation range, not a single number.
💡 Premium and Discount Adjustments — When to Apply
Rarely is your target perfectly comparable to its peers. Consider adjusting (applying a premium or discount) for:

Premium for: Market leader position, superior margins, faster growth, stronger brand, better management, cleaner balance sheet, ESG leadership.

Discount for: Smaller size (small-cap discount, typically 10–20%), lower liquidity, key-man risk (business dependent on one founder), minority stake (lack of control), less diversified revenue base, weaker corporate governance, regulatory risk.

These adjustments require professional judgement — and should always be explicitly stated and justified in a valuation report.
33.8
Precedent Transaction Analysis

Precedent transactions (also called "deal comps") look at what acquirers have actually paid for similar companies in past M&A transactions. Unlike trading comps (which show minority stake prices in the stock market), precedent transactions reflect control premiums — the extra amount buyers pay above market value to acquire control of a company.

💰
Control Premium — Why Deals Are Priced Higher
  • When you buy 51%+ of a company, you gain CONTROL — you decide strategy, management, dividends, capex
  • Control is worth more than a minority stake — hence acquirers pay a premium
  • Typical control premium: 20–40% above pre-deal market price (varies by industry, deal type, competitive bidding)
  • This is why precedent transaction multiples are always HIGHER than trading comp multiples
  • In a competitive auction (multiple bidders), premiums can be 50%+ as bidders compete
  • ACCA AFM: understand that deal multiples > trading multiples due to control premium
M&A Premium
🔍
How to Find Precedent Transactions
  • Bloomberg M&A database — professional standard, requires subscription
  • Capital IQ (S&P Global) — comprehensive deal database
  • Mergermarket — deal news and data
  • In India: SEBI open offer disclosures, stock exchange announcements, Acquisdata, VCCEdge
  • Key filters: same industry, past 3–5 years (older deals less relevant due to market changes), similar target size, strategic vs financial buyer
  • Look for deals where full financials of target were disclosed — not all are
Data Sources
📋
Key Transaction Multiples
  • EV/EBITDA (LTM and NTM) — most common in M&A
  • EV/Revenue — for high-growth or loss-making targets
  • P/E — less common in M&A (capital structure noise), more in minority stake deals
  • EV/EBIT — for capital-intensive businesses
  • Premium to Pre-Deal Market Price — measures control premium directly
  • Always note: was deal all-cash, all-stock, or mixed? Stock deals may be priced differently from cash deals.

33.9
Asset-Based Valuation — NAV Method

Asset-based valuation determines company value by looking at what its assets are worth, minus what it owes. The key principle: you are valuing the balance sheet at fair value, not at historical cost. This is the dominant method for banks, real estate companies, holding companies, and businesses in financial distress.

Net Asset Value (NAV) Calculation
Book NAV (Accounting)
Total Equity on Balance Sheet = Total Assets − Total Liabilities
At historical cost. Quick but not meaningful for valuation purposes — ignores fair value changes in assets.
Adjusted NAV (Fair Value)
Fair Value of All Assets − Fair Value of All Liabilities
Revalue each asset class to current market/fair value (PP&E, investments, real estate, intangibles). Adjust liabilities for current market value of debt. This gives the true economic net asset value per share.
NAV Per Share
Adjusted NAV ÷ Shares Outstanding
Compare to current share price. P/B = Share Price ÷ NAV per share. P/B < 1 means market prices company below its net assets — unusual for profitable companies, common for distressed or capital-destroying businesses.
🏦
NAV for Banks — Why P/B Is the Key Multiple
  • Banks' assets ARE financial instruments (loans, bonds, securities) — they can be reliably fair valued
  • P/B ratio is the primary valuation multiple for banks: P/B = Market Cap ÷ Book Equity
  • A bank with P/B = 3x → market values it at 3x its book equity (implies high ROE, strong franchise)
  • A bank with P/B = 0.8x → market values it below book equity (implies ROE < cost of equity, poor quality assets, or distress)
  • HDFC Bank: P/B ~3–4x (premium private sector bank). Many PSU banks: P/B 0.5–1x.
  • Relationship: P/B = (ROE − g) ÷ (Ke − g). Higher ROE → deserves higher P/B.
Banking Sector
🏠
NAV for Real Estate Companies
  • Real estate companies' primary assets are properties — valued at market price by independent valuers
  • NAV = current market value of property portfolio − debt − other liabilities
  • Discount to NAV: real estate listed companies often trade at 10–20% discount to NAV (illiquidity discount, management fee drag, complexity)
  • REITs (Real Estate Investment Trusts) — in India: Embassy REIT, Mindspace REIT — also valued on NAV and distribution yield
  • Key metric: RNAV (Revalued Net Asset Value) — widely used for real estate developers
Real Estate
📦
NAV for Holding Companies
  • A holding company owns stakes in other listed or unlisted companies
  • NAV = Sum of fair values of all investee company stakes − holding company's own debt and costs
  • For listed investees: value = share price × number of shares held
  • For unlisted investees: value = DCF or multiples-based valuation of that investee
  • Holding company discount: holdcos typically trade at 20–40% discount to their NAV — due to lack of control, tax inefficiency of dividends flowing up, complexity
  • India example: Bajaj Holdings, Tata Investment Corporation — both trade at significant holdco discounts
Holdco / Conglomerate
33.10
Enterprise Value vs Equity Value — The Bridge

One of the most important and frequently confused distinctions in valuation is between Enterprise Value (EV) and Equity Value (Market Cap). Getting this wrong invalidates an entire valuation. Every analyst must master this bridge.

The EV ↔ Equity Value Bridge
Enterprise Value (EV)
EV = Market Cap + Total Debt + Preference Shares + Minority Interest − Cash & Cash Equivalents
EV is the TOTAL value of the business — what you would pay to buy the whole company (all equity + all debt) and then receive the cash sitting in it. Capital structure neutral.
From EV → Equity Value
Equity Value = EV − Total Debt − Preference Shares − Minority Interest + Cash
Alternatively: EV − Net Debt (where Net Debt = Total Debt − Cash). This is what is left for equity shareholders after all debt obligations are accounted for.
From Equity Value → Share Price
Intrinsic Share Price = Equity Value ÷ Diluted Shares Outstanding
Use DILUTED shares — include options, warrants, convertibles that could dilute equity. This is what the stock is worth per share — compare to market price.
⚠️ Most Common Valuation Mistakes — EV/Equity Confusion
Mistake 1: Using an EV multiple (like EV/EBITDA) but comparing to equity value (market cap). EBITDA is pre-debt metric → must compare to EV. Net Income is post-debt → must compare to equity value (P/E).

Mistake 2: Forgetting to subtract debt when going from EV to equity value. If a company has EV of ₹1,000Cr and debt of ₹400Cr, equity value is only ₹600Cr — not ₹1,000Cr.

Mistake 3: Adding cash instead of subtracting debt (or vice versa). Remember: EV = Market Cap + Net Debt. To get from EV back to equity value, subtract net debt (not add it).

ACCA Rule: EV-based multiples (EV/EBITDA, EV/EBIT, EV/Sales) pair with metrics available to ALL capital providers. Equity-based multiples (P/E, P/B, P/FCF) pair with metrics available to EQUITY shareholders only.

  • Base Case: Management guidance or consensus analyst forecasts — the most likely outcome

  • EBITDA margin: What if margins compress by 200bps due to competition?

  • Terminal growth rate: What if the business matures faster than expected?
  • Capex intensity: What if the business needs more reinvestment than planned?
  • Tax rate: What if tax rates change (policy risk)?

Valuation in India — Real World Context

India's capital markets and M&A landscape have specific characteristics that impact how valuation is applied in practice. Understanding these nuances is essential for anyone working in Indian finance — and for ACCA students who want to anchor theoretical concepts in Indian reality.

📈
Indian Market Multiples — Structural Premium
  • Indian listed equities historically trade at a premium to global emerging markets — due to strong growth, rule of law, democracy, large domestic consumption story
  • Nifty 50 forward P/E: typically 18–24x vs global EM average ~12–14x
  • Quality Indian franchises command extreme premiums: HUL 60x+, Asian Paints 70x+, HDFC Bank 3–4x book
  • Justification: superior ROE, strong balance sheets, pricing power, dominant market position, India's long-term growth story
  • Risk: when these premiums compress (global risk-off, macro shock), these stocks correct sharply
India Premium
🏛️
Regulatory Valuation Requirements
  • SEBI Open Offer: If you acquire 25%+ of a listed company, you must make an open offer at a SEBI-specified floor price (based on highest acquisition price and market average)
  • Delisting: Reverse Book Building process — shareholders quote the price at which they are willing to exit. Acquirer must accept if discovered price meets threshold.
  • Related Party Transactions: Listed companies must get independent valuation for material RPTs — SEBI regulations
  • Income Tax (Sec 56(2)(x) & 56(2)(viib)): Shares of unlisted companies issued above fair value — excess is taxable as income for recipient. Drives need for independent valuation for every unlisted share deal.
SEBI / Tax
💼
India M&A — Valuation Dynamics
  • Indian M&A deal volumes growing — driven by PE activity, consolidation in banking, telecom, real estate
  • Control premium in India: typically 25–45% for strategic acquisitions in quality businesses
  • Promoter-driven businesses: valuation often affected by governance questions — markets apply discount if promoter credibility is questioned
  • Family-owned businesses with succession issues: may have depressed valuations due to uncertainty
  • Private equity: PIPE deals (Private Investment in Public Equity) common — PE funds buy stakes in listed companies at a negotiated price, usually at a discount to market
Indian M&A
🚀
Startup Valuation in India
  • India's startup ecosystem — 100+ unicorns. Traditional DCF difficult for loss-making early-stage companies.
  • VC valuation methods: Revenue multiples (EV/Revenue of 5–20x for SaaS), GMV multiples (for marketplaces), user acquisition metrics (cost per user, LTV:CAC ratio)
  • Stage-based approach: Seed (team + idea + TAM), Series A (product-market fit), Series B+ (scale, unit economics), Pre-IPO (profitability path)
  • Zomato, Paytm, Nykaa listed at high revenue multiples — post-listing reality check when profitability questioned
  • Lesson: valuation disconnected from fundamentals always corrects — eventually

Key Takeaways, Common Mistakes, Flashcards, Interview Q&A & Action Steps

Part A — 12 Core Takeaways (Must Know)

1️⃣
Three Valuation Pillars — Never Just One
  • Intrinsic (DCF) — fundamental value based on future cash flows
  • Relative (Multiples) — what market pays for similar businesses
  • Asset-Based (NAV) — balance sheet at fair value
  • Always use at least 2 methods — present a range, never one number
  • If methods diverge, explain WHY — that explanation is the real insight
2️⃣
DCF Core Formula — Memorise This
  • FCFF = NOPAT + D&A − Capex − ΔNWC
  • NOPAT = EBIT × (1 − Tax Rate)
  • Discount FCFF at WACC for each forecast year
  • Add PV of Terminal Value
  • EV − Net Debt = Equity Value ÷ Shares = Intrinsic Share Price
3️⃣
Terminal Value = The Most Important Number
  • Usually 60–80% of total DCF value — do not guess this
  • GGM: TV = FCFFn × (1+g) ÷ (WACC − g)
  • g must always be less than WACC — otherwise formula breaks
  • g should not exceed long-run nominal GDP growth rate
  • Always cross-check: implied EV/EBITDA from TV vs peer multiples
4️⃣
EV vs Equity Value — Never Mix Up
  • EV = Market Cap + Total Debt − Cash (total business value)
  • Equity Value = EV − Net Debt (what belongs to shareholders)
  • EV multiples → pair with pre-debt metrics (EBITDA, EBIT, Revenue)
  • Equity multiples → pair with post-debt metrics (Net Profit, Book Value)
  • Mixing these up = entire valuation is wrong
5️⃣
WACC — Every 1% Change Is Massive
  • Higher WACC = higher discount = lower valuation
  • 1% increase in WACC can reduce DCF value by 15–25%
  • Rising interest rates (Rf ↑) → WACC ↑ → company values fall
  • This is exactly what happened globally in 2022 rate hike cycle
  • Always run WACC sensitivity in your model — non-negotiable
6️⃣
Control Premium — Deal Price vs Market Price
  • Precedent transaction multiples are ALWAYS higher than trading comps
  • Because: buyers pay extra for CONTROL (strategy, management, dividends)
  • Typical control premium: 20–40% above pre-deal market price
  • In competitive auctions: can go to 50%+
  • Max price to pay = Target EV + Synergies − Integration Costs
7️⃣
Multiples — Choose the Right One
  • EV/EBITDA → best all-round multiple for M&A (capital structure neutral)
  • P/E → good for profitable companies, distorted by leverage
  • EV/Revenue → use when company has no earnings (startups, loss-making)
  • P/B → banks and financial companies only (assets = financial instruments)
  • Always compare to same-sector peers — cross-sector multiples are meaningless
8️⃣
Intrinsic vs Market Value — Core Philosophy
  • Intrinsic value = what a business is worth based on fundamentals
  • Market value = what people are currently willing to pay
  • They diverge constantly — and that divergence creates investment opportunity
  • Margin of Safety = buy only when market price is well below intrinsic value
  • Long run: market price converges to intrinsic value (Benjamin Graham)
9️⃣
Sensitivity Analysis — Always Do It
  • Build a 2-variable sensitivity table: WACC vs terminal growth rate
  • Run 3 scenarios: Bull (optimistic), Base (likely), Bear (pessimistic)
  • Assign probability weights — calculate probability-weighted value
  • The range of outputs tells you how confident you can be in the valuation
  • Wide range = high uncertainty = require higher margin of safety
🔟
Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH)
  • Weak form: past prices irrelevant — technical analysis cannot beat market
  • Semi-strong form: all public info already priced in — research cannot beat market
  • Strong form: even insider info priced in — impossible to beat market
  • Reality: markets are roughly semi-strong — but not perfectly efficient
  • ACCA AFM: know all 3 forms and implications for investors and corporates
1️⃣1️⃣
NAV — When to Use Asset-Based Valuation
  • Banks and financial institutions: assets are financial → P/B is key multiple
  • Real estate companies: property at market value → RNAV method
  • Holding companies: sum of investee stakes at fair value
  • Distressed companies: liquidation value → what creditors will recover
  • Never use NAV for asset-light businesses (IT, pharma) — ignores earnings power
1️⃣2️⃣
Football Field Chart — Professional Communication
  • Always present valuation as a range from multiple methods
  • Visualise as horizontal bar chart — one bar per method showing low–high range
  • Mark current market price as a vertical line for reference
  • The overlap zone across methods = most defensible valuation range
  • This is how investment bankers present to boards and clients — learn this format

Part B — Most Common Valuation Mistakes (Don't Repeat These)

❌ MISTAKE 1
Using Book Value Weights in WACC Instead of Market Values
Book value of equity (from balance sheet) is historical cost — it is irrelevant for WACC. Always use market value of equity (share price × shares) and market value of debt (current fair value of outstanding debt). Using book values understates or overstates the WACC and corrupts the entire DCF.
❌ MISTAKE 2
Terminal Growth Rate Higher Than WACC
If g ≥ WACC, the Gordon Growth Model denominator (WACC − g) becomes zero or negative, producing an infinite or negative terminal value. This is mathematically impossible. In an exam, if your numbers produce this, re-check your inputs — you have made an error somewhere. In practice, g should not exceed long-run GDP growth (roughly 5–7% nominal for India).
❌ MISTAKE 3
Forgetting to Discount the Terminal Value Back to Present
The terminal value is calculated at the END of your forecast period (e.g., Year 5). You must discount it back to today: PV of TV = TV ÷ (1 + WACC)^5. Forgetting this step means you overvalue the company by counting future money as if it were present money — a very common and costly error.
❌ MISTAKE 4
Mixing Up EV Multiples and Equity Multiples
Applying an EV/EBITDA multiple to derive Equity Value directly (without subtracting net debt) is wrong. EV multiples → derive EV → then subtract net debt → get Equity Value. Equity multiples (P/E) → apply to earnings directly → get Equity Value. Confusing these inflates or deflates the value by the entire net debt amount.
❌ MISTAKE 5
Using Pre-Tax Cost of Debt in WACC
Interest is tax-deductible. The actual cost of debt to the company is Kd × (1 − tax rate). Using the gross interest rate without the tax adjustment overstates WACC and understates company value. Always: Kd (post-tax) = pre-tax interest rate × (1 − corporate tax rate).
❌ MISTAKE 6
Presenting a Single Valuation Number Without a Range
No professional valuation produces a single answer. DCF assumptions are inherently uncertain — a small change in WACC or growth rate produces a large change in value. Always present a range: "Based on our analysis, the company is worth ₹55–₹72 per share." A single number implies false precision and is unprofessional.
❌ MISTAKE 7
Using Capex Less Than D&A in Terminal Year
In the terminal year, if Capex < D&A, it implies the company is consuming its asset base without replacing it — inconsistent with an assumption that it will grow forever. In the terminal year, Capex should at minimum equal D&A (maintenance capex) plus any incremental capex needed to support the assumed growth rate. This is a subtle but important modelling discipline.
❌ MISTAKE 8
Ignoring Working Capital Changes in FCFF
A growing business usually needs more working capital (more inventory, more receivables). This is a real cash outflow that reduces FCFF. Students often forget to include ΔNWC and overstate free cash flow. Conversely, a shrinking or more efficient business releases working capital — which is a real cash inflow.
❌ MISTAKE 9
Not Adjusting Comparables for Differences
Applying the same multiple to a company that is smaller, riskier, or growing slower than the comparables overstates its value. Always consider: size discount, growth premium/discount, margin differences, geographic risk differences, liquidity discount (for unlisted companies). Acknowledge these in your answer — ACCA marks discussion of adjustments.
❌ MISTAKE 10
Not Concluding After the Calculation
The biggest mistake in ACCA AFM exams: doing all the calculations but not answering the actual question. The question usually asks: "Should the board accept this offer?" or "Is the share undervalued?" After your valuation — always conclude clearly. State whether the price is fair, too high, or too low, and give a recommendation. Calculations without conclusion earn fewer marks.

"When would you NOT use a DCF?"Judgment — knowing when NOT to use a tool is as important as knowing when to use itEarly-stage startups with no earnings or cash flows (use revenue multiples or VC methods). Banks and financial institutions (use P/B and dividend discount model — their FCFF is not meaningful). Companies in distress or likely to go bankrupt (use liquidation/NAV). Situations with extreme uncertainty where any 5-year forecast is essentially fiction. In these cases, multiples or asset-based methods are more appropriate primary valuation tools.
"What is the difference between intrinsic value and market value?"Core investment philosophyIntrinsic value is the fundamental worth of a business based on its expected future cash flows, discounted at an appropriate rate — independent of what the market currently says. Market value is the price the market is currently willing to pay — driven by sentiment, momentum, macro, and information flow. The two diverge constantly. Skilled investors like Buffett identify when market value is significantly below intrinsic value and invest with a margin of safety — the gap between the two.

Part E — All Key Formulas Quick Reference Sheet

Chapter 33 — Complete Formula Sheet
Free Cash Flow to Firm (FCFF)
FCFF = EBIT × (1−t) + D&A − Capex − ΔNWC
Or: NOPAT + D&A − Capex − ΔNWC
WACC
WACC = Ke × [E/(E+D)] + Kd(1−t) × [D/(E+D)]
Always: market value weights, post-tax cost of debt
Cost of Equity (CAPM)
Ke = Rf + β(Rm − Rf)
Terminal Value — Gordon Growth Model
TV = FCFFn × (1+g) ÷ (WACC − g)
Condition: g < WACC always
Terminal Value — Exit Multiple
TV = Final Year EBITDA × EV/EBITDA Multiple
PV of any Cash Flow
PV = Cash Flow ÷ (1 + WACC)^n
n = number of years from today
Enterprise Value (from market)
EV = Market Cap + Total Debt + Pref Shares + Minority Interest − Cash
Equity Value (from EV)
Equity Value = EV − Net Debt
Net Debt = Total Debt − Cash
Intrinsic Share Price
Share Price = Equity Value ÷ Diluted Shares Outstanding
P/E Ratio
P/E = Share Price ÷ Earnings Per Share (EPS)
EV/EBITDA Multiple
EV/EBITDA = Enterprise Value ÷ EBITDA
Price to Book (P/B)
P/B = Market Cap ÷ Book Value of Equity
Or: Share Price ÷ Book Value Per Share
Implied P/B from Fundamentals
P/B = (ROE − g) ÷ (Ke − g)
Higher ROE → deserves higher P/B. If ROE = Ke → P/B = 1
NAV Per Share
NAV/Share = (Fair Value of Assets − Fair Value of Liabilities) ÷ Shares Outstanding
Margin of Safety
Margin of Safety = (Intrinsic Value − Market Price) ÷ Intrinsic Value × 100%

Part F — ACCA AFM Specific Exam Strategy

🎯 How to Score 65%+ in AFM Valuation Questions

1. Read the question TWICE before writing anything. Identify: What valuation method is being tested? What is the ultimate question (buy/sell/accept/reject)? What data has been given? What have they NOT given (and what assumptions must you make)?

2. Structure your answer with clear headings. ACCA markers mark against a marking scheme — they are looking for specific points. Use headings like "WACC Calculation", "FCFF Projection", "Terminal Value", "Enterprise Value", "Equity Value", "Recommendation". This makes it easy for the marker to award you marks.

3. Show ALL workings — even if you think they are obvious. Every intermediate calculation is potentially worth marks. Write: formula → substitute values → intermediate step → final answer. Never skip steps.

4. State assumptions explicitly. "I have assumed a terminal growth rate of 4% based on India's long-run real GDP growth plus inflation." Markers award marks for clearly stated, reasonably justified assumptions — even if your number differs from the model answer.

5. If you get stuck on a number — use a reasonable assumption and move on. ACCA AFM allows own-figure marks (OFM) — if your earlier calculation was wrong but you correctly applied it in later steps, you still get those marks. Never leave a whole section blank because one input is wrong.

6. Always write a conclusion / recommendation. The question always ultimately asks for a recommendation. Write it clearly: "Based on our DCF valuation of ₹X per share, which exceeds the current market price of ₹Y, the share appears undervalued. We recommend the board considers this an attractive acquisition target at a price up to ₹Z (which includes a 25% control premium over intrinsic value)."

7. Discuss limitations — always. 2–4 marks are nearly always available for discussing limitations of the valuation method you used. Mention: sensitivity to assumptions, uncertainty of terminal value, comparability issues with peers, market conditions.
