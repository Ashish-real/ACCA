34.1
Why Audit Exists — The Agency Problem & Need for Assurance

To understand audit, you must first understand why it is needed. In a small owner-managed business, the owner runs the company and knows everything — no audit needed. But in a large corporation, ownership (shareholders) and management (directors/executives) are separated. This separation creates a fundamental problem called the Agency Problem.

"An audit is a quality control mechanism that society has developed to address the natural tension between those who prepare financial information and those who rely on it." — IAASB
⚠️
The Agency Problem — Why Audit is Needed
  • Principals = Shareholders — they own the company but don't run it
  • Agents = Directors/Management — they run the company on behalf of shareholders
  • Problem: Management prepares the financial statements that shareholders rely on to evaluate management's performance — obvious conflict of interest
  • Without audit: management could manipulate numbers to look better, hide losses, inflate profits, conceal fraud
  • Solution: An independent third party (auditor) examines the financial statements and gives an opinion on whether they are true and fair
  • Audit breaks the self-reporting loop — management reports, auditor verifies
Core Rationale
👥
Who Uses Audited Financial Statements?
  • Shareholders: assess returns, decide whether to hold or sell shares, evaluate management performance
  • Banks & lenders: decide whether to lend, at what rate, with what covenants
  • Investors (new): decide whether to invest in the company
  • Suppliers: assess whether the company is creditworthy enough to extend trade credit
  • Employees: assess job security, pension fund viability
  • Government / Tax authorities: verify tax compliance
  • Regulators: monitor financial health of regulated industries (banks, insurance)
Stakeholders
📉
What Happens Without Audit — Famous Scandals
  • Enron (2001): Hid billions of debt off-balance sheet, inflated profits. Auditor Arthur Andersen failed to detect/report. Enron collapsed — $74B shareholder loss. Led to Sarbanes-Oxley Act (SOX) in USA.
  • Satyam (2009, India): Founder Ramalinga Raju confessed to inflating cash and profits by ₹7,000Cr+ over years. Auditor PwC India failed. Led to major audit reforms in India.
  • Wirecard (2020, Germany): €1.9B of cash that didn't exist. EY failed to verify cash balances for years. Company collapsed, CEO arrested.
  • Lesson: Without independent, competent, and rigorous audit — investors are flying blind. Billions of losses and destroyed livelihoods follow failed audits.
Real World
🔑 The Expectation Gap — Critical ACCA Concept
The Expectation Gap is the difference between what the public/investors THINK an audit does and what it ACTUALLY does.

What people think: Auditors check every single transaction, guarantee that financial statements are 100% accurate, and will definitely detect all fraud.

What auditors actually do: Provide REASONABLE assurance (not absolute) that financial statements are free from MATERIAL misstatement. They use sampling (not 100% checking), and cannot guarantee detection of all fraud — especially well-concealed collusion.

Why this matters: When a company collapses after a clean audit opinion, the public blames auditors. Understanding the expectation gap helps explain why auditors are not automatically at fault — and also drives ongoing reforms to narrow the gap.
34.2
What is an Audit? — Definition, Objective & Scope

ISA 200 (Overall Objectives of the Independent Auditor) defines the audit objective clearly. Understanding this precisely is essential for ACCA AA and AAA exams.

Official Definition — ISA 200
Objective of an Audit
To enable the auditor to express an opinion on whether the financial statements are prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with an applicable financial reporting framework (e.g. IFRS or local GAAP).

Key phrase: "in all material respects" — not every single detail. Materiality limits the scope.
Level of Assurance
REASONABLE assurance — not absolute assurance.

Reasonable = high but not 100%. Absolute assurance is impossible due to: use of sampling, inherent limitations of internal controls, professional judgment involved in estimates, possibility of management override.
True and Fair View
Financial statements present fairly the financial position, performance, and cash flows of an entity.

"True" = factually accurate, supported by evidence. "Fair" = presented without bias, in accordance with GAAP/IFRS. This is the standard the auditor is assessing against.
Free From Material Misstatement
Whether due to fraud OR error.

Auditors must consider both. Error = unintentional mistake. Fraud = intentional misrepresentation. Auditors are not primarily fraud investigators but must maintain professional scepticism and respond when fraud risks are identified.
✅
What Audit IS
  • Independent examination of financial statements
  • Expression of opinion on truth and fairness
  • Reasonable (high) level of assurance
  • Testing on a sample basis (not 100%)
  • Assessment of whether accounting policies are appropriate and consistently applied
  • Assessment of management's key estimates and judgements
  • Evaluation of going concern — will the company survive 12+ months?
Audit IS
❌
What Audit is NOT
  • NOT a guarantee that financial statements are 100% correct
  • NOT a certificate that the company is financially healthy or won't fail
  • NOT designed to detect every fraud (especially well-concealed collusion)
  • NOT a full check of every single transaction
  • NOT the preparation of financial statements (that is management's job)
  • NOT an assessment of management quality or future prospects
  • NOT legal advice or assurance on tax compliance (unless specifically engaged)
Audit is NOT

💡 Internal vs External Audit — Key Differences (ACCA Favourite Topic)
Independence: External auditors are completely independent of the company. Internal auditors are employees or report internally — they aim for objectivity but are not legally independent.

Purpose: External audit gives assurance to EXTERNAL stakeholders (shareholders, creditors). Internal audit gives assurance to MANAGEMENT and the BOARD for internal improvement.

Scope: External audit is focused on financial statements. Internal audit covers a broader scope — operations, risk management, controls, efficiency, compliance.

Relationship: External auditors may RELY ON the work of internal auditors (after assessing their competence and objectivity) — but they cannot delegate their own responsibility. The external auditor remains solely responsible for the audit opinion.

The Audit Framework — ISAs & Regulatory Structure

Audits are conducted in accordance with International Standards on Auditing (ISAs) issued by the International Auditing and Assurance Standards Board (IAASB). These are the globally accepted framework for how audits must be planned, performed, and reported.

📋
Key ISAs — Must Know for ACCA
  • ISA 200: Overall Objectives — defines audit objective, reasonable assurance, professional scepticism
  • ISA 210: Agreeing Audit Engagement Terms — engagement letter
  • ISA 220: Quality Control — audit firm quality management
  • ISA 240: Fraud — auditor's responsibilities regarding fraud
  • ISA 260: Communication with Those Charged with Governance (Audit Committee)
  • ISA 300: Planning an Audit — audit strategy and audit plan
  • ISA 315: Identifying and Assessing Risks — risk assessment procedures
  • ISA 320: Materiality — setting performance materiality
  • ISA 330: Auditor's Responses to Assessed Risks — audit procedures
  • ISA 500: Audit Evidence — what constitutes sufficient appropriate evidence
  • ISA 520: Analytical Procedures
  • ISA 530: Audit Sampling
  • ISA 560: Subsequent Events — events after balance sheet date
  • ISA 570: Going Concern
  • ISA 700: Audit Report — forming and expressing opinion
  • ISA 705: Modifications to the Audit Opinion
  • ISA 706: Emphasis of Matter and Other Matter paragraphs
ISA Framework
🏛️
Regulatory Bodies — Global
  • IAASB: Issues ISAs — the global standard setter for auditing standards
  • IESBA: Issues the Code of Ethics for Professional Accountants — independence, objectivity, confidentiality
  • IFAC: International Federation of Accountants — umbrella body overseeing IAASB and IESBA
  • PCAOB (USA): Public Company Accounting Oversight Board — sets standards for audits of SEC-registered companies
  • FRC (UK): Financial Reporting Council — regulates UK auditors and sets UK audit standards (based on ISAs)
Global Regulators
🇮🇳
Audit Regulation in India
  • ICAI: Institute of Chartered Accountants of India — sets Standards on Auditing (SAs) which are based on ISAs. Only CAs can conduct statutory audits in India.
  • NFRA: National Financial Reporting Authority — independent regulator for audit quality of listed and large public interest entities. Can investigate and penalise auditors. Established 2018 post Satyam/IL&FS scandals.
  • Companies Act 2013: Governs statutory audit requirements — who must be audited, auditor appointment, rotation rules
  • SEBI: Regulates audit of listed companies — additional requirements for listed entities
  • Auditor Rotation: India mandates rotation of audit firms every 5 years for listed companies (to protect independence)

34.5
Audit Process — Step by Step (Planning to Report)
The Full Audit Lifecycle

The audit follows a structured process from the moment the firm accepts the engagement to issuing the final report. Understanding this process end-to-end is essential for ACCA AA and for any finance professional working alongside auditors.

STAGE 01
Client Acceptance & Engagement Letter (ISA 210)
Before accepting an audit, the firm must assess: Is the client ethical and honest? Is the firm competent to audit this type/size of business? Is the firm independent (no conflicts of interest)? Are resources available? If yes — send an Engagement Letter confirming: scope of audit, responsibilities of management vs auditor, basis of fee, access to records. This is a contract between firm and client. Existing clients: reassess each year — consider any change in circumstances.
STAGE 02
Audit Planning (ISA 300) — Strategy & Plan
Develop an Audit Strategy — high-level decisions: overall approach (controls-based or substantive), timing, staffing, use of experts. Then develop a detailed Audit Plan — nature, timing, and extent of specific audit procedures for each financial statement area. Planning is driven by: understanding the entity and its environment (ISA 315), risk assessment, and materiality. Good planning = efficient, effective audit.
STAGE 03
Risk Assessment (ISA 315) — Understanding the Entity
The auditor must understand: the entity (business model, industry, key products), its environment (economic conditions, competition, regulation), its internal controls (control environment, risk assessment process, control activities, monitoring). From this understanding, identify and assess risks of material misstatement at: (a) financial statement level and (b) assertion level for each account balance and transaction class. Risk assessment is the engine that drives everything else in the audit.
STAGE 04
Set Materiality (ISA 320)
Calculate overall materiality (typically 5% of PBT, 1% of revenue, or 0.5% of total assets — depending on the entity). Set performance materiality (usually 60–75% of overall materiality) — the threshold used for designing procedures. Set clearly trivial threshold (typically 3–5% of overall materiality) — items below this need not be investigated. Materiality is reassessed throughout the audit.
STAGE 05
Responding to Assessed Risks (ISA 330) — Audit Procedures
Design and perform audit procedures in response to assessed risks. Two types: Tests of Controls — test whether internal controls are operating effectively (if so, can reduce substantive testing). Substantive Procedures — directly test financial statement amounts and disclosures (analytical procedures + tests of detail). Higher risk = more extensive, more reliable procedures required. Obtain sufficient appropriate evidence to support conclusions.
STAGE 06
Completion Procedures
Review for subsequent events (ISA 560) — events after balance sheet date that need adjustment or disclosure. Assess going concern (ISA 570) — is there substantial doubt about the entity's ability to continue as a going concern for 12+ months? Obtain written representations from management (ISA 580) — management confirms key assertions in writing. Perform final analytical review — do the financial statements make sense overall? Evaluate misstatements found — are they material? Request management to correct material misstatements.
STAGE 07
Communication & Reporting (ISA 700/705/706/260)
Communicate findings to those charged with governance (Audit Committee / Board) — ISA 260: significant audit findings, difficulties encountered, key judgements. Issue the Audit Report — the formal public output of the audit. Report contains: opinion paragraph, basis for opinion, key audit matters (for listed entities), going concern assessment, other information. If issues found: modify the opinion (qualified, adverse, or disclaimer — covered in Section 34.13).
34.6
Materiality — The Most Important Concept in Audit
ISA 320

Materiality is the threshold above which misstatements would influence the decisions of users of financial statements. It is the single most important concept in audit — it defines the audit's scope, drives sample sizes, determines what to investigate, and shapes the final opinion.

Materiality — Benchmarks & Calculation
Overall Materiality — Common Benchmarks
5% of Profit Before Tax (PBT) — most common for profitable entities
1% of Revenue — for low-margin businesses or where PBT is volatile
0.5–1% of Total Assets — for banks, holding companies, or asset-heavy entities
1–2% of Equity — for entities where equity is the key metric
Choice of benchmark depends on what users focus on most. Professional judgement required.
Performance Materiality
Typically 60–75% of Overall Materiality
Lower threshold used when designing procedures — so that the AGGREGATE of uncorrected misstatements does not exceed overall materiality. Acts as a buffer. If multiple small errors are found, their total might still be material.
Clearly Trivial Threshold
Typically 3–5% of Overall Materiality
Misstatements below this level are clearly trivial and need not be investigated or accumulated. They cannot — even in aggregate — be material.

Worked Example — Setting Materiality for XYZ Ltd

📊 Materiality Calculation — XYZ Manufacturing Ltd (FY25)

Financial Data: Revenue = ₹500Cr | PBT = ₹45Cr | Total Assets = ₹380Cr | Equity = ₹210Cr

Step 1 — Choose Benchmark: XYZ is a profitable manufacturing company. Users primarily focus on profitability. Choose PBT as benchmark.

Step 2 — Overall Materiality: 5% × ₹45Cr = ₹2.25Cr
Cross-check: 1% of Revenue = ₹5Cr (higher — PBT benchmark seems more conservative and appropriate here)

Step 3 — Performance Materiality: 70% × ₹2.25Cr = ₹1.58Cr
This is the threshold used when testing individual account balances and transactions.

Step 4 — Clearly Trivial: 5% × ₹2.25Cr = ₹11.25L (₹1.125Cr)
Misstatements below ₹1.125Cr need not be accumulated or investigated.

Practical impact: When auditing inventory (say ₹80Cr on balance sheet), auditor designs procedures to detect misstatements above ₹1.58Cr. If they find ₹50L error → below clearly trivial, ignore. If ₹2Cr error → above overall materiality → must be corrected or qualified opinion.
⚠️ Qualitative Materiality — Not Just About Numbers
Sometimes a small misstatement is material for NON-FINANCIAL reasons:

Examples of qualitatively material items:
→ A small fraud by a director — even if immaterial in amount, it signals governance failure and breaches laws
→ A breach of a debt covenant — even a small misstatement that causes a covenant breach is material
→ A misstatement that converts a reported profit into a loss (or vice versa)
→ Transactions with related parties — even small amounts can be qualitatively significant due to conflict of interest
→ Unlawful transactions — even if small, non-compliance with law is always qualitatively material

ACCA Tip: Always consider BOTH quantitative AND qualitative aspects of materiality in exam answers.
34.7
Audit Risk — Inherent, Control & Detection Risk
ISA 315 & ISA 200

Audit Risk is the risk that the auditor expresses an inappropriate opinion — gives a clean opinion when financial statements are materially misstated. Auditors must keep this risk to an acceptably low level. The Audit Risk Model breaks this into three components.

The Audit Risk Model
Audit Risk (AR)
AR = Inherent Risk (IR) × Control Risk (CR) × Detection Risk (DR)
Auditor aims to keep AR at an acceptably low level. IR and CR are assessed — Detection Risk is manipulated by adjusting the nature/timing/extent of audit procedures.
Inherent Risk (IR)
The susceptibility of an assertion to material misstatement BEFORE considering any controls.
High IR examples: complex accounting estimates (fair value, provisions), subjective judgements, unusual transactions, high-volume/high-value transactions, industries prone to fraud (cash-heavy businesses, financial services), businesses under financial pressure.
Control Risk (CR)
The risk that a material misstatement will NOT be prevented or detected by the entity's internal controls.
High CR: weak internal controls, override of controls by management, no segregation of duties, inadequate IT controls. Low CR: strong, effective controls — allows auditor to reduce substantive testing.
Detection Risk (DR)
The risk that the auditor's OWN PROCEDURES will fail to detect a material misstatement.
This is what the AUDITOR CONTROLS. If IR and CR are high → auditor must REDUCE DR by doing more/better procedures. If IR and CR are low → can accept higher DR (do less testing). DR can never be zero — sampling means some items are never examined.
📊
Audit Risk — How to Apply in Practice
  • High IR + High CR → Very Low DR needed → Extensive, rigorous procedures
  • Low IR + Low CR → Higher DR acceptable → Less testing, more reliance on controls
  • Auditor cannot control IR or CR — only assesses them
  • Auditor CONTROLS Detection Risk by choosing: nature (what procedures), timing (when performed), extent (sample size) of audit procedures
  • More reliable, larger-sample, more extensive procedures = lower Detection Risk
Risk Response
⚠️
Significant Risks — Special Treatment
  • Some risks are so significant they require special audit attention — called Significant Risks
  • Examples: revenue recognition (fraud risk — ISA 240 presumes this is always significant), management estimates, related party transactions, unusual/complex transactions
  • For Significant Risks: auditor MUST obtain understanding of relevant controls AND perform substantive procedures — cannot rely on controls alone
  • ACCA exam: always identify and justify significant risks separately from other risks
ISA 315
🔍
Fraud Risk — ISA 240
  • Two types: Fraudulent Financial Reporting (management manipulates statements) and Misappropriation of Assets (theft of company assets)
  • Fraud Triangle: Incentive/Pressure + Opportunity + Rationalisation — all three present → high fraud risk
  • ISA 240: Auditor must ALWAYS presume revenue recognition is a fraud risk (significant risk)
  • Auditor must maintain professional scepticism — do not accept management explanations without corroboration
  • If fraud suspected: report to management (unless they are involved), consider reporting to authorities, consider resigning
ISA 240
34.8
Audit Evidence — Types, Sources & Sufficiency
ISA 500

Audit evidence is ALL information used by the auditor to reach their conclusions. The auditor must obtain evidence that is sufficient (enough of it) and appropriate (relevant and reliable). These two concepts drive every evidence decision.

📦
Sufficiency of Evidence
  • Quantity of evidence — how much is enough?
  • Affected by: assessed risk (higher risk → more evidence needed), quality of evidence (better quality → less quantity needed), materiality of the item
  • Sample size drives sufficiency — larger samples = more sufficient
  • No fixed formula — professional judgement determines when "enough" is enough
  • Key principle: more risk = need more evidence
How Much?
✅
Appropriateness of Evidence — Reliability Hierarchy
  • Most reliable: Auditor's own direct observation and recalculation
  • Highly reliable: External confirmations (bank letters, debtor circularisation, legal letters)
  • Reliable: Documents obtained from third parties (supplier invoices, bank statements)
  • Moderately reliable: Documents created internally but with strong controls
  • Least reliable: Verbal representations from management (oral evidence, unwritten)
  • General rule: external > internal; auditor-generated > client-provided; original > copy
Quality of Evidence
🎯
Financial Statement Assertions — What Evidence Must Prove
  • Existence/Occurrence: Assets exist; transactions occurred
  • Completeness: All assets and transactions have been recorded — nothing omitted
  • Valuation/Accuracy: Amounts are correctly valued or measured
  • Rights & Obligations: Company owns the assets; owes the liabilities
  • Cut-off: Transactions recorded in the correct period
  • Presentation & Disclosure: Items correctly classified and disclosed in financial statements
  • Every audit procedure must be designed to address specific assertion(s)
Assertions
34.9
Audit Procedures — The Toolkit (AEIOU)

Audit procedures are the specific actions auditors take to obtain evidence. A useful mnemonic is AEIOU (or variations) — covering the main categories. In ACCA exams, you must not only list procedures but describe WHAT you will do and WHAT assertion it addresses.

Audit Procedures — Full Toolkit
A — Analytical Procedures (ISA 520)
Compare financial data to: prior year, budget, industry averages, expectations.
Calculate ratios and investigate unusual fluctuations.
Example: GP margin dropped from 35% to 22% → investigate reason → possible misstatement of revenue or cost of sales.
E — Enquiry
Ask questions of management, staff, and third parties.
Least reliable on its own — must be corroborated by other evidence.
Example: Ask the warehouse manager about inventory obsolescence. Do not accept the answer alone — also physically inspect and check slow-moving items.
I — Inspection (Vouching & Tracing)
Examine documents, records, physical assets.
Vouching: start from recorded entry → find supporting document (tests EXISTENCE).
Tracing: start from source document → check it is recorded (tests COMPLETENESS).
Example: Select 50 sales invoices from the revenue ledger → trace to dispatch notes and customer orders (existence). Select dispatch notes → trace to sales ledger (completeness).
O — Observation
Watch a process being performed by client staff.
Limitation: behaviour may change when being observed.
Example: Attend year-end inventory count — observe counting procedures, check for damaged goods, test counts independently. Observe controls operating in real time.
C — Confirmation (External)
Obtain evidence directly from third parties — highly reliable.
Examples: Bank confirmation letter (confirms balances, loans, contingent liabilities). Debtor circularisation (confirms receivable balances). Lawyer letters (confirms litigation, contingencies). Supplier statements (confirms payable balances).
R — Recalculation & Reperformance
Recalculate: check mathematical accuracy of client's calculations.
Reperformance: independently perform a procedure the client performs.
Example: Recalculate depreciation charges, interest accruals, tax provisions, foreign currency translations. Reperform bank reconciliation independently.
💡 ACCA Exam Tip — How to Write Audit Procedures for Maximum Marks
Weak answer: "Inspect invoices to verify revenue." (vague, no marks awarded)

Strong answer: "Select a sample of sales invoices recorded in the revenue ledger and agree the details (amount, date, customer) back to the original customer purchase order and the goods despatch note, to verify that revenue has been recorded for genuine sales transactions that have actually been delivered (addressing existence and occurrence assertions)."

Formula for strong audit procedure: HOW (what exactly you do) + WHAT (specific document/source) + WHY (what assertion it addresses and what misstatement it would detect).

Always be specific about the DIRECTION of testing: Vouching (ledger → document) tests existence. Tracing (document → ledger) tests completeness.
34.10
Internal Controls & Their Role in Audit

Internal controls are processes designed by management to provide reasonable assurance about achieving objectives — reliable financial reporting, operational efficiency, and regulatory compliance. Auditors evaluate internal controls to assess control risk and decide how much substantive testing is needed.

🏗️
COSO Framework — 5 Components of Internal Control
  • 1. Control Environment: The foundation — tone at the top, management integrity, organisational structure, ethics policies. If the control environment is weak, all other controls are undermined.
  • 2. Risk Assessment: Management's own process to identify and respond to risks that could prevent objectives being achieved
  • 3. Control Activities: The actual controls — authorisations, segregation of duties, reconciliations, physical safeguards, IT controls
  • 4. Information & Communication: Systems to identify, capture, and communicate information needed to run controls
  • 5. Monitoring: Management's ongoing assessment of whether controls are operating effectively — internal audit plays a key role here
COSO
🔒
Key Control Activities — Audit Focus
  • Segregation of Duties: Different people authorise, record, and handle assets — prevents one person committing and concealing fraud. E.g. person who raises purchase orders ≠ person who approves payment
  • Authorisation: All significant transactions require approval from appropriate level of management
  • Reconciliations: Bank reconciliations, supplier statement reconciliations — regularly performed and reviewed by independent person
  • Physical Controls: Safeguarding assets — locked storage, access controls, security cameras, inventory counts
  • IT General Controls: Access controls, password policies, change management, backup and recovery
  • Exception Reporting: System flags unusual transactions for management review
Controls in Practice
⚠️
Limitations of Internal Controls
  • Management Override: Senior management can override controls — most dangerous limitation (Enron, Satyam)
  • Collusion: Two or more people working together to circumvent segregation of duties
  • Human Error: Even well-designed controls can fail due to human mistake
  • Cost vs Benefit: Controls must be proportionate to risk — some controls too expensive to implement for small risks
  • System Changes: Controls designed for old systems may not work for new ones
  • Conclusion: Internal controls provide REASONABLE not ABSOLUTE assurance — hence audit cannot rely solely on them
Limitations
34.11
Audit Sampling — How Auditors Test Without Checking Everything
ISA 530

It is impractical (and unnecessary) to check every single transaction. Audit sampling applies procedures to less than 100% of a population so that all sampling units have a chance of selection, and the results are used to draw conclusions about the whole population.

Sampling MethodHow It WorksBest Used ForRisk
Random SamplingEvery item has an equal probability of selection — use random number generatorHomogeneous populations where all items are similar in nature and valueMay miss high-value items — no guarantee of representativeness for value
Systematic SamplingSelect every nth item (e.g. every 10th invoice). Starting point chosen randomly.Large, sequential populations (numbered invoices, purchase orders)If there is a pattern in the population that matches the interval, sample is biased
Monetary Unit Sampling (MUS)Every ₹ of value has an equal chance of selection — high-value items more likely to be selectedBalance testing where value matters — receivables, payables, inventoryIgnores small-value items (which may still be numerous)
Haphazard SamplingAuditor selects items without any structured method — but avoids conscious biasOnly when other methods impractical — should not be used normallyHigh risk of unconscious bias — human nature tends to select "easy" items
Stratified SamplingDivide population into strata (e.g. by value: >₹10L, ₹1–10L, <₹1L) and sample each separatelyHeterogeneous populations where risk differs by stratum — e.g. test all items above materiality 100%, sample lower itemsMust correctly identify strata — misclassification corrupts results
⚠️ Sampling Risk vs Non-Sampling Risk
Sampling Risk: The risk that the auditor's conclusion based on a sample differs from the conclusion that would be reached if the ENTIRE population were tested. This is inherent in sampling — managed by choosing appropriate sample size and method.

Non-Sampling Risk: Risks that have NOTHING to do with sampling — e.g. choosing the wrong audit procedure, misinterpreting evidence, failing to recognise a misstatement. Addressed through training, supervision, and quality control — not by increasing sample size.

Audit Reports — Types & When Each Is Used
ISA 700, 705, 706

The audit report is the final, public output of the audit. It communicates the auditor's opinion to shareholders and other stakeholders. Understanding the different types of opinions and when each is issued is a key ACCA topic.

✅
Unmodified Opinion ("Clean" Opinion)
  • Issued when: financial statements give a true and fair view in all material respects, in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework
  • No material misstatements found, sufficient appropriate evidence obtained
  • This is what most companies receive — the "green light"
  • Standard wording: "In our opinion, the financial statements give a true and fair view..."
  • Does NOT mean the company is healthy, profitable, or without risk — only that the statements are fairly presented
ISA 700
⚠️
Qualified Opinion — "Except For"
  • Issued when: there IS a material misstatement or scope limitation, BUT it is NOT pervasive (does not affect the whole of the financial statements)
  • The opinion is clean EXCEPT FOR the specific matter described
  • Wording: "In our opinion, except for the effects of the matter described in the Basis for Qualified Opinion paragraph, the financial statements give a true and fair view..."
  • Example: Inventory is overstated by ₹15Cr (material but isolated) — qualified on inventory, clean on everything else
Modified — ISA 705
🚫
Adverse Opinion
  • Issued when: there IS a material misstatement AND it is PERVASIVE — affects so much of the financial statements that the overall picture is misleading
  • The most severe negative opinion — essentially says the financial statements are WRONG
  • Wording: "In our opinion, because of the significance of the matter described... the financial statements do NOT give a true and fair view..."
  • Example: Revenue is fundamentally misstated, affecting almost every line in the financial statements
  • Extremely rare — when it occurs, it is major news
Modified — ISA 705
❓
Disclaimer of Opinion
  • Issued when: there is a SCOPE LIMITATION (auditor could not obtain sufficient evidence) AND the possible effects are PERVASIVE
  • Auditor says: "We cannot form an opinion" — does not express any opinion at all
  • Example: Management refuses to provide access to key records, or the company was set up just before year end with no financial history, or going concern uncertainty is so severe evidence cannot be obtained
  • More serious than qualified opinion for scope — because the unknown effects could be pervasive
Modified — ISA 705
📌
Emphasis of Matter Paragraph (EOM)
  • Added to an UNMODIFIED opinion to draw users' attention to a matter that is already properly disclosed in the financial statements
  • Does not modify the opinion — the financial statements are still true and fair
  • Common uses: significant going concern uncertainty (disclosed but not yet fatal), major litigation disclosed in notes, fundamental uncertainty
  • Example: Company has disclosed in notes that it faces a ₹500Cr lawsuit that could be existential — auditor includes EOM to highlight this
ISA 706
📋
Key Audit Matters (KAMs)
  • Required for listed entities — ISA 701
  • Matters that were most significant in the audit — communicated to give users insight into the audit process
  • Typically: complex estimates (goodwill impairment, provisions), significant judgements, areas of high inherent risk (revenue recognition, going concern)
  • Each KAM describes: why it was significant + how the auditor addressed it
  • Introduced to narrow the expectation gap — helps users understand what the auditor actually focused on
  • Do NOT modify the opinion — they are informational
ISA 701
🔑 Modified Opinions — Decision Framework
Is there a material misstatement OR scope limitation?

If YES → Is it PERVASIVE?

Material Misstatement + NOT Pervasive → QUALIFIED OPINION ("except for")
Material Misstatement + PERVASIVE → ADVERSE OPINION ("do not give a true and fair view")
Scope Limitation + NOT Pervasive → QUALIFIED OPINION ("except for possible effects")
Scope Limitation + PERVASIVE → DISCLAIMER OF OPINION ("we do not express an opinion")

Pervasive = affects multiple elements, represents a substantial portion of the statements, or fundamental disclosures to users.
34.14
Auditor Independence & Ethics

Independence is the cornerstone of auditing. An audit opinion has value only if the auditor is independent — genuinely free from bias and conflicts of interest. IESBA's Code of Ethics for Professional Accountants governs auditor independence.

🧠
Two Types of Independence
  • Independence of Mind: The actual state of being free from bias — the auditor genuinely forms their own objective view without being influenced by management
  • Independence in Appearance: A reasonable, informed third party would conclude that the auditor is independent — the PERCEPTION matters as much as reality
  • Both are required. An auditor can be independent in mind but appear conflicted (e.g. if they own shares in the client) — the appearance destroys credibility
  • IESBA Code: "An auditor shall be independent both in mind and in appearance"
Core Principle
⚠️
Threats to Independence — FASSMO
  • F — Financial Interest: Auditor owns shares in client. Loans between auditor and client.
  • A — Advocacy: Auditor promotes client's position (e.g. acting as legal advocate in dispute)
  • S — Self-Review: Auditor reviews their own prior work (e.g. auditor prepared the accounts they now audit)
  • S — Self-Interest: Auditor has personal financial stake in client outcome (unpaid fees, contingent fees)
  • M — Management/Familiarity: Long relationship with client — too close, stops questioning management
  • O — Intimidation: Client threatens to remove auditor unless they agree with management's position
FASSMO Threats
🛡️
Safeguards Against Threats
  • Audit partner rotation — must change lead partner every 5–7 years (India: firm must rotate every 5 years for listed cos)
  • Second partner review — independent partner reviews audit work and opinion
  • Quality control procedures — firm-wide independence checks, ethics policies
  • Separate teams for audit and non-audit services to same client
  • Cooling-off periods — former auditors cannot join client in senior roles immediately after audit
  • Fee cap — fees from one client should not dominate total firm revenue (creates economic dependency)
  • Prohibition on certain non-audit services — e.g. preparing accounts of the company you audit
Safeguards
💡 Professional Scepticism — The Auditor's Mindset (ISA 200)
Professional scepticism is an attitude that includes a questioning mind, being alert to conditions which may indicate possible misstatement due to error or fraud, and a critical assessment of audit evidence.

What it means in practice: Do not simply accept management's explanations — corroborate them. Be alert when evidence seems too perfect or explanations seem too convenient. Challenge assumptions behind estimates. Consider the possibility that management is trying to deceive you.

Professional Judgement — applying relevant training, knowledge, and experience to making decisions during the audit. Different from scepticism: judgement is about making decisions; scepticism is about questioning and challenging.

Why both matter: The biggest audit failures in history (Enron, Satyam, Wirecard) involved auditors who applied too little scepticism and too much trust in management.
34.15
Audit in India — Regulatory Context
🇮🇳
ICAI — Institute of Chartered Accountants of India
  • Statutory body under The Chartered Accountants Act 1949 — only ICAI members (CAs) can conduct statutory audits in India
  • Issues Standards on Auditing (SAs) — India's equivalent of ISAs, largely converged
  • Issues Guidance Notes on specific areas (e.g. audit of banks, NBFCs, stock brokers)
  • Conducts quality reviews of audit firms through its Quality Review Board
  • Has disciplinary authority over members who violate professional standards
  • CA Final exams are among the toughest professional exams globally — 10–15% pass rates
ICAI
🏛️
NFRA — National Financial Reporting Authority
  • Established 2018 under Companies Act 2013 — independent of ICAI
  • Oversees audit quality of listed companies, large unlisted public companies, banks, insurance companies
  • Can investigate auditors and impose penalties — including debarment from practice
  • Created after IL&FS crisis (2018) and Satyam scandal exposed weaknesses in self-regulation by ICAI
  • Sets Accounting Standards for its covered entities (converged with IFRS as Ind AS)
  • Reports annually on audit quality findings — similar to PCAOB in USA
NFRA
📋
Companies Act 2013 — Key Audit Provisions
  • Mandatory Audit: All companies must have their financial statements audited by a qualified CA
  • Auditor Appointment: Appointed by shareholders at AGM on recommendation of Board/Audit Committee
  • Firm Rotation: Listed companies must rotate audit firms every 5 years (10 years with shareholder approval) — to protect independence
  • Restrictions on Non-Audit Services: Auditor cannot provide certain services to audit client (e.g. accounting, internal audit, management consulting) — prevents self-review threat
  • Reporting on Fraud: If auditor suspects fraud of ₹1Cr+ → must report to Central Government within 60 days
  • Auditor's Report: Must comply with Companies (Audit & Auditors) Rules 2014 — specific reporting requirements
Companies Act
📉
Major Indian Audit Failures — Lessons
  • Satyam (2009): ₹7,136Cr fraud — fake cash balances, inflated revenues. PwC India was statutory auditor — missed for years. Led to criminal charges against auditors, NFRA creation.
  • IL&FS (2018): ₹91,000Cr debt default hidden through complex SPV structures. Multiple auditors missed the systemic risk. Triggered NFRA establishment.
  • DHFL (2019): Massive loan diversion fraud. Auditors criticised for not detecting diversion of funds to promoter-linked entities.
  • Byju's (2023): Auditor Deloitte resigned — citing inability to obtain sufficient evidence. Later BBBI (parent) faced massive write-downs.
  • Common theme: Insufficient professional scepticism, over-reliance on management representations, pressure from large/important clients.

Key Takeaways, Common Mistakes, Flashcards, Interview Q&A & Action Steps

Part A — 10 Core Takeaways

1️⃣
Why Audit Exists
  • Agency problem — management prepares statements that shareholders rely on
  • Audit breaks the self-reporting loop — independent third party verifies
  • Without audit: Enron, Satyam, Wirecard-type disasters happen repeatedly
2️⃣
Audit = Reasonable, Not Absolute Assurance
  • Not 100% checking — uses sampling
  • Cannot guarantee detection of all fraud
  • Cannot guarantee company won't fail
  • The Expectation Gap is always relevant to discuss
3️⃣
Audit Risk Model — AR = IR × CR × DR
  • IR and CR assessed — can't control them
  • DR is what the auditor controls via procedures
  • Higher IR/CR → lower DR needed → more extensive procedures
4️⃣
Materiality — Quantitative AND Qualitative
  • Set at ~5% PBT or 1% revenue or 0.5% assets
  • Performance materiality = 60–75% of overall
  • Small amounts can still be material — director fraud, covenant breach
5️⃣
Assertions — The Why Behind Every Procedure
  • Existence, Completeness, Valuation, Rights, Cut-off, Presentation
  • Every audit procedure must target specific assertions
  • Direction matters: vouching (existence) vs tracing (completeness)
6️⃣
Evidence Reliability Hierarchy
  • Auditor-generated > external > internal client documents
  • External confirmations = gold standard (bank letters, debtor circularisation)
  • Management oral explanations alone = least reliable — always corroborate
7️⃣
Audit Reports — 4 Types of Opinion
  • Unmodified = clean (true and fair)
  • Qualified = material issue but not pervasive ("except for")
  • Adverse = material AND pervasive misstatement
  • Disclaimer = pervasive scope limitation (cannot form opinion)
8️⃣
Independence — Mind AND Appearance
  • FASSMO threats: Financial, Advocacy, Self-review, Self-interest, Management familiarity, Over-familiarity, Intimidation
  • Safeguards: rotation, second partner review, prohibitions on non-audit services
  • Independence is the foundation — without it the opinion is worthless
9️⃣
Professional Scepticism — Never Forget This
  • Questioning mind — never blindly accept management
  • Alert to fraud indicators — pressure, opportunity, rationalisation
  • Biggest audit failures: too much trust, too little scepticism
🔟
India — ICAI + NFRA + Companies Act
  • Only CAs can conduct statutory audit in India
  • NFRA oversees audit quality of listed and large public interest entities
  • Firm rotation every 5 years for listed companies
  • Fraud above ₹1Cr must be reported to Central Government

Part B — Common Exam Mistakes

❌ MISTAKE 1
Writing Vague Audit Procedures
"Check invoices" or "Review records" earns zero marks. Always specify: which documents, in which direction, for which assertion, to detect which misstatement. Be specific, be detailed, be directional.
❌ MISTAKE 2
Confusing Inherent Risk with Control Risk
Inherent risk = susceptibility BEFORE controls. Control risk = risk that controls FAIL to catch the error. Two completely different concepts. IR is about the nature of the item; CR is about the quality of controls over it.
❌ MISTAKE 3
Saying Audit Guarantees Accuracy or Fraud Detection
Audit provides REASONABLE (not absolute) assurance. It cannot guarantee detection of all fraud — especially well-concealed collusion or management override. Always qualify this in exam answers.
❌ MISTAKE 4
Ignoring Qualitative Materiality
Students focus only on numbers. Exams frequently give scenarios with small amounts that are qualitatively material — director transactions, covenant breaches, fraud indicators. Always consider both dimensions of materiality.
❌ MISTAKE 5
Selecting Wrong Opinion Type
Common error: saying "adverse opinion" when the issue is a scope limitation (correct answer: disclaimer). Or saying "disclaimer" when there is a material misstatement (correct: qualified or adverse depending on pervasiveness). Use the decision tree: misstatement or scope limitation? → pervasive or not?

Part D — Interview Questions & Model Answers

QuestionModel Answer
"What is the difference between internal and external audit?"External audit: independent of the company, provides assurance to external stakeholders (shareholders) on truth and fairness of financial statements — statutory requirement. Internal audit: employees or outsourced, reports to board/audit committee, covers broader scope (risk, operations, controls), aimed at improving internal processes. External auditors may use internal audit work but cannot delegate responsibility for their opinion.
"What is materiality and why does it matter in audit?"Materiality is the threshold above which a misstatement would influence the decisions of users of financial statements. It limits audit scope — auditors focus effort where misstatements would matter. Set quantitatively (e.g. 5% of PBT) and qualitatively (fraud, covenant breach). Performance materiality (60–75%) is used when designing procedures to ensure aggregate errors don't exceed overall materiality.
"Walk me through the audit process from start to finish."Client acceptance and engagement letter → Planning (audit strategy, risk assessment, materiality) → Risk assessment (understand entity, identify risks at FS and assertion level) → Respond to risks (tests of controls and substantive procedures) → Completion (subsequent events, going concern, written representations, final analytical review) → Report (audit opinion — unmodified or modified as appropriate, communication to audit committee).
"What is professional scepticism and give me an example?"Professional scepticism is a questioning mindset — being alert to conditions that may indicate error or fraud, and critically evaluating audit evidence without blindly accepting management's explanations. Example: management says a debtor who hasn't paid in 18 months is "fine and will pay soon." A sceptical auditor would not accept this and would request post-year-end bank statements from the debtor, or a written confirmation, or consider whether full provision is required.
"Why might an auditor give a qualified opinion?"A qualified opinion is issued when there is a material misstatement OR a scope limitation (the auditor could not obtain sufficient appropriate evidence), but the issue is NOT pervasive — it doesn't affect the whole of the financial statements. Example: inventory is overstated by ₹10Cr (material) but this only affects inventory and cost of sales — all other areas are fine. The opinion would be clean "except for" the inventory misstatement.
