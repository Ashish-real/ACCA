35.1
What Are Internal Controls & Why They Matter

Internal controls are the policies, procedures, systems, and processes put in place by an organisation's management and board to provide reasonable assurance that objectives are being achieved. They are the invisible infrastructure that keeps an organisation from falling into chaos, fraud, and error.

"Internal control is a process, effected by an entity's board of directors, management, and other personnel, designed to provide reasonable assurance regarding the achievement of objectives." — COSO Framework
🏗️
Why Internal Controls Exist
  • Organisations are too large for any one person to supervise everything — controls create systematic oversight
  • People make mistakes — controls catch errors before they become problems
  • Some people are dishonest — controls prevent and detect fraud and theft
  • Laws and regulations must be followed — controls ensure compliance
  • Management needs reliable information to make decisions — controls ensure data quality
  • Investors and lenders need to trust financial statements — controls provide that foundation of trust
Foundation
💼
Who is Responsible for Internal Controls?
  • Board of Directors: Ultimate responsibility — set the tone, oversight of control environment
  • Audit Committee: Specific oversight of financial reporting controls and internal audit function
  • Management (CEO, CFO): Design, implement, and maintain effective controls
  • All Employees: Follow controls in their daily work — controls only work if people actually use them
  • Internal Audit: Independent assurance that controls are working — reports to Audit Committee
  • External Auditor: Evaluates controls for audit purposes — not responsible for designing or maintaining them
Responsibility
🌍
Real Cost of Poor Internal Controls
  • ACFE (Association of Certified Fraud Examiners) estimates organisations lose ~5% of annual revenue to fraud — most due to weak controls
  • Average fraud loss per case: $1.7 million (global median)
  • Regulatory fines for control failures: banks fined billions globally for AML (Anti-Money Laundering) control failures
  • Reputational damage: once trust is lost, it takes years to rebuild — customer and investor flight
  • In India: IL&FS (₹91,000Cr), DHFL, PMC Bank — all involved catastrophic control failures
Cost of Failure
35.2
Objectives of Internal Controls — The Three Pillars

COSO defines three categories of objectives that internal controls are designed to achieve. Every control in an organisation exists to support one or more of these objectives.

📊
1. Financial Reporting Reliability
  • Ensure financial statements are accurate, complete, and prepared in accordance with IFRS/Ind AS
  • Prevent and detect errors and fraud in financial data
  • Ensure transactions are properly authorised, recorded, and reported
  • Examples: reconciliations, journal entry controls, period-end close controls, financial statement review
  • This is the primary focus of external auditors — they assess controls over financial reporting
  • ACCA connection: most audit questions on internal controls relate to this objective
Financial Reporting
⚙️
2. Operational Effectiveness & Efficiency
  • Ensure resources (people, money, assets) are used effectively and efficiently
  • Prevent waste, inefficiency, and operational errors
  • Examples: procurement controls (3 quotes before purchase), inventory management controls, HR controls (proper onboarding/offboarding), production quality controls
  • This is the primary focus of internal audit — operational audits assess efficiency
  • Not directly tested by external auditor — but weak operational controls increase financial misstatement risk
Operations
⚖️
3. Compliance with Laws & Regulations
  • Ensure the organisation complies with applicable laws, regulations, and internal policies
  • Prevent regulatory violations that could result in fines, sanctions, or licence revocation
  • Examples: SEBI compliance controls for listed companies, RBI compliance for banks, GST compliance controls, environmental compliance, data privacy (PDPB India)
  • Compliance failures can be existential — e.g. bank licence revoked due to AML failures
  • Compliance audit is a separate engagement type specifically testing this objective
Compliance
🔑 Reasonable Assurance — Not Absolute
Internal controls provide REASONABLE assurance — not absolute certainty — that objectives will be achieved. This is because:

1. Cost-Benefit: The cost of implementing a control must be proportionate to the risk it mitigates. Perfect controls for every risk would be prohibitively expensive.
2. Human Error: Even the best-designed controls can fail if people make mistakes.
3. Collusion: Two or more people working together can circumvent segregation of duties.
4. Management Override: Senior management can bypass controls they themselves designed.
5. Changing Conditions: Controls designed for yesterday's environment may not work for today's.

This is the same "reasonable assurance" concept used in audit — both build on the same foundation.
35.3
COSO Framework — The Global Standard for Internal Controls
Most Important Framework

The Committee of Sponsoring Organizations (COSO) framework is the globally accepted standard for internal control. First published in 1992 (updated 2013), it defines internal control as consisting of five interrelated components, each of which must be present and functioning for controls to be effective.

COMPONENT 1
Control Environment — The Foundation of Everything
The control environment is the "tone at the top" — the set of standards, processes, and structures that provide the basis for carrying out internal control across the organisation. It includes: management's commitment to integrity and ethical values, board independence and oversight effectiveness, organisational structure and assignment of authority, HR policies (hiring, training, performance evaluation), commitment to competence.

Why it's #1: If the control environment is weak — management is dishonest, ethics are ignored, "win at any cost" culture prevails — all other controls become ineffective. You can have the best procedures on paper, but if the culture is rotten, people won't follow them. Enron had extensive written controls — but the culture glorified rule-breaking.

ACCA/Audit connection: Auditors spend significant time assessing the control environment — it directly affects their overall risk assessment and the nature of the entire audit.
COMPONENT 2
Risk Assessment — Identifying What Could Go Wrong
Management must identify and analyse risks that could prevent the organisation from achieving its objectives. This involves: defining objectives clearly enough to identify and assess risks, identifying risks across the entire entity (strategic, operational, financial, compliance), analysing likelihood and impact of each risk, considering how to respond (accept, avoid, reduce, share the risk), and specifically considering the risk of fraud.

Key concept — Fraud Risk Assessment: Management must specifically consider risks of fraudulent financial reporting and misappropriation of assets — and design controls to address them. This is required under ISA 240 and COSO 2013.

Dynamic nature: Risk assessment is not a one-time exercise. Risks change as the business environment changes — new regulations, new technology, new competitors, economic changes all create new risks that require new controls.
COMPONENT 3
Control Activities — The Actual Controls
Control activities are the actions established through policies and procedures that help ensure management's directives to mitigate risks to achievement of objectives are carried out. These are the specific, tangible controls people think of when they hear "internal controls" — covered in detail in Section 35.5.

Types include: authorisation controls, segregation of duties, physical controls, reconciliations, performance reviews, IT application controls, and many others. Control activities occur at all levels and functions of the organisation — from board level down to individual transactions.
COMPONENT 4
Information & Communication — The Nervous System
For controls to work, people need accurate, timely information — and they need to be able to communicate effectively. This includes: information systems that capture and process financial and operational data reliably, communication flowing downward (policies, procedures, expectations), upward (problems, fraud concerns, control weaknesses), and laterally (between departments).

Whistleblower mechanisms: Critical element — employees must have safe, confidential channels to report suspected fraud or control failures without fear of retaliation. In India, Companies Act 2013 requires listed companies to have a vigil mechanism (whistleblower policy).

External communication: Communication with regulators, auditors, customers, and suppliers must also be controlled — false external communications are themselves a fraud risk.
COMPONENT 5
Monitoring — Checking That Controls Are Working
Controls that were effective last year may not be effective today — people leave, processes change, technology evolves. Monitoring involves ongoing assessments and periodic separate evaluations to determine whether each component of internal control is present and functioning.

Ongoing monitoring: Built into normal operations — supervisory reviews, management reporting, automated exception alerts, customer complaints analysis, reconciliation reviews.

Separate evaluations: Internal audit reviews, external audit, regulatory examinations, management self-assessments (control self-assessments or CSAs).

Reporting deficiencies: Control deficiencies must be reported to appropriate levels of management — significant deficiencies and material weaknesses must reach the audit committee and board. External auditors must report significant deficiencies found during audit to those charged with governance (ISA 265).
35.4
Types of Controls — Preventive, Detective, Corrective

Controls can be classified by WHEN they act — before, during, or after an event. A robust control system uses all three types in combination.

🛡️
Preventive Controls — Stop It Before It Happens
  • Designed to PREVENT errors or fraud from occurring in the first place
  • Most cost-effective — catching a problem before it occurs is always cheaper than fixing it after
  • Examples:
  • Segregation of duties — prevents one person committing and concealing fraud
  • Authorisation requirements — requires approval before transaction proceeds
  • Access controls — prevents unauthorised people from accessing systems or assets
  • Input validation in IT systems — prevents invalid data being entered
  • Password policies — prevents unauthorised system access
  • Pre-employment background checks — prevents hiring of dishonest employees
Before the Event
🔍
Detective Controls — Find It When It Happens
  • Designed to DETECT errors or fraud after they have occurred
  • Essential because preventive controls cannot catch everything
  • Examples:
  • Bank reconciliations — detect discrepancies between cash book and bank statement
  • Supplier statement reconciliations — detect unrecorded liabilities or duplicate payments
  • Inventory counts — detect discrepancies between physical stock and records
  • Exception reports — IT system flags unusual transactions for review
  • Internal audit reviews — detect control failures after the fact
  • Variance analysis — detect differences between budget and actual
  • CCTV cameras — detect theft of physical assets
During/After Event
🔧
Corrective Controls — Fix It After Detection
  • Designed to CORRECT errors or recover from fraud once detected
  • Minimise damage after a control failure has been identified
  • Examples:
  • Backup and recovery systems — restore data after IT failure or cyber attack
  • Insurance — financial recovery after loss events
  • Disciplinary procedures — correct employee behaviour after policy violation
  • Legal action against fraudsters — recover losses, deter future fraud
  • Error correction journals — fix accounting errors once detected
  • Business continuity plans — restore operations after disaster
After Detection
💡 Also Know: Manual vs Automated Controls
Manual Controls: Performed by people — e.g. a manager reviewing and approving invoices before payment. More flexible, can use judgement, but dependent on the person being competent and honest. Can be overridden or skipped when people are busy.

Automated Controls (IT Application Controls): Performed by computer systems automatically — e.g. system will not allow a purchase order to be raised above a limit without electronic approval. More consistent and reliable — doesn't depend on human discipline. But can be circumvented if IT general controls are weak (e.g. if access to change system settings is not properly controlled).

Best practice: Use both — automated controls for routine, high-volume transactions; manual controls for complex, judgement-based decisions. Over-reliance on either alone creates risk.

35.6
Segregation of Duties — The Most Important Control
Core Concept — Always Tested

Segregation of Duties (SoD) is arguably the single most important internal control. The principle is simple: no one person should be able to both commit a fraud AND conceal it. For any significant transaction, the key functions should be separated among different people.

The Four Functions That Must Be Separated
1. Authorisation
The person who APPROVES a transaction
e.g. Manager who approves a purchase order or payment
2. Custody
The person who has PHYSICAL CONTROL of the asset
e.g. Cashier who handles cash; warehouse staff who control inventory
3. Recording
The person who RECORDS the transaction in the books
e.g. Accounts payable clerk who posts invoices to the ledger
4. Reconciliation
The person who CHECKS and RECONCILES the records
e.g. Finance manager who reviews bank reconciliations

SoD in Practice — Purchases & Payables Cycle

✅ Good Segregation vs ❌ Poor Segregation — Purchases Example

✅ GOOD SEGREGATION
Purchasing dept raises Purchase Order → Person A
Finance Manager approves PO → Person B
Warehouse receives goods, signs GRN → Person C
Accounts Payable matches PO+GRN+Invoice, posts to ledger → Person D
Treasury processes payment → Person E
Finance Controller reconciles supplier statements → Person F

Result: No single person can place, approve, receive, record, pay AND reconcile a fictitious purchase.
❌ POOR SEGREGATION
Same person raises PO, approves it, receives goods, posts invoice to ledger, processes payment, AND reconciles supplier statements → ONE PERSON does everything

This person can:
→ Create a fictitious supplier
→ Raise a fake PO for goods never received
→ Pay the fake supplier (their own bank account)
→ Record it as a legitimate expense
→ Reconcile so it looks clean

Result: Perfect opportunity for undetectable fraud.
⚠️ SoD in Small Organisations — The Practical Problem
Perfect segregation of duties requires enough staff. A company with 3 employees CANNOT fully segregate all functions — there simply aren't enough people. This is a fundamental limitation of internal controls in small businesses.

Compensating controls for small organisations:
→ Owner/director personally reviews ALL bank statements monthly
→ All payments above a threshold require two signatures
→ Director personally approves all new supplier additions
→ External accountant performs monthly reconciliations independently
→ Surprise cash counts by director

ACCA exam tip: When a question gives a small company scenario, acknowledge that full SoD may not be possible, and recommend compensating controls instead.
35.7
IT Controls — General Controls & Application Controls

As businesses become increasingly digital, IT controls have become critical. A failure in IT controls can undermine ALL other controls — because if someone can change system settings or access unauthorized data, even the best manual controls become worthless. IT controls fall into two categories.

🖥️
IT General Controls (ITGCs) — The Foundation
  • Controls over the IT environment that support all application controls
  • Access Controls: Who can access which systems and data? User IDs, passwords, role-based access (people can only do what their job requires). Leavers' access removed immediately.
  • Change Management: All changes to IT systems must go through a formal change process — tested in a separate environment before live deployment. Prevents unauthorised system changes.
  • Operations Controls: Systems availability, job scheduling, monitoring of system performance, incident management.
  • Backup & Recovery: Regular backups of all data. Tested recovery procedures. Off-site backup storage. Business continuity plan for IT failure.
  • Physical Security: Data centres physically secured. Access restricted. Environmental controls (fire, flood, temperature).
  • Why ITGCs matter: If ITGCs are weak, application controls cannot be relied upon — auditors will not rely on any automated controls if ITGCs are poor.
IT Environment
⚙️
IT Application Controls — Within Each System
  • Automated controls built into specific applications (ERP, accounting system, payroll system)
  • Input Controls: Validate data as it enters the system. Format checks (date must be valid date), range checks (quantity cannot be negative), completeness checks (mandatory fields), duplicate checks (same invoice cannot be posted twice).
  • Processing Controls: Ensure data is processed completely and accurately. Batch totals, run-to-run totals, application logs.
  • Output Controls: Ensure outputs are complete and distributed to authorised recipients only. Exception reports, control totals on reports, restricted distribution of sensitive reports.
  • System Access Controls: Application-level access — e.g. accounts payable clerk can post invoices but cannot approve payments or change supplier bank details.
  • Key strength: Once programmed correctly, automated controls are applied consistently every time — never tired, never distracted, never dishonest.
Application Level

Cybersecurity Controls — Modern Critical Area

🔒 Key Cybersecurity Controls Every Finance Professional Should Know

Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA): Requires two forms of identity verification — password + mobile OTP. Even if password is stolen, attacker cannot access the system without the second factor.

Encryption: Financial data encrypted at rest and in transit — even if data is stolen, it cannot be read without the encryption key.

Penetration Testing: Regular ethical hacking to find vulnerabilities before malicious hackers do.

Phishing Training: Finance staff are prime targets — training reduces risk of employees clicking malicious links or sharing credentials.

Supplier/Vendor Controls (Business Email Compromise): Fraudsters impersonate suppliers, request change of bank details. Control: always verify bank detail changes via a SEPARATE phone call to a known number — never via email alone. This single control prevents enormous fraud losses.

Incident Response Plan: What to do when a breach occurs — contain, notify, recover, investigate. Required by data protection regulations (India's PDPB, EU's GDPR).

35.9
Limitations of Internal Controls

No internal control system is perfect. Understanding the limitations is as important as understanding the controls themselves — for ACCA exams and for real-world application.

👑
Management Override
  • The most dangerous limitation — senior management can bypass controls they themselves designed
  • Example: CFO instructs accounts team to post a journal entry without normal authorisation — overriding the journal entry control
  • Example: CEO pressures auditors to accept aggressive revenue recognition — overriding the financial reporting control environment
  • Classic fraud pattern: senior management fraud almost always involves override
  • Mitigation: strong, independent audit committee; external auditors sceptical of management; whistleblower mechanisms; board oversight
  • Cannot be fully eliminated — fundamental limitation
Most Dangerous
🤝
Collusion
  • Two or more people working together to circumvent controls — especially segregation of duties
  • Example: Purchasing clerk and warehouse manager collude — purchasing clerk raises fictitious POs, warehouse manager signs fake GRNs confirming receipt of non-existent goods
  • Harder to detect than individual fraud — two people's stories align
  • Mitigation: job rotation, surprise audits, data analytics to detect unusual patterns, culture of integrity, mandatory leave policies (fraudsters often refuse leave because the fraud will be discovered)
  • Cannot be fully eliminated — fundamental limitation
Two-Person Fraud
❌
Human Error
  • Even well-designed, well-intentioned controls fail due to human mistakes
  • Example: Bank reconciliation prepared but reconciling items not investigated — preparer just accepts them without follow-up
  • Example: Authorisation control — manager approves invoice without carefully reading it (rubber-stamping)
  • Mitigation: training, clear procedures, supervision, automated controls where possible, culture that encourages careful checking rather than rushing
  • Automation reduces but doesn't eliminate human error (humans programme the systems)
Honest Mistakes
💰
Cost vs Benefit
  • Every control has a cost — time, money, people, systems
  • Controls must be proportionate to the risk they mitigate
  • Example: requiring three sign-offs for a ₹500 stationery purchase is disproportionate — the control costs more than the potential loss
  • Over-controlled environments: staff spend so much time on controls they cannot do their actual work — operational inefficiency
  • Under-controlled environments: fraud and error flourish
  • Solution: risk-based approach — proportionate controls based on likelihood and impact of each risk
Proportionality
🔄
Changing Conditions
  • Controls designed for the current environment may become obsolete as conditions change
  • New technology creates new risks not anticipated by old controls
  • Business growth — controls suitable for a 50-person company may be inadequate for a 5,000-person company
  • New products, new markets, new regulations — all require reassessment of controls
  • Example: A company implements a new ERP system — all old manual controls need to be redesigned for the new system
  • Mitigation: regular control reviews, change management process, monitoring for control gaps
Obsolescence
🎭
Sophisticated Fraud
  • Determined, sophisticated fraudsters can design schemes specifically to circumvent controls
  • Example: Fraudster studies the control environment carefully, identifies gaps, and exploits them systematically
  • Example: Satyam — Raju specifically ensured his fraud was concealed from auditors through fabricated bank confirmations
  • No control system can prevent a determined, sophisticated fraudster with access and knowledge of the controls
  • Mitigation: rotation of staff, unpredictable audits, data analytics, culture of integrity, strong governance
Determined Fraudster

Evaluating & Testing Internal Controls

Both management and auditors need to evaluate whether controls are designed well and operating effectively. The process has two stages — design effectiveness and operating effectiveness.

STAGE 01
Understand and Document the Control System
Before evaluating controls, you must understand them. Methods include: narrative descriptions (written description of the process and controls), flowcharts (visual mapping of transaction flows and control points — excellent for identifying where controls exist and gaps), and Internal Control Questionnaires (ICQs — structured set of yes/no questions about specific controls). ICQs are particularly useful in ACCA exam questions — a "No" answer typically signals a control weakness.
STAGE 02
Assess Design Effectiveness
Is the control DESIGNED to prevent or detect the relevant risk? A control can be perfectly executed but still fail if it is designed to address the wrong risk. Walk-through tests: trace one or two transactions from start to finish through the system — does the control apply at the right point? Does it address the right risk? Is the control precise enough to catch material misstatements? Design assessment is done BEFORE testing operation.
STAGE 03
Test Operating Effectiveness — Tests of Controls
A well-designed control that is never actually followed provides no protection. Tests of controls assess whether controls are operating as designed throughout the period. Methods: Inspection (examine evidence that control operated — e.g. authorisation signature on invoices), Re-performance (auditor independently re-performs the control — e.g. re-does the bank reconciliation), Observation (watch the control being performed — limitation: behaviour may change when watched), Enquiry (ask staff about how the control operates — least reliable on its own). Sample size for tests of controls depends on frequency: daily controls require larger samples than monthly controls.
STAGE 04
Identify Control Deficiencies
Three levels of severity: Control Deficiency — a control is missing or not operating effectively, but the likelihood and magnitude of potential misstatement is low. Significant Deficiency — more severe than a deficiency; less than a material weakness; requires prompt attention from management. Material Weakness — a deficiency or combination of deficiencies in internal control such that there is a reasonable possibility that a material misstatement will not be prevented or detected. Material weaknesses must be reported to the audit committee and (for listed companies in USA under SOX) publicly disclosed.
💡 ACCA Exam Technique — Internal Control Questions
When a question presents a scenario and asks you to identify control weaknesses and recommend improvements:

Structure your answer as:
1. Weakness identified: State the control deficiency clearly — what is missing or not working
2. Risk/implication: Explain what could go wrong as a result — what fraud or error could occur
3. Recommendation: Suggest a specific, practical control to address the weakness

Example:
Weakness: The same employee who raises purchase orders also approves them.
Risk: This employee could raise fictitious purchase orders for goods or services never received, approve them, and process payment to a fictitious or personally controlled supplier — resulting in misappropriation of company funds.
Recommendation: Purchase order authorisation should be performed by a different person than the one who raises the PO — ideally the department manager or a designated approver with no access to the PO creation function. System controls should prevent the same user from both creating and approving a PO.
35.11
Reporting on Internal Controls
📋
Auditor's Report on Control Deficiencies (ISA 265)
  • External auditors must communicate significant deficiencies in internal control identified during audit to those charged with governance (Audit Committee)
  • Communication must be in writing — management letter or report to those charged with governance
  • Content: description of deficiency, potential effect, recommendation for remediation
  • Timing: communicated promptly — should not wait until audit report is issued
  • Minor deficiencies may be communicated to management (not necessarily to audit committee)
  • Follow up: in subsequent year's audit, check whether prior year deficiencies have been remediated
ISA 265
🏛️
SOX Section 404 — USA Listed Companies
  • Sarbanes-Oxley Act (2002) — USA law enacted after Enron/WorldCom scandals
  • Section 404: Management must assess and report on the effectiveness of internal controls over financial reporting (ICFR) in the annual report
  • External auditors must INDEPENDENTLY attest to management's assessment of ICFR
  • Any material weaknesses in ICFR must be publicly disclosed — major reputational and regulatory consequence
  • Applies to all US-listed companies — including large Indian companies listed on NYSE/NASDAQ (Infosys, TCS ADRs)
  • Cost of compliance is significant — but benefit is much stronger control environments in US-listed companies
USA Regulation
🇮🇳
India — ICFR Under Companies Act 2013
  • Section 134(5)(e) — Board must confirm in the Directors' Responsibility Statement that adequate internal financial controls (IFC) exist and are operating effectively
  • Section 143(3)(i) — Auditors must report whether the company has adequate IFC in place and whether they are operating effectively
  • Applicable to: all listed companies and large public unlisted companies
  • ICAI has issued Guidance Note on Audit of Internal Financial Controls — practical guidance for auditors
  • Introduced after IL&FS and other corporate governance failures — India's version of SOX 404 for IFC
India IFC

Three Lines of Defence Model

The Three Lines of Defence (now called Three Lines Model — IIA 2020) is a widely used framework for understanding how different functions within an organisation provide assurance and oversight over risks and controls.

1️⃣
First Line — Operational Management
  • The business itself — those who OWN and MANAGE risks day-to-day
  • Implement and maintain controls as part of normal operations
  • Examples: Finance team performing reconciliations, Procurement team following 3-way match process, HR team managing payroll controls, Sales team ensuring credit limits are adhered to
  • They are responsible for making the controls WORK in practice
  • First and most important line — if they don't follow controls, nothing else matters
  • Weakness: they may be too close to the business to identify all risks objectively
Business Operations
2️⃣
Second Line — Risk & Compliance Functions
  • Functions that OVERSEE and MONITOR the first line
  • Risk Management function — identifies, assesses, and monitors risks; ensures first line has appropriate controls
  • Compliance function — monitors adherence to laws, regulations, and internal policies
  • Financial Control / Controller function — ensures financial reporting controls are in place
  • IT Security function — monitors cybersecurity controls
  • They do NOT own the risk — that remains with the first line. They provide expertise, oversight, and challenge.
  • Report to senior management and board on risk and control status
Risk & Compliance
3️⃣
Third Line — Internal Audit
  • Provides INDEPENDENT assurance to the board and audit committee
  • Reviews the effectiveness of both first and second lines
  • Reports directly to the Audit Committee — independent of management
  • Cannot be directed by management to avoid certain areas — must have unrestricted access
  • Conducts risk-based audits — focusing on highest-risk areas
  • Issues audit reports with findings, recommendations, and management responses
  • Tracks implementation of recommendations
  • External auditors and regulators are sometimes called "fourth line" — entirely outside the organisation

Key Takeaways, Mistakes, Flashcards, Interview Q&A & Action Steps

Part A — 10 Core Takeaways

1️⃣
COSO — 5 Components
  • Control Environment (tone at top — most important)
  • Risk Assessment (identify what could go wrong)
  • Control Activities (the actual controls)
  • Information & Communication (nervous system)
  • Monitoring (checking controls work)
  • All five must be present and functioning
2️⃣
Three Types of Controls
  • Preventive — stop errors/fraud before they happen
  • Detective — identify them when they happen
  • Corrective — fix the damage after detection
  • A robust system uses ALL three
3️⃣
SoD — The Most Important Control
  • Separate: Authorisation, Custody, Recording, Reconciliation
  • No one person can commit AND conceal fraud
  • Small companies: compensating controls if full SoD impossible
  • Most famous failures involved SoD breakdown (Barings, PNB)
4️⃣
IT Controls — Foundation of Modern Business
  • ITGCs (access, change management, backup) support all application controls
  • If ITGCs weak → cannot rely on any automated controls
  • Application controls: input validation, processing controls, output controls
  • Cybersecurity controls — critical in digital age
5️⃣
Limitations — Always Acknowledge
  • Management override — most dangerous, cannot be eliminated
  • Collusion — two people bypass SoD
  • Human error — even honest people make mistakes
  • Cost vs benefit — controls must be proportionate
  • Changing conditions — controls become obsolete
6️⃣
Three Lines of Defence
  • 1st Line: Business operations — own and manage risks
  • 2nd Line: Risk & Compliance — oversight and monitoring
  • 3rd Line: Internal Audit — independent assurance to board
  • External auditor: independent external assurance
7️⃣
ACCA Exam Format — Weakness + Risk + Recommendation
  • Always follow: Identify weakness → Explain risk → Recommend specific control
  • Be specific — generic answers earn no marks
  • Consider direction of fraud risk (overstatement vs understatement)
  • Consider SoD, authorisation, physical controls, reconciliations
8️⃣
India Regulatory Requirements
  • Companies Act: Section 134 (Board IFC statement) + Section 143 (Auditor IFC opinion)
  • SEBI LODR: Internal audit mandatory for listed companies
  • RBI: Most comprehensive controls for banks (KYC, AML, credit risk)
  • Vigil mechanism (whistleblower) mandatory for listed companies
9️⃣
Key Business Cycles + Their Controls
  • Revenue: credit checks, SoD on cash, aged receivables review
  • Purchases: approved supplier list, 3-way match, two signatories
  • Payroll: HR authorised list, SoD between HR and payroll
  • Cash: bank reconciliation by independent person, dual authorisation
🔟
Control Deficiency Severity Levels
  • Control Deficiency — low likelihood/magnitude
  • Significant Deficiency — more severe, needs prompt attention
  • Material Weakness — reasonable possibility of material misstatement
  • Material weaknesses: report to audit committee, disclosed publicly (SOX/India IFC)

Part B — Common Exam Mistakes

❌ MISTAKE 1
Not Following Weakness → Risk → Recommendation Structure
Stating only the weakness without explaining the RISK of what could go wrong earns half marks. Always complete the full structure: what is wrong, what could happen as a result, and what specific control would fix it.
❌ MISTAKE 2
Generic Recommendations ("Implement Better Controls")
"Management should implement better controls over purchases" is not an answer. Be SPECIFIC: "A purchase order should require authorisation by a manager who did not raise the PO, before any goods can be ordered — this should be enforced at the system level to prevent bypass."
❌ MISTAKE 3
Forgetting IT Controls
Students focus on manual controls and forget IT controls entirely. Modern businesses are digital — IT application controls (input validation, duplicate checks, access controls) and IT general controls (change management, access management) are always relevant. Include them.
❌ MISTAKE 4
Ignoring Control Environment When Discussing Limitations
When asked about limitations of internal controls — students list collusion and human error but forget management override and control environment weakness. If tone at the top is poor — no control will work effectively. Always include this.
❌ MISTAKE 5
Confusing Internal Audit with External Audit
Internal audit reports to the BOARD/AUDIT COMMITTEE — not to management. External auditors can USE internal audit work but cannot DELEGATE their responsibility. Internal audit assesses controls — external audit assesses financial statements (using control evidence). These are distinct functions.

Part D — Interview Questions & Model Answers

QuestionModel Answer
"What is segregation of duties and why is it important?"Segregation of duties means separating the authorisation, custody, recording, and reconciliation functions so that no single individual can both commit a fraud and conceal it. It is the most important internal control because it requires collusion between at least two people to perpetrate and conceal fraud — making it significantly harder. Classic example: the person who raises purchase orders should not also approve them, receive the goods, record the payment, or reconcile supplier statements.
"What is the COSO framework?"COSO (Committee of Sponsoring Organizations) is the globally accepted framework for internal control. It defines internal control as having five interrelated components: Control Environment (tone at the top, integrity, ethics), Risk Assessment (identifying and analysing risks), Control Activities (the actual policies and procedures), Information and Communication (systems to support control), and Monitoring (ongoing assessment that controls are working). All five must be present and functioning for the control system to be effective.
"What are the limitations of internal controls?"Internal controls provide reasonable, not absolute, assurance. Key limitations: management override — senior management can bypass controls they designed; collusion — two or more people can circumvent segregation of duties; human error — even well-designed controls fail when people make mistakes; cost vs benefit — not all controls are cost-effective to implement; and changing conditions — controls can become obsolete as the business and environment evolve. These limitations are why independent audit is still necessary even with strong internal controls.
"What is the difference between IT general controls and IT application controls?"IT General Controls (ITGCs) are controls over the IT environment — access management, change management, operations, and backup/recovery. They support ALL systems and applications. If ITGCs are weak, no automated application control can be relied upon. IT Application Controls are built into specific systems — input validation, duplicate checks, access permissions within the application. They are more granular and transaction-specific. ITGCs are the foundation; application controls are the structure built on top.
