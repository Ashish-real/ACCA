44.1
Why Communication & Leadership Matter More Than Technical Skills

Here is the uncomfortable truth that most finance training ignores: technical skills are the entry ticket to a finance career. Communication and leadership skills determine how far you go. At the associate level, your DCF model matters. At the CFO level, your ability to inspire confidence in the board, navigate difficult conversations with the CEO, and lead a team through a crisis matters far more.

"I hire for intelligence and technical skills. I promote for communication, leadership, and the ability to make other people better." — Every CFO who has built a great finance team
📊
The Data on Soft Skills & Career Success
  • Harvard Study: 85% of career success comes from soft skills (communication, EQ, leadership). Only 15% from technical knowledge.
  • LinkedIn 2025 Global Talent Trends: Communication is the #1 most in-demand skill across all industries — above any technical skill
  • McKinsey: Finance professionals who can translate data into compelling narratives for non-finance audiences earn 30-40% more than equally technical peers who cannot
  • ACCA Global Research: The most common reason finance professionals plateau at Manager level is inability to influence senior stakeholders — not technical gaps
  • Big 4 Partner research: "Technical skills got them to Senior Manager. The inability to build client relationships and develop business kept them from Partner."
The Data
🎯
The Career Progression Shift
  • Associate level: 80% technical, 20% communication. Get the numbers right and document them clearly.
  • Senior Associate level: 70% technical, 30% communication. Begin managing juniors, first client communication.
  • Manager level: 50/50. Technical quality + stakeholder management + team leadership all equally important.
  • Senior Manager level: 30% technical, 70% leadership/communication. You review others' technical work — you focus on client relationships, team development, and business development.
  • Partner/CFO level: 10-20% technical, 80-90% leadership, communication, relationships, strategy. Your value is your judgement and your relationships — not your Excel skills.
  • The earlier you start developing these skills, the faster you progress through each level.
Skill Shift by Level
🚀
The Finance Communication Problem
  • Finance professionals are trained to be precise, detailed, and thorough — which produces excellent analysis but often terrible communication
  • The classic failure: a 50-page financial analysis with every number perfectly calculated, presented to a CEO who loses interest after page 3
  • Finance teams that communicate poorly are seen as "number crunchers" — back-office support. Finance teams that communicate brilliantly are seen as strategic business partners — front-office advisors.
  • The goal: be the finance professional who is sought out by the CEO for strategic advice, not just called when the numbers are needed
  • This requires deliberately developing communication skills — they don't come automatically with technical training
The Problem
44.2
The Finance Communication Challenge — Translating Numbers into Stories

The most valuable communication skill in finance is the ability to translate complex financial data into clear, compelling narratives that non-finance audiences can understand and act upon. This is not dumbing down — it is precision thinking applied to communication.

🔑 The Pyramid Principle — The Foundation of Finance Communication
The Pyramid Principle (Barbara Minto, former McKinsey consultant) is the most important communication framework in professional finance. It states: always lead with your conclusion, then support it with reasoning and evidence.

Most finance professionals communicate like this (BAD):
"We analysed revenue by region, product, and customer segment. We found that North region grew 8%, South grew 3%, East grew 15%, and West declined 5%. When we look at product mix, Product A grew 12% while Product B declined 8%. Customer concentration in the top 5 customers is 42%... [15 minutes of data]... Therefore, our recommendation is to invest in the East region."

Pyramid Principle (GOOD):
"We recommend immediately redirecting 30% of our sales investment from the West region to the East region — this single change could add ₹45Cr to annual revenue. Here's the evidence: East grew 15% with minimal investment while West declined 5% despite our highest spend. The top 3 customers driving East growth match our ideal customer profile. [Then details for those who want them]."

Lead with the conclusion. Lead with the "so what." The audience can always ask for more detail — they cannot un-hear 15 minutes of data before you get to the point.
📖
Audience Adaptation — Know Who You Are Talking To
  • Board of Directors: Big picture, strategic implications, risk. Maximum 3 key messages per presentation. They want: what is the situation, what are the options, what do you recommend, what do you need from us?
  • CEO: Decisions and actions. What do I need to decide today? What is the business impact? Keep it brief — under 10 minutes or they move on.
  • CFO: Financial precision + strategic context. They can handle the numbers but want the story too. 15-20 minutes appropriate for complex topics.
  • Business Unit Head: How does this affect MY P&L and MY team? Make it personal and relevant to their specific situation.
  • Your own Finance team: Technical detail is appropriate. Be precise. They want to understand the methodology and assumptions.
  • Auditors: Evidence, documentation, process. Be thorough. They need to understand and be able to verify.
Audience First
🎯
The "So What?" Test
  • Before presenting any financial information, ask: "So what? Why does this matter? What should the audience DO with this information?"
  • If you cannot answer that question, the information is not ready to present
  • Example: "Revenue grew 8% YoY" → So what? Is 8% good or bad? Vs budget? Vs industry? What caused it? What should we do differently?
  • Better: "Revenue grew 8% YoY, which is 3% below our 11% budget target. The shortfall is entirely from the West region — all other regions are on or above target. We recommend [specific action] to close the gap before year end."
  • Every number you present must have a "so what" — otherwise you are presenting data, not insight
  • The "so what" is where finance creates value — transforming information into action
The So What Test
44.3
Written Communication — Emails, Reports & Memos
📧
Professional Email — Finance Standard
  • Subject line: Specific and action-oriented. "Q3 Revenue Update — Action Required by Friday" not "Revenue." The subject should tell the reader what the email is about AND what they need to do.
  • Opening: State the purpose immediately. "I am writing to request your approval for the Q3 budget reforecast. Key change: we are reducing revenue forecast by ₹40Cr (8%) due to West region shortfall."
  • Body: Maximum 5 sentences for routine emails. Use bullet points for lists. No paragraphs of more than 3 sentences. Bold key numbers and decisions.
  • Clear ask: End with explicit action required. "Please reply with your approval/comments by Thursday 5pm so we can submit the reforecast to the board on Friday."
  • Tone: Professional but not robotic. Warm opening for relationship emails. Direct and concise for information/action emails.
  • Read before sending: Would you be comfortable if this email were forwarded to your MD or the CEO? If not, revise.
Email Best Practice
📋
Financial Reports & Memos
  • Executive Summary first: Always. One page maximum. Key findings, recommendations, and decisions required. Busy executives read only this — make it complete enough to stand alone.
  • BLUF (Bottom Line Up Front): Military communication principle — state your conclusion first, then support it. Never make the reader wade through analysis to find the recommendation.
  • Structure every report: 1) Situation (what is happening), 2) Complication (what is the problem/opportunity), 3) Question (what decision is needed), 4) Answer (your recommendation), 5) Evidence (supporting analysis)
  • One message per paragraph: Each paragraph has one central idea. If you cannot say what the paragraph is about in one sentence, it needs to be restructured.
  • Numbers with context: Never present a number without a benchmark. ₹50Cr is meaningless. ₹50Cr vs ₹60Cr budget is a 17% shortfall requiring action.
Report Writing
✏️
Writing Clarity — Common Finance Writing Mistakes
  • Jargon overload: "The EBITDA CAGR on a LTM basis shows a YoY improvement in the pre-exceptional normalised margin profile." → Write in plain English first, then add technical terms where genuinely necessary.
  • Passive voice: "It was determined by the finance team that..." → "The finance team found that..." Active voice is always clearer and more confident.
  • Hedge words: "It would appear that," "it seems like," "it could be suggested that" — these weaken your message. If you believe it, state it. If you're uncertain, say why.
  • Buried recommendation: Putting your conclusion at the end forces the reader to read everything before understanding the point. Put the recommendation first.
  • Too long: Every report should be cut by 20% after the first draft. Remove any sentence that doesn't add new information or support a specific point.
Common Mistakes
44.4
Presentation Skills — Speaking to Boards, Clients & Colleagues

Presenting to a board, an audit committee, a client CFO, or a senior management team is one of the highest-stakes communication moments in finance. The quality of your presentation often determines how your entire team's work is perceived — regardless of the quality of the underlying analysis.

PRINCIPLE 1
Structure is Everything — Use SCR or STAR
Every presentation needs a clear structure. For financial presentations: SCR (Situation → Complication → Resolution): "Our revenue is on track overall (Situation). However, West region is declining at 5% per month and will miss target by ₹60Cr (Complication). We recommend immediately redirecting 30% of West's marketing budget to East region — projecting a ₹45Cr recovery (Resolution)." The audience always needs to know: where are we, what is the problem, what do we do about it?
PRINCIPLE 2
One Key Message Per Slide
The most common presentation mistake: slides crammed with data, multiple charts, walls of text. One slide should make one point. The slide title should be the message — not a label. "Revenue by Region Q3 2024" tells the audience nothing. "West Region Revenue Declining at 5%/Month — Immediate Action Required" tells them exactly what to think. If a slide has more than 5 bullet points or 2 charts, it is trying to do too much. Split it or cut it.
PRINCIPLE 3
Prepare for Questions — Not Just the Slides
Most presentation value is created in the Q&A, not the slides. Board members and CFOs probe: "Why is West declining?" "Have we spoken to the customers we're losing?" "What confidence do you have in the East recovery?" Anticipate the top 10 questions you might be asked and prepare concise, evidence-backed answers. Have backup slides for detail that you've excluded from the main presentation — show them only if asked. Never say "I don't know" and stop — say "I don't have that specific number with me, but I'll follow up today."
PRINCIPLE 4
Body Language and Delivery
Finance professionals often present looking at their slides, speaking to the screen instead of the audience. Eye contact is critical — look at each person in the room, spending 3-5 seconds making a connection before moving on. Pace: slower than you think. Most nervous presenters speak 30% too fast. Pause after key points — silence is more powerful than filling it with "um" or "so." Stand tall, use gestures purposefully, project your voice to the back of the room. Record yourself presenting and watch it back — what you see will be uncomfortable but invaluable.
PRINCIPLE 5
Practice Out Loud — Not Just in Your Head
The biggest preparation mistake: reading slides silently and thinking "I know this well enough." Out-loud practice is qualitatively different from mental rehearsal. You discover: where you stumble, which transitions are clunky, where you need to pause, whether the timing works. Practice the opening three sentences of any presentation until you can say them perfectly without thinking — because the opening is when nerves are highest and cognitive capacity is lowest. Once you survive the first 60 seconds, the rest flows much more naturally.
💡 Presenting to the Board — Specific Techniques
Board presentations are different from any other presentation. Board members are extremely time-pressured, very experienced, and will challenge you. Here is what works:

Open with the conclusion: In the first 30 seconds, tell them what you want them to know/decide/approve. They may stop you there and say "agreed — let's move on." That is the best possible outcome, not a problem.

Respect their time: Keep to your allocated time slot. Running over signals poor preparation and disrespect for the board's schedule. Practice your timing.

Handle challenges confidently: When a board member challenges your numbers, do NOT immediately capitulate ("you're absolutely right, I'll revisit that"). If you believe you are correct, say so politely: "That's an important challenge. Our calculation is based on [methodology]. Happy to walk you through the detail after the meeting if that would be helpful."

Know what you don't know: If you don't know the answer to a question, say: "I want to give you the accurate answer rather than speculate. I'll confirm and send you a note by end of day." This is far more credible than guessing wrong in front of the board.

Position your recommendation as professional judgement: "Based on the analysis and our professional judgement, we recommend..." Not "the analysis says..." Your job is to advise, not just report data.
44.5
Data Storytelling — Making Numbers Compelling

Data storytelling is the art of combining data, narrative, and visuals to communicate insights that move people to action. It is the highest-value communication skill for finance professionals — because finance is inherently about data, but decisions are made by humans who respond to stories.

📖
The Three Elements of Data Storytelling
  • Data (the evidence): The numbers, analysis, and evidence that underpin the story. Must be accurate, relevant, and sufficient. But data alone does not move people to action.
  • Narrative (the story): The verbal and written explanation that gives the data context, meaning, and emotional resonance. Answers: why does this matter? What caused this? What is at stake? What should we do?
  • Visuals (the show): Charts, graphs, and images that make the data tangible and memorable. The right visual makes a complex pattern instantly obvious. The wrong visual confuses and obscures.
  • All three must work together. Data without narrative is noise. Narrative without data is opinion. Visuals without either are decoration.
  • The most powerful finance presentations in the world (Berkshire Hathaway annual letter, Goldman Sachs M&A presentations) excel at all three simultaneously.
Three Elements
🎬
The Narrative Arc of Financial Storytelling
  • Every great financial story follows a narrative structure: Setup → Tension → Resolution
  • Setup: Establish the context — where are we, what is the business situation, what was expected?
  • Tension: Introduce the problem — what is not as expected, what is at risk, what needs to change?
  • Resolution: Offer the solution — what should we do, what will happen if we do it, what is needed from this audience?
  • Example: "We were on track to hit our ₹500Cr revenue target (Setup). However, three major customers in the West region have reduced orders, putting ₹60Cr at risk (Tension). Immediately redirecting our sales team to focus on East region — where we are seeing 15% growth — can recover ₹45Cr of this gap before year end (Resolution)."
  • This structure works for every board presentation, management report, investor update, and audit committee communication.
Story Structure
📊
Choosing the Right Visual for Your Data Story
  • Showing change over time → Line chart. "Revenue has grown consistently for 8 months but declined sharply in September." Line chart makes this obvious instantly.
  • Comparing categories → Bar chart. "West region is the only declining region." Side-by-side bars make this comparison immediate.
  • Showing composition → Stacked bar or pie. "Product A now represents 60% of revenue, up from 40% two years ago."
  • Explaining a change → Waterfall chart. "Budget was ₹500Cr. Volume added ₹20Cr. Price pressure cost ₹30Cr. Net: ₹490Cr actual." The waterfall makes the moving parts visible.
  • Finding outliers → Scatter plot. "These 5 customers generate the most revenue with the lowest cost to serve — our ideal customer profile."
  • Rule: if the chart takes more than 5 seconds to understand, it is the wrong chart or is too complex.
Visual Selection
44.6
Active Listening — The Most Underrated Finance Skill

Finance professionals are trained to talk — to present analysis, explain numbers, defend recommendations. But the most effective finance leaders are those who listen more than they talk. Active listening is the skill that builds trust, surfaces critical information, and prevents the most expensive mistakes.

👂
What Active Listening Actually Means
  • Active listening is NOT waiting for your turn to speak. It is fully focusing on understanding what the other person is saying — their words, their tone, and what they are NOT saying.
  • Physical signals of active listening: Maintain eye contact. Nod to acknowledge. Lean slightly forward. Put your phone away. Do not look at your slides while they are speaking.
  • Cognitive signals: Ask follow-up questions that show you heard. Summarise back: "So what I'm hearing is that your main concern is the working capital impact, not the revenue miss itself — is that right?" This confirms understanding and shows you listened.
  • Don't interrupt: Let the person finish their complete thought before responding. In finance meetings, the most important information often comes at the end of what someone is saying — if you interrupt, you miss it.
  • Silence is okay: After someone finishes, a 2-3 second pause before responding signals thoughtfulness, not confusion.
True Listening
💎
Why Listening Drives Finance Career Success
  • Surfaces the real problem: The stated problem is rarely the real problem. "Our revenue is declining" might really be "we're losing a key customer because of service quality." Listening carefully and asking the right questions uncovers the real issue — which leads to the right solution.
  • Builds trust with clients and senior management: People feel heard when you genuinely listen. People who feel heard trust you. People who trust you bring you their real problems — not just the sanitised version.
  • Better analysis: Listening to business leaders explains WHY the numbers look the way they do — context that no financial model can provide.
  • Fewer expensive mistakes: Many financial errors result from proceeding based on assumed understanding rather than confirmed understanding. Listening and confirming prevents this.
  • Influence: The paradox of influence — you have MORE influence over people when they feel listened to, not less. People are more open to your recommendations when they feel you've genuinely understood their perspective first.
Why It Matters
44.7
Difficult Conversations — Delivering Bad News in Finance

Finance professionals are frequently the bearers of bad news — budget shortfalls, missed targets, audit findings, control failures, impairments. How you handle these conversations determines your credibility, your relationships, and your career.

⚡
The Golden Rules of Bad News
  • Never delay bad news: The instinct to delay bad news hoping it will improve is one of the most expensive mistakes in finance. Bad news that reaches the board late — when it could have been surfaced earlier — destroys credibility far more than the original bad news.
  • Come with the problem AND a solution: Don't just report the problem — bring an analysis of causes and a recommended action. "Revenue missed budget by ₹50Cr. Here is why. Here is what we propose to do about it." This positions you as a problem-solver, not just a reporter.
  • Be direct, not brutal: Deliver the bad news clearly and unambiguously in the first 30 seconds — don't bury it. But deliver it with empathy and respect. "I need to share some challenging news about our Q3 performance."
  • Own your team's failures: If your team made an error, own it — don't deflect to individuals or circumstances. "We made an error in our revenue recognition that has resulted in a ₹15Cr overstatement. Here is our corrective action plan."
  • Don't shoot the messenger: A culture where bad news is penalised is a culture where bad news is hidden until it becomes catastrophic. Model the behaviour you want: thank people who bring you bad news early.
Bad News Rules
🗣️
Specific Difficult Finance Conversations
  • Telling a business unit they've missed budget: Private conversation first. Lead with the data. Ask for their explanation before giving your view. Agree on a recovery plan. Document the plan and agreed actions.
  • Challenging an accounting treatment you disagree with: Prepare your technical argument in writing. Request a meeting with the relevant person. Present your position respectfully but clearly: "I have a concern about how we're accounting for this — can I walk you through my thinking?" If overruled by a senior and it's a material issue, escalate appropriately.
  • Telling the CFO/Board that a target will be missed: Never surprise the board. Give them maximum possible warning. Bring the full analysis of causes and a credible recovery plan. Recommend whether to revise guidance and how.
  • Performance conversations with your team: Specific, evidence-based feedback. "The report you submitted had three factual errors and missed the deadline" not "your work quality has been disappointing." Clear expectation for improvement with a timeline.

44.8
Influence Without Authority — Getting Things Done in Finance

Finance professionals rarely have direct authority over the people they need to influence most — business unit heads, operational teams, IT, HR. The CFO cannot order the Sales Director to improve the revenue forecast process. The finance business partner cannot compel the factory manager to submit cost variance explanations on time. Influence without authority is the core skill of effective finance leadership.

🤝
Building Credibility — The Foundation of Influence
  • People follow those they trust and respect. Credibility in finance is built through: consistently accurate, reliable analysis (numbers that are never wrong); deep understanding of the business (not just the spreadsheet); following through on commitments (if you say you'll send it by 5pm, send it by 5pm); confidentiality (business leaders share sensitive information only with those they trust to keep it); being right more often than not on forward-looking judgements.
  • Credibility is built over months and years — and can be destroyed in a single incident. Protect it fiercely.
  • Early in your career: build credibility through flawless execution of technical work. Later: through business insight, strategic advice, and people development.
Credibility First
🧠
Techniques for Influencing Without Authority
  • Understand their interests first: Before trying to influence someone, understand what they care about. A sales director cares about hitting revenue targets, not about IFRS 15 compliance. Frame your message in their language: "This change will protect your commission plan from being adjusted at year-end" not "this is required by IFRS 15."
  • Build relationships before you need them: Influence is built on relationships. Get to know people before you need something from them. Regular informal touchpoints (coffee, brief check-ins) build the relationship capital that you need when asking for action.
  • Use data to persuade, not to bludgeon: Data should illuminate, not overwhelm. Present the one or two numbers that most powerfully support your position — not everything in your analysis.
  • Find shared goals: "We both want to close the year on target — the working capital improvement I'm proposing would contribute ₹20Cr to your P&L." Position finance initiatives as helping the business achieve its own goals.
  • Involve them in the solution: People commit to solutions they helped design. Rather than telling a business unit what they must do, involve them in designing the process. They will implement what they created.
Influence Techniques

44.10
Emotional Intelligence (EQ) — The CFO's Secret Weapon

Emotional Intelligence (EQ) — the ability to recognise, understand, and manage your own emotions and those of others — is consistently identified as the most important differentiator between good and great leaders. In finance, where the work is technically demanding and the stakes are high, EQ determines whether you can maintain performance under pressure and bring out the best in your team.

🧠
Daniel Goleman's 5 Components of EQ
  • 1. Self-Awareness: Knowing your own emotions, strengths, weaknesses, values, and how they affect others. Finance application: knowing when you are under pressure and how it affects your communication and decisions. The self-aware CFO says "I'm stressed about this audit — let me take 10 minutes before I respond to this email."
  • 2. Self-Regulation: Managing disruptive emotions and impulses. Not exploding when a team member makes a costly error. Not catastrophising when a target is missed. Maintaining professional composure under pressure — especially in front of the board or clients.
  • 3. Motivation: Being driven by internal standards of excellence beyond external rewards. The finance leader who cares about the quality of their team's work and the organisation's success — not just their own bonus.
  • 4. Empathy: Understanding the emotions of others. The CFO who recognises that the production director is under personal stress when their factory just had an accident — and adjusts the timing and manner of a difficult financial conversation accordingly.
  • 5. Social Skills: Managing relationships effectively. Building networks, leading teams, influencing without authority, managing conflict productively. The culmination of the other four EQ components in practice.
EQ Framework
📈
Developing Your EQ — Practical Steps
  • Get 360-degree feedback: Ask 5-10 people (boss, peers, direct reports, clients) for honest feedback on your communication and leadership style. The gap between how you see yourself and how others see you is where the most powerful EQ development happens.
  • Pause before reacting: When you feel a strong emotion (frustration, anxiety, pride), take 60 seconds before responding. This pause allows the rational brain to engage before the emotional brain drives your response.
  • Name your emotions: Research shows that labelling emotions reduces their intensity. "I am frustrated that this report is late" is more manageable than an unnamed feeling of anger. This sounds simple — it is profoundly powerful in practice.
  • Practice empathy deliberately: Before a difficult conversation, spend 5 minutes thinking about the other person's perspective — what are they worried about, what do they care about, what is their context? This shifts your approach from confrontational to collaborative.
  • Seek a coach or mentor: EQ development is most effective with a trusted third party who can observe you in real situations and give honest feedback. Executive coaching is increasingly common and valuable for senior finance professionals.
EQ Development
44.11
Managing Up, Down & Across — Navigating Finance Organisations
⬆️
Managing Up — Working with Your Boss & Senior Leaders
  • Understand their priorities: What does your CFO care most about this quarter? What is keeping them awake at night? Align your work to their priorities — not your own perception of what's important.
  • No surprises: The first rule of managing up. Your boss should never learn about a problem from someone else first. Be the first to tell them, even when the news is bad.
  • Bring solutions, not just problems: Don't walk into your CFO's office with a problem and no proposed solution. "We have a problem with the audit timeline. I've spoken to the team and we have three options. My recommendation is option 2 because..."
  • Communicate in their preferred style: Some CFOs want daily brief updates. Others want weekly structured reports. Some prefer verbal, others written. Learn their style and adapt — not vice versa.
  • Make your work visible (appropriately): Your boss cannot advocate for you if they don't know what you're achieving. Brief them on significant wins — not boastfully, but informatively. "Just wanted to let you know we completed the ERP migration 2 weeks ahead of schedule."
Managing Up
⬇️
Managing Down — Leading Your Finance Team
  • Set clear expectations: Every team member must know exactly what is expected — quality standards, deadlines, communication norms. Ambiguity is the enemy of performance.
  • Give specific, regular feedback: Not just in annual reviews. Brief, specific feedback after every significant piece of work: "The variance analysis you produced was excellent — particularly the customer-level breakdown. For next time: the executive summary could be condensed to half the length."
  • Delegate real work: Delegation is not about offloading admin. It is about giving people work that stretches them — with appropriate support. The most effective managers delegate client conversations, board presentation preparation, and technical analysis — not just data collection.
  • Protect your team: When your team makes a mistake, own it with senior management. In the team meeting afterwards, debrief and learn. Don't throw individuals under the bus publicly — this destroys trust.
  • Develop their careers actively: Know each person's career goals. Create opportunities for them to develop toward those goals. Advocate for their promotions. Great managers are measured by how many leaders they have developed.
Managing Down
↔️
Managing Across — Working with Peers & Business Partners
  • Invest in relationships before you need them: The finance business partner who has built a genuine relationship with the Sales Director over 6 months gets information, cooperation, and honest disclosure that a transactional relationship never produces.
  • Understand their world: Spend time in the business — understand what success looks like for your counterpart, what their pressures are, what their team deals with. This understanding makes you a genuine business partner rather than a cost-control function.
  • Collaboration over confrontation: When Finance and a Business Unit disagree (over budget, over an accounting treatment, over a capital allocation), the natural dynamic can become adversarial. Reframe: "We're both trying to achieve the best outcome for the company — let's find a solution that works."
  • Be reliable and follow through: In cross-functional relationships, nothing builds reputation faster than consistently doing what you say you will do, when you say you will do it. Be the person other departments trust to deliver.
Managing Across

Building & Leading High-Performance Finance Teams
ELEMENT 1
Hire for Attitude, Train for Skill
In finance, it is tempting to hire purely on technical competence. The best finance team builders hire for intellectual curiosity, communication ability, integrity, and growth mindset — then invest in technical training. A highly analytical person with poor communication and low EQ creates more problems than they solve as they become more senior. Technical skills can be taught — character and mindset are much harder to develop in adults.
ELEMENT 2
Create Psychological Safety
High-performing finance teams are characterised by psychological safety — team members feel safe to raise concerns, admit mistakes, challenge assumptions, and bring bad news without fear of punishment or ridicule. Google's Project Aristotle found psychological safety to be the single most important factor in team performance — more important than talent, resources, or process. In finance, psychological safety is especially critical because catching errors early requires people to feel safe surfacing them.
ELEMENT 3
Clarity of Purpose and Standards
Every member of your finance team should be able to articulate: what does this team exist to do? What does excellent work look like here? How do we make decisions? Great finance teams have a shared understanding of their purpose (strategic finance partner, not just compliance function), their standards (work that is accurate, insightful, and communicated clearly), and their values (integrity, curiosity, collaboration). This clarity drives consistent high performance without constant management intervention.
ELEMENT 4
Development Culture — Everyone is Growing
The best finance teams I have seen have a culture where everyone is continuously learning — from the CFO down to the newest associate. Leaders invest time in developing their people. Mistakes are treated as learning opportunities, not just failures. Stretch assignments are given deliberately. External training (ACCA, CFA, data analytics courses) is supported financially and with time. People who feel they are growing are engaged. People who feel stagnant leave.
ELEMENT 5
Celebrate Success and Learn from Failure
High-performance teams recognise achievement explicitly. The Finance Manager who built the board's favourite dashboard gets recognised. The team that closed the books 2 days early gets celebrated. This is not just about morale — it reinforces the behaviours and standards you want repeated. Equally: when things go wrong, conduct a blame-free post-mortem. "What happened, why did it happen, what can we do to prevent it next time?" — not "whose fault is this?"
44.13
Executive Presence — How to Show Up at the Senior Level

Executive presence is the combination of qualities that makes senior leaders command attention, inspire confidence, and be taken seriously in high-stakes situations. It is not about being the loudest voice or the most confident person — it is about being the most credible, composed, and compelling person in the room.

💼
The Three Dimensions of Executive Presence
  • Gravitas (67% of Executive Presence): How you carry yourself — confidence, composure under pressure, decisiveness. Knowing when to speak and when to listen. Being calm when others are panicking. Making decisions without needing validation.
  • Communication (28% of Executive Presence): Clarity of message, commanding speaking voice, ability to read the room and adapt, compelling storytelling with data, ability to simplify the complex. The finance professional who can explain a complex accounting issue simply and compellingly has communication executive presence.
  • Appearance (5% of Executive Presence): Professional dress, grooming, and body language appropriate to the environment. While the smallest component, it signals respect for the occasion and professionalism. Don't overthink this — just be appropriately and neatly presented for the context.
  • Source: Sylvia Ann Hewlett's research on executive presence for senior leadership.
EP Framework
🎯
Building Gravitas in Finance
  • Know your subject deeply: Gravitas starts with genuine expertise. You cannot fake it in a room of experienced executives — but deep knowledge creates natural confidence.
  • Speak with conviction: State your view directly: "My recommendation is X" not "one option might be X." Own your professional judgement.
  • Maintain composure in crisis: When the audit finds an unexpected issue, when the board questions your numbers, when a major deal falls apart — the most valuable finance leaders remain calm, analytical, and solution-focused. This composure is noticed and remembered.
  • Contribute meaningfully, not constantly: In senior meetings, quality of contribution matters more than quantity. One insightful observation is more powerful than five average comments. Choose your moments.
  • Handle pushback without crumbling: When a board member challenges your analysis, don't immediately capitulate. If you believe you are right, defend your position respectfully: "I understand your concern. Our analysis accounts for that factor in the following way..."
Building Gravitas
💡 Executive Presence in Practice — The First 60 Seconds
Research shows that impressions of executive presence are formed in the first 60 seconds of a new interaction. This is especially important in interviews, first meetings with senior clients, and presentations to new audiences.

Walking into a room: Walk at a confident pace. Make eye contact with people as you enter. Extend a firm handshake. Have your opening sentence ready and say it without hesitation.

Starting a presentation: Stand comfortably, make eye contact with the most senior person in the room, and begin with your conclusion — not with "Good morning everyone, thank you for being here, my name is... today I'm going to take you through..."

In a meeting when you don't know something: Don't fidget, avoid eye contact, or give a rambling non-answer. Simply say: "I want to give you the accurate answer on this. I'll confirm and send you a note within the hour." Then actually do it.

Being challenged publicly: Take a slow breath. Don't react immediately. Respond at a measured pace: "That's an important point. Let me address it directly." Then do so clearly and confidently — whether you're agreeing, partially agreeing, or respectfully disagreeing.

Part B — Common Mistakes

❌ MISTAKE 1
Presenting Data Without a Conclusion
"Here are the revenue numbers" without "here is what we recommend doing about them" is the most common finance communication failure. Every data presentation must end with a clear "so what" and a recommended action. Numbers without context and recommendation are just noise to non-finance audiences.
❌ MISTAKE 2
Delaying Bad News
Waiting until bad news is confirmed beyond doubt, fully analysed, and you have a perfect solution before telling your boss or the board. By then, they may have already heard it from someone else (destroying your credibility) or the problem has grown larger than it needed to. Surface bad news as soon as you become aware of it, even if your analysis is incomplete. "I need to flag something early — I'm still assessing the full impact, but I wanted you to be aware."
❌ MISTAKE 3
Being a Talking Spreadsheet
Finance professionals who present every number in their analysis, explain every assumption, and share every caveat before getting to the point are treating their audience like they are reading a technical report — when they need a business decision. Edit ruthlessly. Your audience is busy and smart. They don't need to see your work — they need your conclusion and your top 3 supporting facts.
❌ MISTAKE 4
Ignoring Soft Skill Development in Early Career
"I'll work on communication skills when I'm more senior" — this is the most expensive career mistake a young finance professional can make. The time to develop these skills is when the stakes are lower. Presenting to a junior client is where you develop the skill set you'll use in front of the board in 10 years. Every presentation, every email, every difficult conversation is a training opportunity.
❌ MISTAKE 5
Confusing Busyness with Leadership
Many finance managers believe that being the most technically involved person on every piece of work demonstrates leadership. It doesn't — it demonstrates a failure to delegate. Real leadership is when your team produces excellent work without your constant involvement. The most powerful leadership question is not "have I done this right?" but "have I developed my team enough to do this right without me?"
