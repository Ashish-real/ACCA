46.1
What is ESG & Why It Has Become Central to Finance

ESG stands for Environmental, Social, and Governance — the three broad categories of factors beyond traditional financial metrics that are used to assess a company's sustainability, ethical impact, and long-term risk profile. What began as a niche concern for ethical investors has become mainstream — reshaping how companies are valued, how capital is allocated, and what finance professionals need to know.

"Climate risk is investment risk. ESG is not a constraint on returns — it is a lens that reveals risks and opportunities that traditional financial analysis misses." — Larry Fink, BlackRock CEO, Annual Letter 2020
📈
Why ESG Has Become Central to Finance
  • Investor demand: $35+ trillion in ESG-screened assets under management globally (2025). BlackRock, Vanguard, State Street — the world's largest asset managers all have major ESG commitments. FIIs increasingly screen Indian stocks on ESG criteria.
  • Regulatory pressure: EU Taxonomy, ISSB standards, SEC climate disclosure rules (USA), SEBI BRSR (India) — governments globally are mandating ESG disclosure. Non-compliance risks regulatory action and exclusion from capital markets.
  • Risk management: Physical climate risks (floods, droughts, extreme heat) and transition risks (carbon taxes, stranded assets) are material financial risks that must be assessed and managed. Ignoring them is poor risk management.
  • Stakeholder expectations: Employees (especially young talent), customers, suppliers, and communities increasingly demand that companies operate responsibly. Companies that fail on ESG face talent flight, customer boycotts, and reputational damage.
  • Cost of capital: Companies with strong ESG scores access cheaper capital — lower bond yields, higher equity valuations, better banking terms. Poor ESG scores increase financing costs.
Why ESG Matters
🌍
The Three Pillars — Quick Overview
  • E — Environmental: How a company impacts and is impacted by the natural environment. Carbon emissions, energy use, water consumption, waste management, biodiversity, supply chain environmental impact.
  • S — Social: How a company manages relationships with employees, suppliers, customers, and communities. Labour practices, health & safety, diversity & inclusion, human rights, community impact, data privacy.
  • G — Governance: How a company is directed and controlled. Board composition and independence, executive remuneration, shareholder rights, anti-corruption, transparency, audit quality. (Covered in depth in Chapter 38.)
  • All three are interconnected — poor governance (G) makes credible E and S commitments impossible to sustain. Strong G is the foundation for genuine E and S performance.
  • Different sectors have different ESG priorities: E is most critical for energy, mining, and manufacturing. S is most critical for retail, food, and technology. G is universally critical.
E + S + G
💰
ESG & Financial Performance — The Evidence
  • MSCI research: Companies with high ESG ratings have lower cost of capital, lower earnings volatility, and higher valuations over the long run
  • Harvard Business School study: Companies with high sustainability standards outperformed on stock market by 4.8% annually over 18-year study period
  • S&P 500 ESG Index vs S&P 500: near-identical long-term performance — ESG investing does not sacrifice returns in diversified portfolios
  • India evidence: Nifty100 ESG Index: companies with high ESG scores have historically shown lower drawdowns during market crises — better risk-adjusted returns
  • The debate: The relationship between ESG and financial performance is complex — some studies show outperformance, others show neutral returns. What is clear: ESG risks are real financial risks that prudent investors cannot ignore
ESG & Returns
46.2
E — Environmental Factors in Finance

Environmental factors assess a company's relationship with the natural world — its impacts on the environment and the financial risks it faces from environmental changes. Climate change is the dominant environmental concern in finance today, but it is far from the only one.

🌡️
Climate Risk — Physical & Transition
  • Physical Risks: Financial losses from climate events and long-term climate shifts. Acute: floods, cyclones, droughts, wildfires damaging assets and disrupting operations. Chronic: sea level rise, average temperature increases, changing rainfall patterns affecting agriculture, water supply, and coastal assets.
  • Transition Risks: Financial losses from the economic transition to a low-carbon economy. Policy risk: carbon taxes, emissions trading schemes making current business models uneconomical. Technology risk: renewable energy making fossil fuels obsolete. Market risk: changing consumer preferences toward sustainable products. Stranded assets: assets (coal mines, oil fields, high-emission factories) that become worthless before end of useful life.
  • India exposure: Indian companies face significant physical climate risk — extreme heat, floods, cyclones, water scarcity. India's coal-dependent power sector faces major transition risk as renewable energy costs fall below coal.
  • Finance implication: Climate risks must be factored into: loan risk assessments (collateral values at risk), investment valuations (DCF cash flows affected by carbon costs), insurance pricing, capital planning.
Climate Risk
💨
Carbon Emissions — Scope 1, 2 & 3
  • Scope 1 (Direct emissions): Greenhouse gases directly emitted by the company — burning fuel in factories, company vehicles, industrial processes. The company controls these directly.
  • Scope 2 (Indirect — energy): Emissions from purchased electricity and heat. When a company buys electricity, it is responsible for the emissions from generating that electricity (even though they happened at the power plant).
  • Scope 3 (Value chain): All other indirect emissions — from the company's supply chain (suppliers' emissions), product use by customers, business travel, employee commuting, investments. Typically the largest category (70-90% of total for most companies) but hardest to measure and control.
  • Net Zero commitments: Companies commit to reducing emissions to zero (Scope 1+2 minimum, increasingly Scope 3) by 2050 or earlier. India at COP26: committed to Net Zero by 2070 and 50% renewable energy by 2030.
  • Finance role: Finance teams are now responsible for carbon accounting — measuring, verifying, and reporting emissions data with the same rigour as financial data.
Carbon Accounting
💧
Other Key Environmental Factors
  • Water: Water scarcity is a critical risk for Indian companies — agriculture, textiles, beverages, semiconductors all water-intensive. Water stress in certain regions can shut down operations entirely. Adani Ports, Tata Steel — all disclose water usage and stress assessments.
  • Biodiversity: TNFD (Taskforce on Nature-related Financial Disclosures) — emerging framework for nature-related risk disclosure. Companies operating in or near biodiversity-rich areas face rising regulatory and reputational risk.
  • Waste & Circular Economy: Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) regulations in India require companies to take responsibility for end-of-life disposal of products. Circular economy: designing products to be reused, repaired, or recycled — reduces waste and resource costs.
  • Supply Chain: Deforestation-linked supply chains (palm oil, soya, timber) face growing regulatory and investor pressure. Companies must now trace their supply chains for environmental violations.
Environmental Factors
46.3
S — Social Factors in Finance
👥
Labour & Human Capital
  • Why it matters financially: Companies with poor labour practices face: strikes and operational disruption, regulatory fines, talent flight (especially critical in knowledge economy), reputational damage, consumer boycotts.
  • Key metrics: Employee turnover rate, training hours per employee, gender pay gap, diversity of workforce (gender, ethnicity, ability), employee satisfaction scores, safety incident rates, fatality rates.
  • Living wage: Growing expectation that all employees — including supply chain workers — receive a living wage, not just the legal minimum. Companies that rely on below-living-wage labour face growing investor and consumer pressure.
  • India context: Labour practices in supply chains (especially garments, electronics assembly) under intense scrutiny from international buyers and investors. Companies like Zara, H&M require their Indian suppliers to meet social standards or lose contracts.
Labour Practices
🌍
Human Rights & Supply Chain
  • Modern slavery and forced labour in supply chains are now financial risks — not just ethical concerns. Companies found to have forced labour in their supply chains face: regulatory fines (UK Modern Slavery Act, EU Due Diligence Directive), export bans (USA Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act), loss of major customers.
  • Due diligence requirement: Companies must now map and audit their supply chains for human rights violations — not just their direct suppliers, but Tier 2 and Tier 3 suppliers.
  • India: Many international companies source from India. Indian exporters must demonstrate compliance with labour standards to maintain contracts with European and American brands — this is a real commercial risk for Indian manufacturers.
  • The EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (CS3D) — requires large companies to conduct human rights and environmental due diligence across their entire value chain. Effective from 2026-2028. Will affect Indian companies supplying European companies.
Human Rights
🏘️
Community & Diversity
  • Community impact: Companies that damage local communities face licence-to-operate risk — protests, legal challenges, regulatory action. Mining and infrastructure projects particularly vulnerable. Positive community engagement protects licence to operate and builds goodwill.
  • Diversity & Inclusion (D&I): Research consistently shows diverse teams make better decisions and perform better financially. Companies with higher gender and ethnic diversity at board and management level show better long-term performance. Investors and employees increasingly require meaningful D&I commitments — not just tokenism.
  • India CSR (Companies Act 2013): Companies meeting size thresholds must spend 2% of average 3-year net profit on CSR activities — education, healthcare, environment, poverty alleviation. The largest mandatory CSR regime globally by number of companies.
  • Data privacy: With DPDPA 2023 in India — how companies collect, store, and use personal data is increasingly a social AND regulatory risk. Data breaches have both financial (regulatory fines) and reputational consequences.
Social Impact
46.4
G — Governance Factors in Finance

Governance is the foundation of ESG — without strong governance, environmental and social commitments are meaningless promises. The G in ESG covers how companies are directed, controlled, and held accountable. This was covered comprehensively in Chapter 38 — here we focus specifically on the ESG governance dimension.

🏛️
ESG Governance — Key Factors
  • Board ESG oversight: Does the board have a dedicated ESG/Sustainability Committee? Are board members sufficiently knowledgeable about climate and ESG risks? Is ESG a standing agenda item at board level?
  • Executive pay linked to ESG: Are CEO and senior management bonuses partially linked to ESG targets (carbon reduction, safety performance, diversity targets)? If not — where is the accountability for ESG outcomes?
  • Anti-corruption: Robust anti-bribery and corruption policies, training, reporting mechanisms, and enforcement. FCPA (USA), UK Bribery Act, India Prevention of Corruption Act — all create legal liability for corporate corruption.
  • Tax transparency: Aggressive tax avoidance is increasingly viewed as a governance failure — shifting the tax burden from corporations to ordinary citizens and creating reputational risk when exposed.
  • Political lobbying: Disclosure of political donations and lobbying activities — especially when companies lobby against climate policy while claiming ESG leadership (a form of governance hypocrisy).
  • Whistleblower protection: Safe, accessible channels for employees and others to report governance, environmental, and social concerns without fear of retaliation.
ESG Governance
📊
Governance & ESG Ratings
  • Governance typically carries the highest weight in ESG ratings — because it is the most measurable and the most predictive of overall ESG performance
  • MSCI ESG Ratings, Sustainalytics, S&P Global CSA — all weight governance heavily, especially: board independence, ownership structure, accounting quality, business ethics track record
  • India governance challenges: Promoter-controlled companies, related party transactions, governance of group companies — these reduce governance scores for many Indian companies vs global peers
  • Companies with poor governance scores face: higher cost of capital, exclusion from ESG indices, reduced FII investment, lower valuations
  • Tata Group, Infosys, HUL, HDFC Bank — regularly score highest on governance among Indian companies. Promoter-heavy conglomerates typically score lower.
G in Ratings

46.6
ESG Reporting Standards — ISSB, GRI, TCFD & More

The ESG reporting landscape has historically been fragmented — dozens of different frameworks, different metrics, different definitions. This is rapidly changing with the emergence of the ISSB (International Sustainability Standards Board) as the global standard setter.

🌐
ISSB — The Global Standard (Most Important)
  • ISSB (International Sustainability Standards Board) created in 2021 — part of the IFRS Foundation (same organisation that sets accounting standards)
  • Published two landmark standards in June 2023:
  • IFRS S1 — General Requirements: Requires companies to disclose material sustainability-related risks and opportunities that could affect their enterprise value. Applicable to all sectors.
  • IFRS S2 — Climate-related Disclosures: Specifically addresses climate risk disclosure — physical and transition risks, governance, strategy, risk management, metrics and targets. Based on TCFD framework.
  • India: ICAI and SEBI are both monitoring ISSB adoption. Expected to be incorporated into India's sustainability reporting requirements progressively — aligned with BRSR.
  • Why it matters: finally a global, consistent framework for ESG reporting — like IFRS did for financial accounting. Enables investor comparability across companies and countries.
ISSB Standards
📋
TCFD — Climate Disclosure Framework
  • Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD) — created by FSB (Financial Stability Board) in 2015, chaired by Mark Carney
  • Four pillars of TCFD disclosure:
  • Governance: Board and management oversight of climate risks and opportunities
  • Strategy: Actual and potential impacts of climate risks on the organisation's businesses and strategy, including scenario analysis (1.5°C, 2°C, 4°C warming scenarios)
  • Risk Management: How climate risks are identified, assessed, and managed — integration with overall enterprise risk management
  • Metrics & Targets: Metrics used to assess climate risks, Scope 1/2/3 emissions, targets for emissions reduction
  • TCFD now forms the basis of ISSB S2 — so learning TCFD is learning the foundation of the global standard
TCFD
📊
Other Major ESG Frameworks
  • GRI (Global Reporting Initiative): Most widely used sustainability reporting framework globally. Focus on impact on society and environment (stakeholder perspective — different from ISSB which focuses on financial materiality). GRI Standards cover economic, environmental, and social topics.
  • SASB (Sustainability Accounting Standards Board): Sector-specific standards for the most material ESG issues in 77 industries. Now integrated into ISSB — SASB metrics used as starting point for industry-specific ISSB disclosures.
  • UN SDGs (Sustainable Development Goals): 17 goals for 2030 — poverty, hunger, health, education, gender equality, clean water, clean energy, etc. Many companies report how their activities contribute to specific SDGs.
  • CDP (Carbon Disclosure Project): Global disclosure system for environmental data — companies answer standardised questionnaire on climate, water, and forests. CDP scores (A to D) widely used by investors.
  • EU CSRD (Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive): Requires large EU companies and non-EU companies with significant EU operations to report under European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS) — very detailed requirements. Affects Indian companies with EU subsidiaries or large EU customer bases.
Other Frameworks

ESG in India — SEBI BRSR & Regulatory Framework
🇮🇳
BRSR — Business Responsibility and Sustainability Reporting
  • SEBI introduced BRSR in 2021 — mandatory for top 1,000 listed companies by market cap from FY 2022-23
  • Replaces earlier BRR (Business Responsibility Report) — more detailed, more data-driven, more aligned with global standards
  • Structure — 9 Principles (National Voluntary Guidelines):
  • P1: Ethics, transparency, accountability
  • P2: Sustainable products and services
  • P3: Employee wellbeing
  • P4: Stakeholder engagement
  • P5: Human rights
  • P6: Environment
  • P7: Public and regulatory policy
  • P8: Inclusive growth
  • P9: Customer responsibility
  • Each principle has essential (mandatory) and leadership (voluntary) indicators
BRSR India
📊
BRSR Core & Assurance
  • BRSR Core (2023): SEBI introduced a sub-set of 9 Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) within BRSR that require third-party assurance — not just self-reporting. This significantly increases the credibility and rigour of ESG disclosures.
  • BRSR Core KPIs include: Intensity of GHG emissions per rupee of turnover, water intensity per rupee of turnover, waste generated per rupee of turnover, number of POSH complaints, pay gap between male and female employees at various levels, and others.
  • Phased implementation: Top 150 companies mandatory from FY 2023-24, expanding to top 1,000 over time
  • The assurance requirement creates significant work for Big 4 and sustainability assurance providers — new revenue stream and new skillset demand
  • Finance teams now responsible for collecting, verifying, and reporting non-financial ESG data with same rigour as financial data
BRSR Core
🏛️
India ESG Regulatory Landscape
  • SEBI: BRSR mandatory disclosure + ESG rating providers regulated by SEBI + green bonds framework + SEBI ESG Ratings Providers Regulations 2023
  • RBI: Climate risk framework for banks and NBFCs — requiring financial institutions to assess and disclose climate-related financial risks in their loan portfolios. Discussion paper released 2023.
  • Companies Act 2013: Mandatory CSR spending (2% of net profit) — over ₹25,000Cr spent annually on CSR in India
  • Environment Ministry: Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (CCTS) — India's domestic carbon market being developed under Energy Conservation Act. Will create financial incentives for emissions reduction.
  • India's NDCs (Nationally Determined Contributions): Committed to 45% reduction in emissions intensity of GDP by 2030 (vs 2005 levels), 50% of electricity from non-fossil fuel sources by 2030, Net Zero by 2070
India Regulation

Climate Risk & Finance — TCFD Framework

Climate risk is now recognised as a systemic financial risk — not just an environmental concern. Finance professionals must understand how to identify, measure, and manage climate risk in their organisations and portfolios.

🌡️ Climate Scenario Analysis — A Finance Tool

TCFD recommends that companies analyse their financial performance under different climate scenarios. This is increasingly required by investors and regulators.

Three Standard Scenarios:
1.5°C Scenario (Ambitious Climate Action): Strong, early policy action — carbon taxes rise rapidly, fossil fuels phased out, renewable energy dominant by 2040. Companies in fossil fuels, high-emission manufacturing face major transition risk. Renewable energy, electric vehicles, energy efficiency companies benefit.

2°C Scenario (Moderate Action): Moderate policy action — carbon pricing introduced but more gradually, some fossil fuels continue but declining. Balanced transition and physical risk profile.

4°C Scenario (Business as Usual): Minimal climate policy. Severe physical risks — extreme weather events become much more frequent and severe. Companies with physical assets (real estate, agriculture, coastal infrastructure) face major risk. But transition risk is lower in the short term.

Finance Application: Run your company's financial model under each scenario — how does revenue, cost, asset value, and access to capital change? This scenario analysis is now required by ISSB S2 and recommended by TCFD for all companies with material climate exposure.
🏦
Climate Risk in Banking — RBI Focus
  • Banks face climate risk both directly (physical damage to their own assets) and through their loan portfolios (borrowers' climate risks become credit risks)
  • Transition risk in loan books: Bank lending to coal mines, high-emission manufacturers, petrol stations — if these businesses become unviable due to carbon policy, the loans become NPAs. This is a systemic risk to Indian banking system.
  • Physical risk: Loans secured against coastal property, agricultural land, or assets in flood-prone areas — collateral values at risk from climate events
  • RBI has issued guidance requiring banks to start assessing and disclosing climate-related financial risks — following global central bank standards (NGFS — Network for Greening the Financial System)
  • Bank stress tests now increasingly include climate scenarios — how does the bank's capital adequacy hold up if carbon tax is introduced at ₹2,000/tonne of CO₂?
Banking Climate Risk
📉
Stranded Assets — The Hidden Valuation Risk
  • Stranded assets: assets that suffer unexpected or premature write-downs, devaluations, or conversion to liabilities due to changes in regulation, technology, or market preferences — driven by climate transition
  • Most at risk globally: Coal reserves (unburnable if 1.5°C target met — 80% of existing coal reserves must stay in the ground), oil sands (high-cost, high-emission), natural gas infrastructure in regions transitioning rapidly to renewable energy, high-emission factories
  • India specific: Coal India — largest coal producer globally. If India's energy transition accelerates, Coal India's reserves face stranded asset risk. Old thermal power plants with remaining life face early decommissioning risk.
  • Finance implication: DCF valuations of companies with significant fossil fuel or high-emission assets must consider stranded asset risk in terminal value assumptions. Ignoring this overstates value.
Stranded Assets

Green Finance — Green Bonds, Sustainability-Linked Loans
🟢
Green Bonds
  • Bonds where proceeds are ring-fenced for specific green projects — renewable energy, energy efficiency, clean transportation, sustainable water, green buildings
  • Green Bond Principles (GBP): ICMA (International Capital Markets Association) framework defining what qualifies as a green bond. Four key components: Use of Proceeds, Process for Project Evaluation, Management of Proceeds, Reporting.
  • India Green Bond Market: Growing rapidly. Adani Green Energy, ReNew Power, NTPC, REC Limited — all issued green bonds. India's first Sovereign Green Bond (Jan 2023): ₹16,000Cr raised at a 5-6bps "greenium" (lower interest rate) vs conventional sovereign bond.
  • Finance role: Structuring green bonds, independent verification ("Second Party Opinion"), use-of-proceeds tracking, annual impact reporting. Big 4 and specialist firms providing assurance on green bond frameworks.
  • Green bond premium ("greenium"): Green bonds typically price 2-10bps lower than equivalent conventional bonds — investors accept lower yield for the ESG label. Real financial benefit to issuers with credible green credentials.
Green Bonds
🔗
Sustainability-Linked Loans & Bonds (SLLs/SLBs)
  • Unlike green bonds (where proceeds must fund green projects), SLLs/SLBs link the INTEREST RATE to the borrower's ESG performance — regardless of how the funds are used
  • How it works: Borrower agrees to specific Sustainability Performance Targets (SPTs) — e.g. reduce Scope 1+2 emissions by 30% by 2025, achieve gender parity at management level by 2026. If targets are met → interest rate reduces (reward). If targets are missed → interest rate increases (penalty).
  • India examples: JSW Steel — $1B SLL linked to green steel production targets. Tata Power — SLL linked to renewable energy capacity targets. HDFC Ltd — SLL linked to social housing targets.
  • Finance role: Structuring SPTs, annual KPI verification, interest rate adjustment calculations, reporting to lenders. Finance teams now responsible for non-financial KPI tracking with same rigour as financial reporting.
SLL/SLB
🌱
Social Bonds & Blended Finance
  • Social bonds: Proceeds used for social projects — affordable housing, healthcare, education, financial inclusion. Grew significantly during COVID-19 (governments issued social bonds to fund pandemic response).
  • Sustainable bonds: Combination of green and social use of proceeds in one bond — growing category.
  • Blended finance: Combining public/concessional capital with private capital to mobilise private investment in sustainable development. Development Finance Institutions (DFIs) provide first-loss or guarantee to de-risk projects for private investors. NaBFID (National Bank for Financing Infrastructure and Development) in India plays this role.
  • India opportunity: India needs $10T+ of infrastructure investment by 2050 — much of this must be sustainable (green infrastructure). Blended finance can bridge the gap between public budgets and private capital requirements.
Other Instruments

⚠️ The ESG Ratings Problem — Low Correlation Between Agencies
Studies show that ESG ratings from different agencies for the same company correlate only 0.3-0.6 (where 1.0 would be perfect agreement). This is far lower than the 0.99 correlation between credit ratings from Moody's and S&P for the same bond.

Why they differ so much: Different materiality judgements (what ESG factors matter for this company?), different data sources, different weightings, different assessment horizons, different treatment of controversies.

Result: Tesla is simultaneously one of the highest-rated companies on some ESG metrics (EV → zero emissions) and one of the lowest-rated on others (labour relations, governance, social controversies). How you weight these factors determines the overall ESG score.

For finance professionals: Don't rely on a single ESG rating. Understand the methodology behind each rating. Use multiple sources. Supplement ratings with your own fundamental ESG analysis. And always apply scepticism — ESG ratings are imperfect tools, not definitive judgements.
46.11
Greenwashing — The Biggest Risk in ESG

Greenwashing is the practice of making misleading or exaggerated claims about environmental or ESG practices — presenting a false or overstated picture of sustainability performance. It is the defining risk in ESG and the primary regulatory concern globally.

⚠️
Types of Greenwashing
  • Misleading claims: "We are carbon neutral" — but only through cheap offsets of dubious quality, while actual emissions continue rising
  • Cherry-picking: Highlighting one positive ESG achievement while ignoring material negative ESG issues in other areas
  • Vague language: "Eco-friendly," "sustainable," "green" — without specific, measurable commitments or data
  • Hidden trade-offs: "Our product is made from recycled materials" — but has poor energy efficiency and non-recyclable packaging
  • Irrelevant claims: "CFC-free" — when CFCs are already banned and all products are CFC-free
  • ESG fund greenwashing: Funds marketed as "ESG" but containing companies with significant ESG controversies. DWS (Deutsche Bank's asset management arm) fined by US and German regulators for overstating ESG integration in 2023.
Greenwashing Types
🔍
Regulatory Action Against Greenwashing
  • EU Anti-Greenwashing Regulation (2024): Bans vague environmental claims without substantiation. Requires all environmental claims to be independently verified. Major brands already removing "eco-friendly" from packaging.
  • SEC (USA) ESG Disclosure Rules: Climate risk disclosure rules (finalised 2024) require public companies to disclose material climate risks in financial filings. False or misleading ESG disclosures = securities fraud.
  • FCA (UK): Sustainability Disclosure Requirements (SDR) and anti-greenwashing rule — any ESG claim made to UK customers must be "fair, clear, and not misleading."
  • SEBI India: Has issued guidance on ESG rating agencies and is monitoring BRSR disclosures for accuracy. Expects BRSR Core indicators to be independently assured — providing accountability for ESG claims.
  • Criminal liability risk: In several jurisdictions, deliberate greenwashing in financial products is now being prosecuted as fraud — creating personal liability for finance professionals who sign off on misleading ESG disclosures.
Regulatory Action
✅
How Finance Professionals Protect Against Greenwashing
  • Verify before disclosing: Any ESG claim in a financial filing, bond prospectus, or investor communication must be supported by evidence. Apply the same verification standards as financial disclosures.
  • Carbon offset scrutiny: If company claims carbon neutrality through offsets — verify offset quality (Gold Standard, VCS certified), additionality (would the carbon reduction have happened anyway?), permanence (will the carbon stay sequestered?). Cheap, low-quality offsets are a major greenwashing risk.
  • Independent assurance: BRSR Core requires assurance — this is the market's response to greenwashing. Third-party verification of ESG data is the standard being adopted globally.
  • Don't claim what you can't measure: ESG claims must be based on actual data, collected through robust processes, verified by independent parties. If you can't measure it accurately, don't claim it publicly.
  • Professional ethics: ACCA's Code of Ethics applies to ESG-related professional work — honesty, accuracy, and not being associated with misleading information. Greenwashing assistance = professional misconduct.

Part A — 12 Core Takeaways

1️⃣
ESG = Material Financial Risk
  • Climate risk is investment risk (Larry Fink)
  • Physical risk: floods, droughts, heat damaging assets
  • Transition risk: carbon taxes, stranded assets, technology disruption
  • Ignoring ESG = ignoring material financial risk
2️⃣
E = Environmental
  • Scope 1: direct emissions (company controls)
  • Scope 2: purchased electricity emissions
  • Scope 3: value chain emissions (70-90% of total)
  • Net Zero: reduce all to zero by target date
3️⃣
ISSB — The Global Standard
  • IFRS S1: general sustainability disclosure
  • IFRS S2: climate-specific disclosure (based on TCFD)
  • Part of IFRS Foundation — like IFRS for financial reporting
  • India aligning BRSR with ISSB progressively
4️⃣
BRSR — India Mandatory
  • Mandatory for top 1,000 listed companies
  • 9 Principles covering E, S, and G
  • BRSR Core: 9 KPIs requiring independent assurance
  • Finance teams now report ESG data like financial data
5️⃣
ESG Investing Approaches
  • Integration: ESG as financial risk lens
  • Negative screening: exclude sin stocks/fossil fuels
  • Positive: best-in-class within sectors
  • Thematic: invest in ESG solutions
  • Impact: measurable positive impact
6️⃣
Green Finance Instruments
  • Green bonds: proceeds for green projects
  • SLLs/SLBs: interest rate linked to ESG performance
  • Greenium: lower interest rate for credible green issuers
  • India sovereign green bond: ₹16,000Cr (2023)
7️⃣
TCFD Four Pillars
  • Governance: board oversight of climate risk
  • Strategy: scenario analysis (1.5°C, 2°C, 4°C)
  • Risk Management: how climate risk is managed
  • Metrics & Targets: emissions, reduction targets
8️⃣
Greenwashing = Career Risk
  • Misleading ESG claims = regulatory violation and reputational damage
  • ACCA ethics: do not be associated with misleading information
  • Verify all ESG claims before disclosing
  • Independent assurance protects against greenwashing risk

ESG Ratings Limitations
  • Low correlation between agencies (0.3-0.6)

Part B — Common Mistakes

❌ MISTAKE 1
Treating ESG as a PR/Marketing Exercise
ESG is not about producing a beautiful sustainability report — it is about managing real financial risks and creating measurable value. Companies that treat ESG as a communication exercise (greenwashing) face regulatory action. Finance professionals must ensure ESG disclosures are accurate, material, and verifiable — applying the same standards as financial reporting.
❌ MISTAKE 2
Ignoring ESG in Financial Valuations
Building a DCF model for a coal company or a coastal real estate developer without considering climate risk in the terminal value assumptions produces a systematically overvalued result. Climate transition risk and physical risk must be integrated into financial models — this is no longer optional for credible financial analysis.
❌ MISTAKE 3
Relying on a Single ESG Rating
As demonstrated by the low inter-agency correlation in ESG ratings, no single rating is definitive. Using one ESG score as the sole basis for an investment or lending decision is methodologically weak. Always understand the methodology behind any ESG score you use, and supplement with your own fundamental ESG analysis.
❌ MISTAKE 4
Assuming ESG is Only for Large Companies
BRSR is mandatory for the top 1,000 listed companies — but ESG considerations affect all companies through their supply chains (international buyers requiring ESG compliance from Indian suppliers), their banking relationships (RBI climate risk guidelines affecting how banks assess borrowers), and their access to capital markets.
❌ MISTAKE 5
Not Investing in ESG Knowledge
ESG is the fastest-growing area in finance. Finance professionals who have not developed basic ESG literacy will find themselves less relevant in a world where every major investment decision, lending decision, and financial report includes ESG dimensions. 6-12 months of focused learning can transform your career trajectory in this space.
