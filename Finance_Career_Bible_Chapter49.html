49.5
LW (Corporate & Business Law) — Applied Skills

Contract law fundamentals — the 4 elements every valid contract needs, and the remedies available on breach.

Ch23 — The 4 Essential Elements of a Valid Contract
1. Offer
A clear, definite statement of terms one party is willing to be bound by if accepted. Must be distinguished from an "invitation to treat" (e.g. goods displayed in a shop window are an invitation to treat, not an offer — the customer makes the offer at the till).
2. Acceptance
Unqualified agreement to all terms of the offer, communicated to the offeror. A "counter-offer" (changing any term) destroys the original offer and becomes a new offer itself.
3. Consideration
Something of value given by each party — the "price" of the promise. Must have SOME value (need not be adequate/fair value) and must NOT be past (a promise to pay for something already done is generally not valid consideration).
4. Intention to Create Legal Relations
Both parties must intend the agreement to be legally binding. Commercial agreements are PRESUMED to have this intention; social/domestic agreements are PRESUMED NOT to — either presumption can be rebutted with evidence.
Ch23 — Available Remedies
Damages
Monetary compensation to put the innocent party in the position they would have been in had the contract been performed. Must be caused by the breach and not too "remote" (unforeseeable). The innocent party has a duty to "mitigate" loss — cannot recover losses that reasonable steps could have avoided.
Specific Performance
A court order requiring the breaching party to actually perform the contract. Discretionary — granted mainly where damages are inadequate (e.g. sale of unique goods or land), not for personal service contracts.
Injunction
A court order preventing a party from doing something that would breach the contract (e.g. preventing breach of a non-compete clause).
Rescission
Cancels the contract, putting parties back in their pre-contract position — typically the remedy for misrepresentation rather than breach.

49.7
TX (Taxation) — Applied Skills

India-specific corporate tax rates and deferred tax mechanics, plus the personal tax / double tax relief methods.

Ch37 — Corporate Tax Rates — India (AY 2024-25)
Domestic Company (General Rate)
30% + Surcharge + Health & Education Cess 4% Surcharge: 7% if income ₹1–10Cr; 12% if income above ₹10Cr Effective rate for large companies: ~34.94%
Concessional Rate — Section 115BAA
22% + Surcharge 10% + Cess 4% = Effective ~25.17% Available to all existing domestic companies — cannot claim certain deductions (80IC, 80IE etc.). Most large companies have opted for this. Announced in September 2019 — major corporate tax cut.
New Manufacturing Companies — Section 115BAB
15% + Surcharge 10% + Cess 4% = Effective ~17.16% For new domestic manufacturing companies incorporated after 1 Oct 2019 and commencing production before 31 Mar 2024 (extended). India's lowest corporate tax rate — designed to attract manufacturing investment (Make in India). Cannot claim most deductions.
Foreign Companies
40% + Surcharge + Cess 4% On income arising in India. May be reduced by DTAA (Double Tax Avoidance Agreements) with the country of the foreign company's residence.
Minimum Alternate Tax (MAT)
15% of Book Profit (if tax payable under normal provisions is less) Ensures companies with high book profits but low taxable income (due to deductions) still pay minimum tax. MAT credit can be carried forward 15 years. Companies opting for 115BAA/BAB are exempt from MAT.
Ch37 — Deferred Tax — Core Concepts
Temporary Difference
Carrying Amount (per IFRS balance sheet) − Tax Base (per tax rules) Carrying amount = IFRS book value of asset or liability. Tax base = amount attributed to asset/liability for tax purposes (i.e. what tax law says it's worth).
Deferred Tax Liability (DTL)
When carrying amount of ASSET > tax base → TAXABLE temporary difference → DTL Will result in more tax in the future. Example: Accelerated tax depreciation — asset depreciated faster for tax than for accounting. Book value > tax base → DTL recognised.
Deferred Tax Asset (DTA)
When carrying amount of ASSET < tax base → DEDUCTIBLE temporary difference → DTA Will result in less tax in the future. Example: Provision for bad debts — not deductible until debt actually written off. Provision reduces book value of receivable below tax base → DTA. Also: tax losses carried forward → DTA (if probable future profits to use them against).
Deferred Tax Calculation
Deferred Tax = Temporary Difference × Applicable Tax Rate Use the tax rate expected to apply when the difference reverses — typically the enacted or substantively enacted future tax rate, not current year's rate.
Ch37 — Tax in the P&L and Balance Sheet — Full Picture
Tax Expense in P&L (IAS 12)
Tax Expense = Current Tax + Deferred Tax Expense (or − Deferred Tax Income) Current Tax = Tax payable on this year's taxable profit per tax return. Deferred Tax = Change in deferred tax liability/asset during the year. Both components separately disclosed in notes.
Current Tax
Taxable Profit × Current Tax Rate Taxable profit ≠ accounting profit. Adjust for: disallowed expenses (add back), non-taxable income (deduct), timing differences. Recognised as a liability (tax payable) until paid.
Effective Tax Rate
Tax Expense ÷ Profit Before Tax × 100% Shows the true % of accounting profit paid as tax. Differs from statutory rate due to: deferred tax, permanent differences (exempt income, disallowed expenses), prior year adjustments. Important metric for financial analysis — compare to statutory rate to understand tax efficiency.
Balance Sheet Presentation
Current Tax Liability: tax payable for current and prior years (current liability) Deferred Tax Liability: non-current liability (usually) Deferred Tax Asset: non-current asset (if recognition criteria met) DTA and DTL for same tax jurisdiction may be offset if legally enforceable right to offset exists.
Ch37 — Key Formulas & Rates
Deferred Tax
DT = (Carrying Amount − Tax Base) × Future Tax Rate | Positive = DTL | Negative = DTA
Effective Tax Rate
ETR = Tax Expense ÷ Profit Before Tax × 100%
Interest Tax Shield
Tax Shield = Interest Paid × Tax Rate | After-tax cost of debt = Kd × (1 − tax rate)
GST Payable
GST Payable = Output Tax (on sales) − Input Tax Credit (on purchases)
Advance Tax Schedule
15% by 15 June | 45% by 15 Sep | 75% by 15 Dec | 100% by 15 Mar
Section 115BAA Rate
22% + 10% surcharge + 4% cess = Effective 25.17%
Section 115BAB Rate
15% + 10% surcharge + 4% cess = Effective 17.16%
HRA Exemption (Minimum of 3)
Min of: (1) Actual HRA received | (2) 50%/40% of salary (metro/non-metro) | (3) Actual rent − 10% of salary
Ch28 — Double Tax Relief Methods
Exemption Method
Income taxed in the source country is fully EXEMPTED from tax in the residence country
Credit Method (Most Common)
Residence country taxes worldwide income but gives a CREDIT for tax already paid in the source country, up to the residence country's own tax rate on that income
Deduction Method (Least Favourable)
Foreign tax paid is simply deducted as an EXPENSE before calculating residence-country tax — provides only partial relief

49.9
AA (Audit & Assurance) — Applied Skills

Materiality, the audit risk model, and the segregation-of-duties principle that underpins internal control testing.

Ch34 — Official Definition — ISA 200
Objective of an Audit
To enable the auditor to express an opinion on whether the financial statements are prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with an applicable financial reporting framework (e.g. IFRS or local GAAP). Key phrase: "in all material respects" — not every single detail. Materiality limits the scope.
Level of Assurance
REASONABLE assurance — not absolute assurance. Reasonable = high but not 100%. Absolute assurance is impossible due to: use of sampling, inherent limitations of internal controls, professional judgment involved in estimates, possibility of management override.
True and Fair View
Financial statements present fairly the financial position, performance, and cash flows of an entity. "True" = factually accurate, supported by evidence. "Fair" = presented without bias, in accordance with GAAP/IFRS. This is the standard the auditor is assessing against.
Free From Material Misstatement
Whether due to fraud OR error. Auditors must consider both. Error = unintentional mistake. Fraud = intentional misrepresentation. Auditors are not primarily fraud investigators but must maintain professional scepticism and respond when fraud risks are identified.
Ch34 — Materiality — Benchmarks & Calculation
Overall Materiality — Common Benchmarks
5% of Profit Before Tax (PBT) — most common for profitable entities 1% of Revenue — for low-margin businesses or where PBT is volatile 0.5–1% of Total Assets — for banks, holding companies, or asset-heavy entities 1–2% of Equity — for entities where equity is the key metric Choice of benchmark depends on what users focus on most. Professional judgement required.
Performance Materiality
Typically 60–75% of Overall Materiality Lower threshold used when designing procedures — so that the AGGREGATE of uncorrected misstatements does not exceed overall materiality. Acts as a buffer. If multiple small errors are found, their total might still be material.
Clearly Trivial Threshold
Typically 3–5% of Overall Materiality Misstatements below this level are clearly trivial and need not be investigated or accumulated. They cannot — even in aggregate — be material.
Ch34 — The Audit Risk Model
Audit Risk (AR)
AR = Inherent Risk (IR) × Control Risk (CR) × Detection Risk (DR) Auditor aims to keep AR at an acceptably low level. IR and CR are assessed — Detection Risk is manipulated by adjusting the nature/timing/extent of audit procedures.
Inherent Risk (IR)
The susceptibility of an assertion to material misstatement BEFORE considering any controls. High IR examples: complex accounting estimates (fair value, provisions), subjective judgements, unusual transactions, high-volume/high-value transactions, industries prone to fraud (cash-heavy businesses, financial services), businesses under financial pressure.
Control Risk (CR)
The risk that a material misstatement will NOT be prevented or detected by the entity's internal controls. High CR: weak internal controls, override of controls by management, no segregation of duties, inadequate IT controls. Low CR: strong, effective controls — allows auditor to reduce substantive testing.
Detection Risk (DR)
The risk that the auditor's OWN PROCEDURES will fail to detect a material misstatement. This is what the AUDITOR CONTROLS. If IR and CR are high → auditor must REDUCE DR by doing more/better procedures. If IR and CR are low → can accept higher DR (do less testing). DR can never be zero — sampling means some items are never examined.
Ch34 — Audit Procedures — Full Toolkit
A — Analytical Procedures (ISA 520)
Compare financial data to: prior year, budget, industry averages, expectations. Calculate ratios and investigate unusual fluctuations. Example: GP margin dropped from 35% to 22% → investigate reason → possible misstatement of revenue or cost of sales.
E — Enquiry
Ask questions of management, staff, and third parties. Least reliable on its own — must be corroborated by other evidence. Example: Ask the warehouse manager about inventory obsolescence. Do not accept the answer alone — also physically inspect and check slow-moving items.
I — Inspection (Vouching & Tracing)
Examine documents, records, physical assets. Vouching: start from recorded entry → find supporting document (tests EXISTENCE). Tracing: start from source document → check it is recorded (tests COMPLETENESS). Example: Select 50 sales invoices from the revenue ledger → trace to dispatch notes and customer orders (existence). Select dispatch notes → trace to sales ledger (completeness).
O — Observation
Watch a process being performed by client staff. Limitation: behaviour may change when being observed. Example: Attend year-end inventory count — observe counting procedures, check for damaged goods, test counts independently. Observe controls operating in real time.
C — Confirmation (External)
Obtain evidence directly from third parties — highly reliable. Examples: Bank confirmation letter (confirms balances, loans, contingent liabilities). Debtor circularisation (confirms receivable balances). Lawyer letters (confirms litigation, contingencies). Supplier statements (confirms payable balances).
R — Recalculation & Reperformance
Recalculate: check mathematical accuracy of client's calculations. Reperformance: independently perform a procedure the client performs. Example: Recalculate depreciation charges, interest accruals, tax provisions, foreign currency translations. Reperform bank reconciliation independently.

Ch35 — The Four Functions That Must Be Separated
1. Authorisation
The person who APPROVES a transaction e.g. Manager who approves a purchase order or payment
2. Custody

FM (Financial Management) — Applied Skills

Ch22 — Cost of Equity — Two Models
CAPM (Capital Asset Pricing Model)
Ke = Rf + β(Rm − Rf) Rf = Risk-free rate (Government bond yield — typically 10-yr G-Sec rate in India) β (Beta) = Systematic risk of the share vs the market. β=1 moves with market. β>1 more volatile. β<1 less volatile. Rm = Expected market return (historical average stock market return) (Rm − Rf) = Equity Risk Premium (ERP) — extra return for taking equity risk vs risk-free
Dividend Growth Model (Gordon Growth Model)
Ke = D1/P0 + g D1 = Expected next dividend (D0 × (1+g)) P0 = Current share price (ex-dividend) g = Constant dividend growth rate (sustainable long-term growth) Used when: company pays regular dividends with stable growth rate
Ch22 — Cost of Debt — Formulas
Post-Tax Cost of Debt (Simple)
Kd (post-tax) = Interest Rate × (1 − Tax Rate) Example: 10% loan, 25% tax rate → Kd = 10% × (1−0.25) = 7.5% The tax deductibility of interest reduces the effective cost by the tax rate.
Yield to Maturity (YTM) — For Bonds/Debentures
YTM = approximate IRR of bond cash flows YTM ≈ [Annual Coupon + (Redemption Value − Market Price)/n] ÷ [(Redemption Value + Market Price)/2] Used when bond trades at a discount or premium to face value. Market price reflects current required return, not the coupon rate. Post-tax YTM = YTM × (1−tax rate).
Irredeemable Debt (Perpetual)
Kd = Annual Interest / Market Price of Debt × (1 − Tax Rate) For perpetual bonds or irredeemable debentures — no redemption date.
Ch22 — WACC Formula
WACC
WACC = Ke × [E/(E+D)] + Kd(1−T) × [D/(E+D)] Ke = Cost of Equity (from CAPM or DGM) Kd(1−T) = Post-tax Cost of Debt E = Market Value of Equity (shares outstanding × current share price) D = Market Value of Debt (market price of all debt instruments) E/(E+D) = Equity weight as proportion of total capital D/(E+D) = Debt weight as proportion of total capital Note: Always use MARKET values for weights — not book values
Ch22 — Investment Appraisal Methods — Formulas & Interpretation
Net Present Value (NPV)
NPV = Σ [Cash Flow_t / (1+r)^t] − Initial Investment r = discount rate (WACC). t = time period. Sum all discounted cash flows and deduct initial outlay. Decision rule: Accept if NPV > 0. Maximise NPV when choosing between projects. NPV is the gold standard — directly measures value created in absolute ₹ terms. Theoretically superior to all other methods.
Internal Rate of Return (IRR)
IRR = discount rate that makes NPV = 0 Calculated by trial and error or interpolation: IRR ≈ a + [NPVa/(NPVa−NPVb)] × (b−a) Decision rule: Accept if IRR > Cost of Capital (WACC). Expresses return as a %, easy to compare to cost of capital. Limitation: may give multiple IRRs for non-conventional cash flows. Does not rank mutually exclusive projects correctly (use NPV instead).
Payback Period
Payback = Year before full recovery + (Remaining cost ÷ Cash flow in recovery year) Decision rule: Accept if Payback Simple, intuitive, focuses on liquidity and risk. Major limitation: ignores time value of money and cash flows after payback. Not theoretically sound but widely used in practice for quick screening.
Discounted Payback Period
Same as payback but uses discounted cash flows (PV of each year's CF) Improvement on simple payback — accounts for time value of money. Still ignores cash flows after payback date. Better than simple payback but inferior to NPV.
Accounting Rate of Return (ARR)
ARR = Average Annual Profit (after depreciation) ÷ Average Investment × 100 Average Investment = (Initial Investment + Scrap Value) ÷ 2 Decision rule: Accept if ARR > target rate. Higher ARR = better. Uses accounting profit, not cash flow. Ignores time value of money. Easy to calculate. Widely used by non-finance managers but theoretically weak. ACCA FM: know its limitations.
Ch25 — EOQ Formula
Economic Order Quantity
EOQ = √[(2 × D × Co) ÷ Ch] D = Annual Demand (units); Co = Cost per Order; Ch = Holding Cost per unit per year
Total Annual Ordering Cost
(D ÷ EOQ) × Co
Total Annual Holding Cost
(EOQ ÷ 2) × Ch
Key Insight at EOQ
At the EOQ point, Total Ordering Cost EXACTLY EQUALS Total Holding Cost — this is the mathematical minimum of total inventory cost

Ch36 — Three Main Categories of Financial Risk
Market Risk
Risk of loss from adverse movements in market prices — interest rates, exchange rates, commodity prices, equity prices. Examples: Interest rate rise increases debt cost. Dollar strengthens vs Rupee — Indian exporter receives less in Rupee terms. Oil price spike increases production costs.
Credit Risk
Risk that a counterparty (customer, borrower, investment issuer) fails to meet their financial obligations. Examples: Customer defaults on ₹5Cr receivable. Bank's loan portfolio suffers defaults. Bond issuer defaults. Swap counterparty fails to make payment.
Liquidity Risk
Risk that the organisation cannot meet its financial obligations as they fall due — runs out of cash even if technically solvent. Examples: Company has large assets but cannot sell them fast enough to pay creditors. Bank faces sudden deposit withdrawals (bank run). Bond market seizes up — cannot roll over short-term debt.
Ch36 — Key Liquidity Ratios
Current Ratio
Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities Benchmark: >1 (assets exceed short-term obligations). Too high may indicate inefficient use of assets. Industry varies — retail often lower.
Quick Ratio (Acid Test)
(Current Assets − Inventory) ÷ Current Liabilities More conservative — excludes inventory (least liquid current asset). Benchmark: >1 for most industries.
Cash Ratio
(Cash + Short-term Investments) ÷ Current Liabilities Most conservative — only cash and near-cash. Shows ability to meet obligations with immediately available funds.
Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC)
Receivable Days + Inventory Days − Payable Days How long cash is tied up in the operating cycle. Lower = better. Negative CCC (e.g. Amazon, supermarkets) = customers pay before suppliers are paid — excellent liquidity position.
Ch36 — Risk Appetite, Tolerance & Capacity — Defined
Risk Appetite
The AMOUNT and TYPE of risk an organisation is WILLING to accept in pursuit of its objectives. Set by the Board. Expressed qualitatively ("we do not accept regulatory compliance risk") or quantitatively ("maximum credit loss of ₹50Cr per year"). Aligns risk-taking with strategy.
Risk Tolerance
The ACCEPTABLE VARIATION around risk appetite — the boundaries within which risk must stay. Example: Appetite = 5% NPL ratio. Tolerance = up to 6% before escalation required. Between 5–6% = monitor closely. Above 6% = breach → immediate action.
Risk Capacity
The MAXIMUM amount of risk the organisation CAN bear — before its survival is threatened. Determined by financial strength, regulatory capital, stakeholder expectations. Risk appetite should always be LESS than risk capacity — there must be a buffer.
Risk Attitude
The overall disposition of the organisation towards risk — Risk Averse, Risk Neutral, or Risk Seeking. A startup is risk-seeking (high growth, accept high risk). A bank is typically risk-averse (depositors' money, regulatory capital). A utility company is risk-neutral to averse.
Ch36 — Key Formulas & Concepts
Risk Score (Matrix)

Will the strategy deliver acceptable RETURNS (financial appraisal — NPV, ROI), acceptable RISK levels, and be acceptable to key STAKEHOLDERS (shareholders, employees, regulators)?

Ch38 — The Agency Problem — Core Framework
Principals
Shareholders — they OWN the company but don't manage it day-to-day Want: maximum long-term shareholder value, sustainable returns, transparency, dividends. Risk-tolerant (diversified portfolio). Long-term view.
Agents
Directors and Management — they RUN the company on behalf of shareholders May want: personal wealth maximisation, job security, prestige, empire building. Risk-averse (concentrated human capital in one company). Potentially short-term view (linked to bonus cycle).
Agency Costs
Costs arising from the separation of ownership and control Monitoring costs (audit, board oversight, shareholder meetings), Bonding costs (management incentive schemes, employment contracts), Residual loss (value destroyed by management self-interest even after monitoring).
Governance Solution
Mechanisms to align agent behaviour with principal interests Independent board oversight, performance-linked remuneration, shareholder voting rights, audit and disclosure requirements, regulatory enforcement. Goal: minimise residual agency loss.

3-4: Vision & Communication

Remove obstacles and empower broad-based action; generate visible SHORT-TERM WINS to build momentum and credibility

Consolidate gains and produce more change, rather than declaring victory too early; anchor new approaches firmly in the organisational culture

Ch33 — FCFF Build — From P&L and Balance Sheet
EBIT (Earnings Before Interest & Tax)
Revenue − COGS − Operating Expenses − D&A This is operating profit — the return generated by the business before financing costs and tax.
NOPAT (Net Operating Profit After Tax)
EBIT × (1 − Tax Rate) Tax-adjusted operating profit. Removes the tax benefit of debt interest — treats firm as if all equity financed (tax effect captured in WACC separately).
+ Depreciation & Amortisation
Add back D&A (non-cash charge) D&A reduced EBIT but did not consume cash — we add it back to get to cash earnings.
− Capital Expenditure (Capex)
Subtract investment in PP&E and other long-term assets Cash actually spent on maintaining and growing the asset base. From cash flow statement or capex schedule.
− / + Change in Working Capital
−ΔInventory − ΔReceivables + ΔPayables Growing businesses often need more working capital — this consumes cash. Shrinking WC (e.g. reducing receivables) releases cash.
= Free Cash Flow to Firm (FCFF)
NOPAT + D&A − Capex − ΔNWC The cash the business generates after all operating investments. Available to be distributed to both debt and equity holders — hence "to firm."
Ch33 — WACC Formula — Recap
WACC
WACC = Ke × [E/(E+D)] + Kd(1−t) × [D/(E+D)] Ke = Cost of Equity (from CAPM: Rf + β(Rm−Rf)) Kd = Pre-tax cost of debt (interest rate or YTM on debt) t = Corporate tax rate E = Market value of equity | D = Market value of debt Always use MARKET values for weights, never book values.
Ch33 — Terminal Value — Two Methods
Method 1 — Gordon Growth Model (GGM)
TV = FCFFn × (1 + g) ÷ (WACC − g) FCFFn = Free Cash Flow in final forecast year g = Long-term sustainable growth rate (usually GDP growth or inflation rate — 3–5% typically) WACC − g = the "spread" — must be positive (WACC must exceed g or formula breaks) Key rule: g should never exceed long-run GDP growth — no company can grow faster than the economy forever
Method 2 — Exit Multiple
TV = Final Year EBITDA × EV/EBITDA Multiple Multiple = current trading multiple of comparable companies (EV/EBITDA, EV/EBIT, P/E) More pragmatic — anchored to real market pricing But introduces circular logic: if multiples compress (as in a bear market), TV falls dramatically Commonly used by investment bankers and PE analysts in LBO models
Ch33 — Net Asset Value (NAV) Calculation
Book NAV (Accounting)
Total Equity on Balance Sheet = Total Assets − Total Liabilities At historical cost. Quick but not meaningful for valuation purposes — ignores fair value changes in assets.
Adjusted NAV (Fair Value)
Fair Value of All Assets − Fair Value of All Liabilities Revalue each asset class to current market/fair value (PP&E, investments, real estate, intangibles). Adjust liabilities for current market value of debt. This gives the true economic net asset value per share.
NAV Per Share
Adjusted NAV ÷ Shares Outstanding Compare to current share price. P/B = Share Price ÷ NAV per share. P/B < 1 means market prices company below its net assets — unusual for profitable companies, common for distressed or capital-destroying businesses.
Ch33 — The EV ↔ Equity Value Bridge
Enterprise Value (EV)
EV = Market Cap + Total Debt + Preference Shares + Minority Interest − Cash & Cash Equivalents EV is the TOTAL value of the business — what you would pay to buy the whole company (all equity + all debt) and then receive the cash sitting in it. Capital structure neutral.
From EV → Equity Value
Equity Value = EV − Total Debt − Preference Shares − Minority Interest + Cash Alternatively: EV − Net Debt (where Net Debt = Total Debt − Cash). This is what is left for equity shareholders after all debt obligations are accounted for.
From Equity Value → Share Price
Intrinsic Share Price = Equity Value ÷ Diluted Shares Outstanding Use DILUTED shares — include options, warrants, convertibles that could dilute equity. This is what the stock is worth per share — compare to market price.
Ch33 — Complete Formula Sheet
Free Cash Flow to Firm (FCFF)
FCFF = EBIT × (1−t) + D&A − Capex − ΔNWC Or: NOPAT + D&A − Capex − ΔNWC
WACC
WACC = Ke × [E/(E+D)] + Kd(1−t) × [D/(E+D)] Always: market value weights, post-tax cost of debt
Cost of Equity (CAPM)
Ke = Rf + β(Rm − Rf)
Terminal Value — Gordon Growth Model
TV = FCFFn × (1+g) ÷ (WACC − g) Condition: g < WACC always
Terminal Value — Exit Multiple
TV = Final Year EBITDA × EV/EBITDA Multiple
PV of any Cash Flow
PV = Cash Flow ÷ (1 + WACC)^n n = number of years from today
Enterprise Value (from market)
EV = Market Cap + Total Debt + Pref Shares + Minority Interest − Cash
Equity Value (from EV)
Equity Value = EV − Net Debt Net Debt = Total Debt − Cash
Intrinsic Share Price
Share Price = Equity Value ÷ Diluted Shares Outstanding
P/E Ratio
P/E = Share Price ÷ Earnings Per Share (EPS)
EV/EBITDA Multiple
EV/EBITDA = Enterprise Value ÷ EBITDA
Price to Book (P/B)
P/B = Market Cap ÷ Book Value of Equity Or: Share Price ÷ Book Value Per Share
Implied P/B from Fundamentals
P/B = (ROE − g) ÷ (Ke − g) Higher ROE → deserves higher P/B. If ROE = Ke → P/B = 1
NAV Per Share
NAV/Share = (Fair Value of Assets − Fair Value of Liabilities) ÷ Shares Outstanding
Margin of Safety
Margin of Safety = (Intrinsic Value − Market Price) ÷ Intrinsic Value × 100%
EV/Revenue Multiple
EV/Revenue = Enterprise Value ÷ Revenue — useful for early-stage or loss-making companies where EBITDA/EBIT multiples aren't meaningful
EV/EBIT Multiple
EV/EBIT = Enterprise Value ÷ EBIT — captures depreciation/capex differences that EV/EBITDA ignores, useful for capital-intensive businesses

Ch29 — Materiality Concepts
Overall Materiality
The threshold above which misstatements, individually or in aggregate, could reasonably influence users' economic decisions — typically set as a % of a benchmark (revenue, profit, or total assets, depending on the entity's nature)
Performance Materiality
Set LOWER than overall materiality — a buffer that reduces the risk that the sum of many individually immaterial misstatements could aggregate to a material amount
Specific Materiality
A LOWER materiality level applied to particular classes of transactions/balances where even smaller misstatements could reasonably influence users (e.g. related party transactions, director remuneration)
Ch29 — The Complete Opinion Decision Framework
Unmodified (Unqualified)
Financial statements give a true and fair view in all material respects — no significant issues found
Qualified ("Except For")
Used when a misstatement or scope limitation is MATERIAL but NOT PERVASIVE — the issue is significant but contained/isolated
Adverse
Used when a misstatement is BOTH MATERIAL AND PERVASIVE — the financial statements as a whole are misleading, not just one isolated area
Disclaimer of Opinion
Used when a SCOPE LIMITATION is both material and pervasive — the auditor simply cannot obtain enough evidence to form ANY opinion

=AVERAGEIF(range, criteria, average_range) =AVERAGEIFS(average_range, range1, criteria1, range2, criteria2) Finance example: =AVERAGEIF(Region, "South", DaysToCollect) → Average debtor days for Southern region only
IFERROR / IFNA
=IFERROR(formula, value_if_error) — catch ANY error =IFNA(formula, value_if_na) — catch only #N/A errors Example: =IFERROR(VLOOKUP(A1,Table,2,FALSE), "Not Found") Essential in financial models — prevents ugly #REF!, #N/A, #DIV/0! errors from showing. But be careful: IFERROR can hide genuine formula errors. Only use when errors are expected (e.g. no match found).

=DATE(2025,12,31) — create a date from year/month/day =YEAR(A1) / =MONTH(A1) / =DAY(A1) — extract components =DATE(YEAR(A1),MONTH(A1)+1,0) — last day of current month (very useful for financial period end dates)

Ch39 — Financial Functions — Full Reference with Finance Context
NPV (Net Present Value)
=NPV(rate, value1, value2, ...) + initial_investment CRITICAL: Excel's NPV assumes cash flows start at END of period 1 (not period 0). Always add period 0 investment separately. Example: Initial investment ₹100Cr (now), cash flows ₹30Cr, ₹40Cr, ₹50Cr, ₹60Cr, discount rate 12%: =NPV(12%, 30, 40, 50, 60) + (-100) → correct NPV NOT: =NPV(12%, -100, 30, 40, 50, 60) → WRONG — this discounts the initial investment which should not be discounted. If NPV > 0 → Accept project. If NPV < 0 → Reject. Links directly to Chapter 22 (Corporate Finance) and Chapter 33 (Valuation).
XNPV (NPV with Actual Dates)
=XNPV(rate, values, dates) Example: =XNPV(12%, B2:B6, A2:A6) where A2:A6 are actual dates and B2:B6 are cash flows More accurate than NPV when cash flows don't occur at exactly equal intervals. Use XNPV in real financial models — NPV assumes equal periods.
IRR (Internal Rate of Return)
=IRR(values, [guess]) Example: =IRR({-100, 30, 40, 50, 60}) → rate at which NPV = 0 Initial investment must be NEGATIVE. Values must include at least one negative (outflow) and one positive (inflow). If IRR > cost of capital (hurdle rate) → Accept. Multiple IRRs possible if cash flows change sign more than once — be careful.
XIRR (IRR with Actual Dates)
=XIRR(values, dates, [guess]) Example: =XIRR(B2:B6, A2:A6) — IRR using actual payment dates Use XIRR instead of IRR in real models — more accurate when cash flows don't occur at exactly annual intervals. Standard in PE/VC investment returns modelling.
PV (Present Value)
=PV(rate, nper, pmt, [fv], [type]) Example: PV of ₹10,000 received annually for 5 years at 10%: =PV(10%, 5, -10000) → ₹37,908 rate = periodic rate. nper = number of periods. pmt = periodic payment (negative = outflow). fv = future value. type = 0 (end of period, default) or 1 (beginning of period).
FV (Future Value)
=FV(rate, nper, pmt, [pv], [type]) Example: FV of ₹10,000 invested annually for 10 years at 8%: =FV(8%, 10, -10000) → ₹1,44,866 FV of lump sum: =FV(8%, 10, 0, -100000) → FV of ₹1L invested today for 10 years at 8%. Useful for retirement planning, savings projections.
PMT (Loan Payment)
=PMT(rate, nper, pv, [fv], [type]) Example: Monthly EMI for ₹50L home loan at 8.5% for 20 years: =PMT(8.5%/12, 20*12, -5000000) → ₹43,391 per month rate must match period — for monthly payments, divide annual rate by 12. nper = total number of payments (years × 12 for monthly). Used for loan amortisation schedules, lease payment calculations.
RATE / NPER
=RATE(nper, pmt, pv) — find the interest rate given payments =NPER(rate, pmt, pv) — find number of periods to repay a loan Example: =RATE(60, -5000, 200000) → monthly rate on loan of ₹2L, 60 monthly payments of ₹5,000 =NPER(8%/12, -10000, 500000) → months to repay ₹5L at 8% with ₹10,000/month payments
Ch40 — Essential DAX Measures for Finance
Basic Aggregations
Total Revenue = SUM(Sales[Revenue]) Total Cost = SUM(Sales[Cost]) Gross Profit = [Total Revenue] - [Total Cost] GP Margin % = DIVIDE([Gross Profit], [Total Revenue], 0) Always use DIVIDE() instead of / to handle division by zero gracefully.
Year-over-Year Comparison
Revenue LY = CALCULATE([Total Revenue], SAMEPERIODLASTYEAR(Dates[Date])) YoY Growth = [Total Revenue] - [Revenue LY] YoY Growth % = DIVIDE([YoY Growth], [Revenue LY], 0) SAMEPERIODLASTYEAR is a time intelligence function — requires a proper Date table marked as Date Table.
Year-to-Date (YTD)
Revenue YTD = CALCULATE([Total Revenue], DATESYTD(Dates[Date])) Revenue YTD LY = CALCULATE([Total Revenue], DATESYTD(SAMEPERIODLASTYEAR(Dates[Date]))) For financial year YTD (not calendar year): DATESYTD(Dates[Date], "31-03") — ends March 31 (India financial year).
Budget Variance
Budget = SUM(Budget[BudgetAmount]) Variance = [Total Revenue] - [Budget] Variance % = DIVIDE([Variance], [Budget], 0) Variance Flag = IF([Variance %] < -0.05, "⚠️ Below Budget", "✅ On Track")
CALCULATE — The Most Important DAX Function
CALCULATE(expression, filter1, filter2, ...) Revenue North = CALCULATE([Total Revenue], Region[Region] = "North") Revenue excl Returns = CALCULATE([Total Revenue], Sales[Type] <> "Return") Revenue Last Month = CALCULATE([Total Revenue], PREVIOUSMONTH(Dates[Date])) CALCULATE modifies the filter context — it's the engine behind 80% of all advanced DAX. Master this and you can build almost any measure.
ALL — Remove Filters
% of Total Revenue = DIVIDE([Total Revenue], CALCULATE([Total Revenue], ALL(Sales))) ALL() removes ALL filters from a table — giving the grand total regardless of slicers. DIVIDE([Revenue], [Revenue with ALL]) gives % contribution of each row to the total. Essential for % share calculations.
RANKX — Ranking
Revenue Rank = RANKX(ALL(Products[Product]), [Total Revenue],,DESC) Ranks each product by revenue across the entire product table (ALL removes filter). Use to show Top 10 products, rank sales reps by performance, rank cost centres by spend.
SWITCH — Multiple Conditions
Performance Rating = SWITCH(TRUE(),   [Variance %] >= 0.1, "🟢 Excellent",   [Variance %] >= 0, "🟡 On Target",   [Variance %] >= -0.1, "🟠 Watch",   "🔴 Critical") Cleaner than nested IF statements. SWITCH(TRUE(),...) evaluates each condition in order and returns the first TRUE result.
Moving Average
3-Month Moving Avg Revenue = CALCULATE([Total Revenue], DATESINPERIOD(Dates[Date], LASTDATE(Dates[Date]), -3, MONTH)) / 3 DATESINPERIOD defines a rolling window of 3 months ending at the current date. Dividing by 3 gives the average. Use for smoothing volatile monthly data in dashboards.

Ch41 — Core SQL Commands — Finance Reference
SELECT — Retrieve Data
SELECT Revenue, Cost, Region, Month FROM Sales WHERE Year = 2024 AND Region = 'North' ORDER BY Revenue DESC; The most fundamental SQL statement. SELECT what columns, FROM which table, WHERE conditions, ORDER BY for sorting. Like a filtered, sorted PivotTable — but running directly on the database.
GROUP BY + Aggregate Functions
SELECT Region, SUM(Revenue) AS Total_Revenue,     AVG(Margin) AS Avg_Margin,     COUNT(*) AS Transaction_Count FROM Sales WHERE Year = 2024 GROUP BY Region HAVING SUM(Revenue) > 1000000 ORDER BY Total_Revenue DESC; GROUP BY aggregates rows — like a PivotTable. SUM, AVG, COUNT, MIN, MAX are aggregate functions. HAVING filters AFTER grouping (WHERE filters before). Essential for financial summaries by dimension.
JOIN — Combine Tables
SELECT s.Invoice_No, s.Revenue, c.Customer_Name, c.Region, p.Product_Name FROM Sales s INNER JOIN Customers c ON s.Customer_ID = c.Customer_ID INNER JOIN Products p ON s.Product_ID = p.Product_ID WHERE s.Date >= '2024-01-01'; JOIN combines data from multiple tables using a matching key — like VLOOKUP but for entire tables simultaneously. INNER JOIN: only matching rows. LEFT JOIN: all rows from left table + matches from right. This is the SQL equivalent of building a data model.
CASE WHEN — Conditional Logic
SELECT Invoice_No, Amount, CASE   WHEN Days_Overdue = 0 THEN 'Current'   WHEN Days_Overdue   WHEN Days_Overdue   ELSE '60+ Days' END AS Aging_Bucket FROM Receivables; CASE WHEN is SQL's IF statement — creates conditional categories. Essential for aged receivables analysis, risk banding, performance rating. Same logic as Excel's IFS function.
Window Functions — Running Totals & Rankings
SELECT Month, Revenue, SUM(Revenue) OVER (ORDER BY Month) AS Running_Total, LAG(Revenue, 1) OVER (ORDER BY Month) AS Prev_Month_Revenue, Revenue - LAG(Revenue,1) OVER (ORDER BY Month) AS MoM_Change, RANK() OVER (PARTITION BY Region ORDER BY Revenue DESC) AS Revenue_Rank FROM Sales; Window functions perform calculations across rows related to current row — without collapsing to summary. Running totals, month-over-month changes, ranking within partitions — all in one query. OVER() defines the window. Essential for time-series financial analysis in SQL.
CTEs — Readable Complex Queries
WITH Monthly_Revenue AS (   SELECT Month, SUM(Revenue) AS Revenue   FROM Sales GROUP BY Month ), Revenue_with_Growth AS (   SELECT Month, Revenue,   LAG(Revenue) OVER (ORDER BY Month) AS Prev_Revenue   FROM Monthly_Revenue ) SELECT Month, Revenue, ROUND((Revenue - Prev_Revenue) / Prev_Revenue * 100, 2) AS Growth_Pct FROM Revenue_with_Growth; CTEs (Common Table Expressions) create temporary named result sets — like named ranges in Excel. Break complex queries into readable, manageable steps. Much cleaner than deeply nested subqueries.
Ch41 — Key Statistical Concepts for Finance
Descriptive Statistics
Mean (Average): Sum ÷ Count — central tendency Median: Middle value — better than mean for skewed data (e.g. income distribution) Standard Deviation: Average distance from mean — measures spread/volatility Variance: Standard Deviation² — used in portfolio theory Skewness: Asymmetry of distribution — positive skew (tail right), negative skew (tail left) Finance use: Mean and StDev of returns for investment analysis. Median for customer spend (not mean — one large customer skews mean dramatically).
Correlation
Correlation coefficient (r): -1 to +1 +1 = perfect positive correlation (both move together) 0 = no linear relationship -1 = perfect negative correlation (one goes up, other goes down) Finance uses: Portfolio diversification (combine negatively correlated assets). Regression (identify key revenue drivers). Fraud detection (transactions that don't correlate with normal patterns). WARNING: Correlation ≠ Causation. Always ask WHY two things correlate before concluding one causes the other.
Linear Regression
Y = a + bX (+ error) Y = dependent variable (what we want to predict) X = independent variable (the driver/predictor) b = slope (how much Y changes for one unit increase in X) a = intercept (value of Y when X = 0) R² = goodness of fit (0–1, higher = model explains more variance) Finance uses: Revenue forecasting (Y=Revenue, X=GDP growth or advertising spend). Cost modelling (Y=total cost, X=production volume). Risk modelling (Y=default probability, X=leverage ratio). In Excel: Data → Data Analysis → Regression. In Python: sklearn LinearRegression.
Time Series Analysis
Components of time series: Trend: Long-term direction (revenue growing at 10% CAGR) Seasonality: Regular pattern (retail revenue peaks in December every year) Cyclicality: Longer irregular cycles (economic cycle, 5-10 years) Irregular/Noise: Random variation not explained by above Finance use: Isolate seasonality to understand true underlying trend. Compare this December to last December (same seasonal position) rather than to November. Moving averages smooth out noise to reveal trend.
Probability & Expected Value
Expected Value = Σ (Probability × Outcome) Example: New product launch Success (40% probability) → ₹200Cr NPV Failure (60% probability) → -₹50Cr NPV Expected Value = (0.4 × 200) + (0.6 × -50) = ₹50Cr Foundation of scenario analysis, risk management, and decision trees. ACCA AFM: expected values in project appraisal questions. Risk management: expected loss = Probability of Default × Loss Given Default (credit risk).
