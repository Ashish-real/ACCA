Financial accounting tells the world what happened. Cost accounting tells management why it happened — and what to do about it. It is the internal language of business — used by CFOs, FP&A teams, and operations managers to understand where money is being spent, which products are profitable, and how to price goods and services.

"You cannot manage what you cannot measure. Cost accounting gives you the measurements that drive every management decision."
5.1
What is Cost Accounting — and Why It Matters

Cost accounting is the branch of accounting that records, classifies, analyses, and controls costs within a business. Unlike financial accounting (which reports to outsiders), cost accounting is primarily for internal management use.

🏭
What Cost Accounting Does
  • Tracks the cost of every product, service, or department
  • Helps set selling prices correctly
  • Identifies wastage and inefficiency
  • Supports budgeting and forecasting
  • Helps decide: make or buy? continue or stop?
Internal Decision Tool
🆚
Cost Accounting vs Financial Accounting
  • Financial A/C: for external users (investors, banks)
  • Cost A/C: for internal users (management, CFO)
  • Financial A/C: follows IFRS — mandatory
  • Cost A/C: no fixed rules — flexible for each business
  • Both feed into ACCA MA and PM papers
Internal vs External
💼
Who Uses Cost Accounting in MNCs
  • CFO — overall cost structure and margins
  • FP&A team — budgets, variance analysis
  • Operations — efficiency and waste reduction
  • Sales — pricing decisions
  • Supply chain — procurement cost control
Every MNC Department

5.3
Fixed vs Variable Costs
The Most Important Cost Behaviour

Understanding how costs behave as output changes is the most fundamental concept in cost accounting. It drives pricing, budgeting, and break-even analysis.

📌
Fixed Costs
  • Do NOT change with level of output/activity
  • Remain constant over a relevant range
  • Examples: rent, insurance, senior salaries, depreciation
  • Per unit cost DECREASES as output increases
  • Key insight: spreading fixed costs = economies of scale
Constant Total
📈
Variable Costs
  • Change directly with level of output
  • Double output → double variable cost
  • Examples: raw materials, direct labour (piece rate), packaging
  • Per unit cost stays CONSTANT
  • Key insight: contribution = Revenue − Variable Costs
Changes with Output
〰️
Semi-Variable (Mixed) Costs
  • Has both a fixed and a variable component
  • Examples: electricity bill (fixed standing charge + usage)
  • Phone bill, machine maintenance costs
  • Separated using High-Low method
  • Important for budgeting accuracy
Fixed + Variable

High-Low Method — Separating Semi-Variable Costs

Example: Electricity Bill for Ashish Manufacturing

MonthUnits ProducedElectricity Cost (₹)
Highest activity (March)10,000 units₹85,000
Lowest activity (July)4,000 units₹49,000
Step 1 — Variable cost per unit:
(₹85,000 − ₹49,000) ÷ (10,000 − 4,000) = ₹36,000 ÷ 6,000 = ₹6 per unit

Step 2 — Fixed cost:
₹85,000 − (₹6 × 10,000) = ₹85,000 − ₹60,000 = ₹25,000 fixed

Formula: Total Cost = ₹25,000 + (₹6 × units produced)
5.4
Direct vs Indirect Costs (Overheads)

This classification answers: can we directly trace this cost to a specific product or service? This is critical for accurate product costing and pricing.

🎯
Direct Costs
  • Can be directly traced to a specific product/job
  • Direct Materials — raw materials in the product
  • Direct Labour — workers who make the product
  • Direct Expenses — specific costs for that product
  • Together = Prime Cost
Traceable to Product
🌐
Indirect Costs (Overheads)
  • Cannot be directly traced — shared across products
  • Factory rent, supervisor salaries, machine depreciation
  • Must be allocated or absorbed into products
  • Three types: Production, Admin, Selling overheads
  • Major source of complexity in product costing
Shared — Must Allocate
📐
Overhead Absorption Rate (OAR)
  • Method to share overheads across products
  • OAR = Budgeted Overhead ÷ Budgeted Activity
  • Activity base: labour hours, machine hours, units
  • Over/under absorption arises when actual ≠ budget
  • Major topic in ACCA MA paper
OAR Formula
Prime Cost = Direct Materials + Direct Labour + Direct Expenses
Production Cost = Prime Cost + Production Overheads
Total Cost = Production Cost + Admin + Selling + Distribution Overheads
The cost build-up — from raw materials to total cost of a product
5.5
Product Costs vs Period Costs
📦
Product Costs
  • Costs attached to units of inventory
  • Remain on Balance Sheet until product is sold
  • Hit P&L only when inventory is sold (as COGS)
  • Includes: Direct Materials, Direct Labour, Production Overhead
  • Also called "inventoriable costs"
Balance Sheet First
📅
Period Costs
  • Costs expensed in the period they are incurred
  • Not attached to inventory — hit P&L immediately
  • Includes: Admin expenses, selling costs, finance costs
  • Even if nothing is sold, period costs are still expensed
  • Cannot be deferred to future periods
P&L Immediately
💡 Why This Distinction Matters
Under Absorption Costing (IFRS requirement for inventory under IAS 2), production overheads are product costs — they stay in inventory until sold. Under Marginal Costing (used internally for decisions), all fixed costs are period costs. This causes profit differences between the two methods — a key ACCA MA exam topic.

5.7
Absorption Costing vs Marginal Costing
The Big Debate in Cost Accounting

These are the two main approaches to product costing. They give different profit figures — and understanding why is a classic ACCA exam question.

FeatureAbsorption CostingMarginal Costing
Fixed Production OverheadsIncluded in product cost (absorbed)Treated as period cost — expensed immediately
Inventory ValuationHigher — includes fixed overhead per unitLower — only variable costs in inventory
Profit when production > salesHigher profit (some fixed cost in inventory)Lower profit (all fixed cost expensed)
Profit when production = salesSame profit as marginal costingSame profit as absorption costing
IFRS Requirement (IAS 2)Required for external reportingNot allowed for external reporting
Best Used ForFinancial statements, inventory valuationInternal decisions — pricing, CVP analysis
Key MetricProfit per unitContribution per unit
Contribution = Revenue − Variable Costs
Profit (Marginal) = Contribution − Fixed Costs
Profit (Absorption) = Revenue − Total Absorbed Cost
Contribution is the key concept in marginal costing — used for break-even, pricing, and decision-making
5.8
Break-Even Analysis
When Does the Business Start Making Profit?

Break-even analysis answers: how many units must we sell before we start making a profit? It is one of the most practical tools in management accounting — used in every business from a street chai stall to a multinational corporation.

Contribution per unit = Selling Price − Variable Cost per unit

Break-Even Point (units) = Fixed Costs ÷ Contribution per unit

Margin of Safety = Actual Sales − Break-Even Sales
The three core break-even formulas — memorise these for ACCA MA

Margin of Safety shows how much sales can fall before the business makes a loss. Higher margin of safety = lower risk.

📌 C/S Ratio (Contribution to Sales Ratio)
C/S Ratio = Contribution ÷ Revenue × 100 = ₹3,500 ÷ ₹5,000 × 100 = 70%

This means for every ₹100 of revenue, ₹70 goes towards covering fixed costs and then profit. Used to quickly calculate break-even revenue: Fixed Costs ÷ C/S Ratio = ₹3,50,000 ÷ 0.70 = ₹5,00,000 ✅
5.9
Job, Batch & Process Costing
Costing Systems for Different Industries

Different industries use different costing systems. The choice depends on how the product or service is produced. As a finance professional in any MNC, you will encounter one or more of these systems.

🔧
Job Costing
  • Each job/order is unique — costed separately
  • Costs tracked per individual job using a Job Card
  • Examples: construction projects, custom furniture, legal cases
  • Direct costs easily traced; overheads absorbed via OAR
  • Big 4 audit — each client engagement is a "job"
Unique Products/Services
📦
Batch Costing
  • Similar to job costing but for a batch of identical items
  • Total batch cost calculated, then ÷ units in batch
  • Examples: printing, bakeries, clothing manufacturers
  • Cost per unit = Total Batch Cost ÷ Batch Size
  • Efficient for repetitive production in groups
Identical Batches
🏭
Process Costing
  • Used for continuous mass production processes
  • Costs averaged across all units produced
  • Examples: oil refining, chemicals, food processing, cement
  • Concept of equivalent units for WIP valuation
  • Normal loss vs abnormal loss/gain — key exam topic
Mass Production
🎯
Activity-Based Costing (ABC)
  • Modern approach — overheads assigned by activities
  • Different products use resources differently
  • Identifies true cost drivers (not just labour hours)
  • More accurate than traditional absorption costing
  • Used in Big 4 advisory and MNC finance teams
Modern · Accurate

Common Mistakes, Flashcards & Interview Q&A

Part A — Common Mistakes (Don't Repeat These)

❌ MISTAKE 1
Confusing Fixed Costs "in Total" vs "Per Unit"
Fixed costs are fixed in TOTAL, not per unit — fixed cost per unit actually falls as volume rises (same fixed cost spread over more units). Students often wrongly treat fixed cost per unit as a constant, breaking break-even and costing calculations.
❌ MISTAKE 2
Using Absorption Costing Profit for Break-Even Analysis
Break-even and CVP (cost-volume-profit) analysis rely on CONTRIBUTION (marginal costing), not absorption costing profit. Fixed overheads absorbed into inventory distort the true break-even point — always use marginal costing logic for break-even questions.
❌ MISTAKE 3
Mixing Up Product Costs and Period Costs
Product costs (direct materials, direct labour, production overheads) attach to inventory and hit the P&L only when sold. Period costs (admin, selling, distribution) are expensed immediately in the period incurred, regardless of sales. Misclassifying a period cost as a product cost distorts both inventory value and profit timing.
❌ MISTAKE 4
Forgetting Absorption and Marginal Costing Give the Same Profit When Production = Sales
The two methods only diverge when opening/closing inventory levels change. When production equals sales (no inventory movement), both methods produce identical profit — a frequently tested reconciliation point.
❌ MISTAKE 5
Wrong Formula Direction for Break-Even Point
Break-Even Point = Fixed Costs ÷ Contribution per unit — NOT Fixed Costs ÷ Selling Price, and not Contribution ÷ Fixed Costs. Getting the ratio inverted is one of the most common calculation slip-ups under exam time pressure.
❌ MISTAKE 6
Ignoring Semi-Variable Costs
Some costs (e.g. electricity with a fixed line-rent plus per-unit charge) have both a fixed and variable component. Treating them as purely fixed or purely variable understates the accuracy of cost-volume-profit analysis — the High-Low method is used to split these.

QuestionWhat They Are TestingModel Answer
"What's the difference between fixed and variable costs, and why does it matter?"Foundational cost behaviour understandingFixed costs remain constant in total regardless of activity level (e.g. rent), while variable costs change directly with volume (e.g. raw materials). This distinction matters because it drives break-even analysis, pricing decisions, and understanding how profit changes as sales volume changes — a core input for budgeting and forecasting.
"Explain break-even analysis and why a business would use it."Practical application of cost accountingBreak-even analysis identifies the sales volume at which total revenue equals total costs — the point of zero profit or loss. Businesses use it to set minimum sales targets, evaluate the risk of new products, and understand how sensitive profit is to volume changes, especially useful before launching a new product or service.
"When would you use marginal costing instead of absorption costing?"Depth of understanding — not just definitions but applicationMarginal costing is better for short-term internal decisions — pricing special orders, make-or-buy decisions, break-even analysis — because it isolates contribution and avoids fixed cost allocation distortions. Absorption costing is required for external financial reporting under IAS 2, as it matches all production costs to the units they relate to.
