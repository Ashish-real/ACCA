Cost accounting (Chapter 5) tells you what things cost. Management accounting takes that information and uses it to plan, control, and make decisions. It is the intelligence system of every organisation — producing the reports, budgets, and analyses that drive every strategic decision from the boardroom to the shop floor.

"Management accounting is the difference between a business that reacts to problems and one that anticipates and prevents them."
6.1
What is Management Accounting

Management accounting is the process of preparing financial and non-financial information for internal decision-makers. It combines cost accounting data with strategic thinking to help management plan, control, and improve performance.

🎯
3 Core Purposes
  • Planning: Setting budgets, targets, and strategies
  • Control: Comparing actual vs budget, taking corrective action
  • Decision Making: Make or buy, pricing, product mix, invest or not
  • All three are tested in ACCA MA and PM papers
Plan · Control · Decide
🔄
MA vs Financial Accounting
  • No legal requirement — flexible format
  • Future-focused (budgets, forecasts)
  • Both financial and non-financial data
  • Weekly/monthly — not just annual
  • Confidential — not published externally
Internal · Flexible
💼
MA in Your MNC Career
  • FP&A teams prepare management accounts monthly
  • CFO uses MA reports for board presentations
  • Budget vs actual variance reports — every month
  • Big 4 advisory teams use MA tools for client projects
  • ₹30–80 LPA roles built on MA skills
High-Value Career Skill
6.2
The Planning & Control Cycle
How Organisations Run

Every well-run organisation — from a Big 4 firm to a FinTech startup — follows a continuous planning and control cycle. Management accounting sits at the heart of this cycle.

🏔️
1. Set Objectives
  • Define what the organisation wants to achieve
  • Profit targets, market share, growth rate
  • SMART goals — Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound
🗺️
2. Plan (Budget)
  • Create a financial plan to achieve objectives
  • Revenue budget, cost budget, capital expenditure
  • Master budget = all budgets combined
⚙️
3. Execute
  • Implement the plan — run operations
  • Track actual results as they happen
  • Record transactions in accounting systems
📊
4. Measure & Compare
  • Compare actual results to budget
  • Calculate variances (differences)
  • Prepare management reports for decision-makers
🔧
5. Investigate & Control
  • Investigate significant variances
  • Understand root causes — good or bad?
  • Take corrective action where needed
🔄
6. Revise & Repeat
  • Update plans based on learnings
  • Rolling forecasts replace outdated budgets
  • Cycle repeats — monthly, quarterly, annually
6.3
Budgeting — Types & Purpose
The Financial Roadmap

A budget is a quantified plan expressed in financial terms for a defined period. It is the most important management accounting tool — used by every organisation to allocate resources, set targets, and measure performance.

Budget TypeWhat It IsBest Used When
Incremental BudgetLast year's budget + % increase for inflation/growthStable environments, low time pressure
Zero-Based Budget (ZBB)Start from zero — every cost must be justified freshCost-cutting drives, new organisations
Rolling BudgetContinuously updated — always 12 months aheadUncertain environments, fast-changing businesses
Flexed BudgetBudget adjusted to actual activity level achievedVariance analysis — fair performance comparison
Activity-Based BudgetBudget built around cost drivers and activitiesComplex organisations, service businesses
⚡ Incremental vs Zero-Based — Key Exam Debate
Incremental budgeting is fast and simple but perpetuates waste — inefficiencies from last year are automatically carried forward.

Zero-based budgeting eliminates waste and forces managers to justify every rupee — but is time-consuming and can kill morale.

ACCA MA and PM examiners love asking you to evaluate which approach suits a given organisation. Always consider: stability, time available, cost of process, and organisational culture.

6.5
Standard Costing & Variance Analysis
The Deep Dive

Standard costing sets predetermined costs for materials, labour, and overheads. Variance analysis then compares actual costs to these standards to identify exactly where and why performance differed from the plan.

Variance = Standard Cost − Actual Cost
Positive = Favourable (F)  |  Negative = Adverse (A)
The core variance formula — applies to every type of variance

The Key Variances — ACCA MA Must Know

VarianceFormulaWhat It Reveals
Material Price Variance(Standard Price − Actual Price) × Actual QuantityDid we pay more or less than planned per kg/unit?
Material Usage Variance(Standard Qty − Actual Qty) × Standard PriceDid we use more or less material than planned?
Labour Rate Variance(Standard Rate − Actual Rate) × Actual HoursDid we pay workers more or less per hour?
Labour Efficiency Variance(Standard Hours − Actual Hours) × Standard RateDid workers take more or fewer hours than planned?
Fixed Overhead Volume(Actual Output − Budgeted Output) × Std Fixed OH/unitDid we produce more or less than planned?
Sales Price Variance(Actual Price − Standard Price) × Actual VolumeDid we sell at a higher or lower price than planned?
Sales Volume Variance(Actual Volume − Budgeted Volume) × Standard MarginDid we sell more or fewer units than planned?

Complete Variance Example — Ashish Manufacturing

Material Variances — Product: Finance Study Kit

Standard: 2 kg per unit at ₹50 per kg
Actual: Produced 500 units, used 1,050 kg at ₹48 per kg

Material Price Variance:
(₹50 − ₹48) × 1,050 kg = ₹2 × 1,050 = ₹2,100 Favourable ✅
(We paid less per kg than planned — good!)

Material Usage Variance:
Standard qty for 500 units = 500 × 2 = 1,000 kg
(1,000 − 1,050) × ₹50 = −50 × ₹50 = ₹2,500 Adverse ❌
(We used 50 kg more than planned — bad!)

Total Material Cost Variance = ₹2,100 F − ₹2,500 A = ₹400 Adverse
Investigation needed: Was cheaper material lower quality, causing more wastage?
6.6
Key Management Accounting Decisions
When Numbers Drive Strategy

Management accounting provides the analysis behind the most important business decisions. These decision-making scenarios are core ACCA MA and PM exam topics — and real-world skills used in every MNC.

🏭
Make or Buy Decision
  • Should we make a component in-house or outsource?
  • Compare: marginal cost of making vs buying price
  • Only relevant (incremental) costs matter
  • Consider: spare capacity, quality, dependency risk
  • Sunk costs and fixed costs ignored (if unavoidable)
Relevant Costing
⛔
Shut Down or Continue
  • Should we close a loss-making product/division?
  • If contribution > 0, closing may reduce total profit
  • Fixed costs reallocated — not saved — if shared
  • Consider: long-term strategy, complementary products
  • Short-term vs long-term perspective critical
Contribution Analysis
🏷️
Pricing Decisions
  • Cost-plus pricing: Total Cost + Mark-up %
  • Marginal cost pricing: Variable Cost + Contribution
  • Target costing: Market price − desired profit = target cost
  • Penetration vs skimming strategy
  • Transfer pricing for intra-group transactions
Pricing Strategy
🔒
Limiting Factor Analysis
  • When a resource is scarce (machine hours, labour, material)
  • Rank products by contribution per unit of limiting factor
  • Produce in order of ranking until resource exhausted
  • Example: If machine hours are limited → ₹ contribution per machine hour
  • Linear programming for multiple constraints
Scarce Resource
💰
Relevant Costing
  • Only future, incremental, cash costs are relevant
  • Sunk costs — already spent — always irrelevant
  • Committed costs — future but unavoidable — irrelevant
  • Opportunity cost — always relevant
  • Non-cash costs (depreciation) — irrelevant for decisions
Decision Costing
📈
Capital Investment Appraisal
  • Should we invest ₹50L in new equipment?
  • Payback Period — how long to recover investment?
  • NPV — Net Present Value (time value of money)
  • IRR — Internal Rate of Return
  • Covered in depth in Corporate Finance chapter
Long-Term Decisions

Performance Measurement — KPIs & Balanced Scorecard

Management accounting is not just about costs and budgets — it is also about measuring whether the organisation is achieving its objectives. Financial KPIs alone are not enough. Modern organisations use the Balanced Scorecard.

💰
Financial Perspective
  • Revenue growth, profit margin
  • Return on Capital Employed (ROCE)
  • EPS growth, cash generation
  • Cost reduction targets
How do we look to shareholders?
😊
Customer Perspective
  • Customer satisfaction scores (NPS)
  • Market share, customer retention
  • On-time delivery rate
  • Product/service quality metrics
How do customers see us?
⚙️
Internal Process Perspective
  • Cycle time, throughput efficiency
  • Defect rates, waste reduction
  • Order processing time
  • Innovation pipeline metrics
What must we excel at?
📚
Learning & Growth Perspective
  • Employee training hours per year
  • Staff satisfaction and retention
  • Technology adoption rates
  • Innovation and R&D investment
Can we continue to improve?
📌 Why Balanced Scorecard Matters for Your Career
The Balanced Scorecard (BSC) was developed by Kaplan & Norton and is used by virtually every large MNC. As an ACCA professional in FP&A or advisory, you will build and present BSC dashboards to senior management. It is tested in ACCA PM paper — and Power BI skills make you the person who visualises this data beautifully. This is where ₹30–50 LPA roles are created.

Responsibility Centres
Who is Accountable for What

Large organisations divide themselves into responsibility centres — units where a manager is held accountable for specific financial results. This is the foundation of management control in any MNC.

Centre TypeManager Accountable ForKey MeasureExample
Cost CentreCosts only — no revenue controlCost variance vs budgetIT dept, HR dept, factory floor
Revenue CentreRevenue only — no cost controlRevenue variance vs budgetSales team, regional sales office
Profit CentreBoth revenues and costsProfit vs budgetBusiness division, product line
Investment CentreProfit AND capital investedROCE, RI, EVASubsidiary company, SBU

Investment Centre Metrics — Key ACCA PM Topic

Evaluating Investment Centre Performance

ROCE (Return on Capital Employed):
ROCE = Operating Profit ÷ Capital Employed × 100
Example: ₹50L profit ÷ ₹2.5Cr capital = 20% ROCE

RI (Residual Income):
RI = Operating Profit − (Capital Employed × Cost of Capital %)
Example: ₹50L − (₹2.5Cr × 12%) = ₹50L − ₹30L = ₹20L RI

RI is preferred over ROCE because it avoids the problem of managers rejecting positive NPV projects that would dilute their ROCE.

Common Mistakes, Flashcards & Interview Q&A

Part A — Common Mistakes (Don't Repeat These)

❌ MISTAKE 1
Getting Favourable/Adverse Backwards
The convention is Variance = Standard − Actual for costs (positive = favourable, meaning we spent less than planned). For revenue/sales variances, it flips: Actual − Standard (positive = favourable, meaning we earned more than planned). Applying the cost-side rule to a sales variance gives the exact opposite (wrong) sign.
❌ MISTAKE 2
Using Actual Quantity in Usage Variance Instead of Standard Price
Material Usage Variance = (Standard Qty − Actual Qty) × STANDARD Price — not actual price. Mixing actual price into the usage variance blends the price effect into the usage number, double-counting and making it impossible to isolate the true cause of the variance.
❌ MISTAKE 3
Not Flexing the Budget Before Comparing to Actual
Comparing actual results directly to the original fixed budget is misleading if actual production volume differs from planned. A flexed budget (adjusted to actual activity level) is needed first — otherwise volume differences get mistaken for efficiency or spending problems.
❌ MISTAKE 4
Treating Sunk Costs as Relevant to a Decision
Sunk costs (already spent, unrecoverable) should NEVER influence a forward-looking decision — only relevant costs (future, incremental, cash-based) matter. Students frequently include past R&D spend or prior purchase costs in make-or-buy or continue/discontinue analysis, which is a fundamental error.
❌ MISTAKE 5
Ignoring Opportunity Cost in Decision-Making
When a resource has an alternative use (e.g. machine capacity that could be used for another profitable product), the opportunity cost of using it for the current decision must be included as a relevant cost. Overlooking this understates the true cost of a decision.
❌ MISTAKE 6
Confusing Cost Centres, Profit Centres, and Investment Centres
A cost centre is only responsible for costs; a profit centre for both revenue and costs; an investment centre for profit AND the capital invested to generate it (measured via ROI/RI). Evaluating a cost centre manager on profit, or a profit centre manager on ROI, misapplies the responsibility accounting framework.

Part D — Interview Questions & Model Answers

QuestionWhat They Are TestingModel Answer
"What's the difference between financial accounting and management accounting?"Foundational clarity on the two accounting branchesFinancial accounting produces statutory statements for external users (investors, regulators, tax authorities) following IFRS, focused on historical, verified data. Management accounting produces internal reports for managers — budgets, variances, cost analysis — with no mandated format, forward-looking, and designed purely to support decision-making.
"How would you investigate an adverse labour efficiency variance?"Practical, investigative thinking beyond formula recallI'd first confirm the variance calculation, then look for root causes — was there new/undertrained staff, equipment breakdowns causing downtime, poor quality materials requiring rework, or unrealistic standards set in the first place? Variance analysis identifies WHERE a problem occurred; investigation with operations teams identifies WHY.
"Why should sunk costs be ignored in decision-making?"Core relevant costing principle — frequently testedSunk costs are costs already incurred and unrecoverable regardless of the decision made now — they have no bearing on future cash flows. Including them in a decision analysis (e.g. continuing a failing project because of money already spent) is a classic behavioural bias, not sound financial logic; only future, incremental, relevant costs should drive the decision.
