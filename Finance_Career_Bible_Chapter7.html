Revenue is the top line of every Income Statement — the number that everything else flows from. But when exactly should revenue be recognised? When a contract is signed? When cash is received? When the product is delivered? When service is completed? IFRS 15 answers all of these questions with one unified 5-step model.

"Revenue is vanity, profit is sanity, cash is reality — but recognising revenue at the right time is the foundation of all three."
7.1
Why Revenue Recognition Matters

Revenue recognition is one of the most important — and most manipulated — areas of accounting. Getting it wrong, deliberately or accidentally, can massively distort financial statements.

📈
Why It's Critical
  • Revenue drives every key financial metric
  • Earnings per share, P/E ratio, market cap — all linked to revenue
  • Investors make buy/sell decisions based on revenue trends
  • Early or late recognition = misleading financial statements
  • Major frauds (Satyam, Enron) involved revenue manipulation
Most Audited Line Item
🔍
The Core Principle
  • Recognise revenue when (or as) performance obligations are satisfied
  • At the amount of consideration the entity expects to receive
  • This replaces old "risks and rewards" approach
  • Now based on transfer of control to customer
  • Applies to ALL contracts with customers — every industry
IFRS 15 Core Principle
🏢
Who is Affected
  • Every company that earns revenue from customers
  • Especially complex: software, construction, telecom, retail
  • Big 4 audit teams spend significant time on revenue
  • MNC finance teams need IFRS 15 expertise for reporting
  • ACCA FR and SBR papers test it heavily
Universal Application
7.2
Before IFRS 15 — The Problem

Before IFRS 15 (effective 2018), revenue was governed by two separate standards — IAS 18 (Revenue) for goods and services, and IAS 11 (Construction Contracts) for long-term contracts. These standards were inconsistent, incomplete, and left too much room for judgment — creating opportunities for manipulation.

📌 The Satyam Fraud — Revenue Recognition Gone Wrong
India's biggest accounting scandal — Satyam Computers (2009) — involved the CEO falsifying revenue and cash figures for years. Fictitious invoices were raised, revenue was inflated by ₹5,040 crore, and fake bank balances were created. The result: investors lost billions, the company nearly collapsed, and PwC (the auditor) faced massive penalties. IFRS 15's stricter controls and disclosure requirements make this type of fraud much harder to execute today.
7.3
IFRS 15 — The 5-Step Model
The Heart of Revenue Recognition

IFRS 15 introduced one unified model for all revenue recognition — the 5-Step Model. Every revenue transaction, no matter how simple or complex, must go through these five steps.

Step 1: Identify the CONTRACT with a customer
Step 2: Identify the PERFORMANCE OBLIGATIONS
Step 3: Determine the TRANSACTION PRICE
Step 4: ALLOCATE the price to performance obligations
Step 5: RECOGNISE revenue when obligation is satisfied
The IFRS 15 Five-Step Model — applies to every contract with every customer
7.4
Step 1 — Identify the Contract

A contract is an agreement between two or more parties that creates enforceable rights and obligations. Under IFRS 15, a contract exists only if all five criteria are met.

#CriterionWhat It Means
1Approved & CommittedBoth parties have approved the contract and are committed to performing obligations
2Rights IdentifiedEach party's rights regarding goods/services are clearly identified
3Payment TermsPayment terms for the goods/services are clearly identified
4Commercial SubstanceThe contract has commercial substance — it changes expected future cash flows
5Probable CollectionIt is probable that the entity will collect the consideration it is entitled to
💡 Contract Combination & Modification
Contract Combination: Multiple contracts entered at same time with same customer may be combined if linked.
Contract Modification: When contract terms change — account as a new contract OR modification of existing one depending on whether distinct goods/services are added and at standalone price.
7.5
Step 2 — Identify Performance Obligations

A performance obligation is a promise to transfer a distinct good or service to a customer. One contract can have multiple performance obligations — and each must be accounted for separately.

✅
When is a Good/Service Distinct?
  • Capable of being distinct: Customer can benefit from it on its own or with other available resources
  • Distinct in context of contract: Separately identifiable from other promises in contract
  • BOTH criteria must be met for it to be distinct
  • If not distinct → combine with other obligations until distinct
Both Criteria Required
📦
Examples — Multiple Obligations
  • Phone + 24-month service plan = 2 obligations
  • Software licence + annual support = 2 obligations
  • Car + 3-year warranty = 2 obligations
  • Construction contract (single building) = 1 obligation
  • Audit + tax return = 2 separate obligations
Real World Examples
⏰
Series of Distinct Services
  • Substantially the same services transferred over time
  • Same pattern of transfer (over time)
  • Treated as single performance obligation
  • Example: Monthly cleaning service for 12 months
  • Revenue recognised evenly each month
Ongoing Services
7.6
Step 3 — Determine the Transaction Price

The transaction price is the amount of consideration the entity expects to be entitled to in exchange for transferring goods or services. It sounds simple — but many contracts have variable or complex consideration.

💰
Variable Consideration
  • Price includes discounts, rebates, refunds, bonuses, penalties
  • Must estimate variable consideration
  • Use Expected Value OR Most Likely Amount method
  • Only include if highly probable no significant reversal will occur
  • Example: Bonus of ₹10L if project completed on time
Estimation Required
⏳
Significant Financing Component
  • If payment deferred more than 12 months — adjust for time value
  • Discount the transaction price to present value
  • Recognise interest income separately over deferral period
  • Practical expedient: ignore if less than 12 months
  • Example: ₹1.1L receivable in 1 year → ₹1L revenue now
Time Value of Money
🔄
Non-Cash Consideration
  • Customer pays in goods, shares, or services
  • Measure at fair value of non-cash consideration
  • If fair value can't be measured → use standalone selling price
  • Example: Customer gives company shares as payment
Fair Value Measurement
🎁
Consideration Payable to Customer
  • If entity pays consideration to customer (e.g. vouchers, slotting fees)
  • Reduce transaction price UNLESS it's for distinct good/service
  • Example: Supermarket pays ₹5L slotting fee to retailer
  • Reduces revenue, not an expense
Revenue Reduction
7.7
Step 4 — Allocate the Transaction Price

When a contract has multiple performance obligations, the total transaction price must be allocated to each obligation based on its relative standalone selling price (SSP).

Allocation = (SSP of Obligation ÷ Total SSP of all Obligations) × Transaction Price
Standalone Selling Price (SSP) = price charged when sold separately

Complete Worked Example — Phone + Service Plan

Ashish Telecom — Contract with Customer

Contract: Customer pays ₹24,000 for a phone + 24-month service plan
Standalone prices: Phone = ₹18,000 | Service plan = ₹12,000 (₹500/month × 24)
Total SSP: ₹18,000 + ₹12,000 = ₹30,000

Allocation:
Phone: ₹18,000 ÷ ₹30,000 × ₹24,000 = ₹14,400
Service plan: ₹12,000 ÷ ₹30,000 × ₹24,000 = ₹9,600

Revenue recognition:
₹14,400 recognised immediately when phone delivered (point in time)
₹9,600 recognised over 24 months = ₹400/month (over time)
7.8
Step 5 — Recognise Revenue
The Final Step

Revenue is recognised when (or as) a performance obligation is satisfied — i.e., when control of the good or service transfers to the customer. This happens either at a point in time or over time.

⚡
At a Point in Time
  • Most goods and some services recognised at a single point
  • When control transfers — indicated by:
  • Customer has right to payment
  • Customer has legal title / physical possession
  • Customer has risks and rewards of ownership
  • Example: Selling a laptop — revenue on delivery
Single Moment
📅
Over Time
  • Revenue spread over the period of performance
  • If ANY of these criteria met:
  • Customer simultaneously receives and consumes benefit
  • Entity creates/enhances asset customer controls
  • No alternative use + right to payment for work done
  • Example: Construction contract, SaaS subscription, cleaning service
Spread Over Period

Measuring Progress for Over-Time Recognition

MethodHow It WorksBest Used For
Output MethodBased on value of outputs delivered (surveys, milestones, units produced)When output can be clearly measured
Input MethodBased on resources consumed (costs incurred, hours worked, materials used)Construction contracts, long-term projects
Straight-LineEqual amount each period (special case of output/input)Subscriptions, licences, maintenance

Construction Contract Example — Over-Time Recognition

Ashish Infrastructure — ₹1 Crore Building Contract

Contract value: ₹1,00,00,000 | Total expected cost: ₹80,00,000
Year 1 costs incurred: ₹32,00,000

Stage of completion (Input method):
₹32,00,000 ÷ ₹80,00,000 = 40% complete

Revenue recognised Year 1:
40% × ₹1,00,00,000 = ₹40,00,000

Gross Profit Year 1:
₹40,00,000 − ₹32,00,000 = ₹8,00,000
7.9
Special Topics
Contract Costs, Licences & More
💸
Contract Costs
  • Costs to obtain a contract (e.g. sales commission) — capitalise if expected to be recovered
  • Amortise over the period of benefit
  • Costs to fulfil a contract — capitalise if they generate resources used to satisfy future obligations
  • Practical expedient: expense if amortisation period ≤ 1 year
Capitalise if Recoverable
🔑
Licences
  • Right to access — revenue over licence period (e.g. Netflix, SaaS)
  • Right to use — revenue at point in time (e.g. software licence, patent)
  • Sales-based/usage-based royalties — recognise when later of sale/usage or obligation satisfied
  • Film/music royalties are a classic exam example
Access vs Use
↩️
Returns, Warranties & Repurchases
  • Expected returns → reduce revenue (refund liability)
  • Assurance warranties → not a performance obligation (expense under IAS 37)
  • Service warranties → separate performance obligation
  • Repurchase agreements → may be a lease or financing arrangement
Common Exam Areas
🎟️
Customer Loyalty Programmes
  • Points/rewards = separate performance obligation
  • Allocate portion of transaction price to points
  • Recognise when points redeemed or expire
  • Example: Airline miles, credit card reward points
  • Major revenue recognition issue for retail & airlines
Loyalty Points

Common Mistakes, Flashcards & Interview Q&A

Part A — Common Mistakes (Don't Repeat These)

❌ MISTAKE 1
Recognising Revenue When Cash is Received, Not When Control Transfers
The core principle of IFRS 15 is that revenue is recognised when control of the goods/service transfers to the customer — not when invoiced or when cash is collected. Students often default to cash-basis thinking, which contradicts the entire 5-step model.
❌ MISTAKE 2
Missing Separate Performance Obligations
A single contract can contain multiple distinct performance obligations (e.g. a phone + service plan), each recognised on its own timeline. Treating the whole contract as one obligation and recognising all revenue at delivery understates deferred revenue for ongoing services.
❌ MISTAKE 3
Allocating Transaction Price Using Contract Price Instead of Standalone Selling Price
Allocation must be based on relative STANDALONE selling prices (SSP) of each obligation — not simply splitting the contract price evenly, and not using the price stated in the contract for each item (which may include a bundled discount).
❌ MISTAKE 4
Confusing "Point in Time" vs "Over Time" Recognition
Revenue is recognised over time only if specific criteria are met (customer simultaneously receives/consumes benefits, or the entity's performance creates/enhances an asset the customer controls, or the asset has no alternative use with a right to payment). Otherwise, revenue is recognised at a point in time — when control transfers, typically on delivery.
❌ MISTAKE 5
Not Adjusting Transaction Price for Variable Consideration
Discounts, rebates, refunds, and performance bonuses must be estimated and included in the transaction price (using expected value or most-likely-amount methods), subject to a constraint that avoids overstating revenue that is highly uncertain to be earned. Ignoring these adjustments overstates revenue.
❌ MISTAKE 6
Treating a Contract Modification as a Brand New Contract Automatically
IFRS 15 has specific rules for contract modifications — depending on whether the additional goods/services are distinct and priced at standalone value, the modification may be treated as a separate contract, a termination of the old contract with a new one, or a cumulative catch-up adjustment. Applying a blanket "always treat as new contract" rule is incorrect.
