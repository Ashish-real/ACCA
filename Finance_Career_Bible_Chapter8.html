Every business owns physical assets — buildings, machinery, vehicles, computers, furniture. These are called Property, Plant & Equipment (PPE) — and IAS 16 governs exactly how they must be recognised, measured, depreciated, revalued, and eventually removed from the books. This is one of the most tested topics in ACCA FA and FR papers.

"PPE is the backbone of every manufacturing, infrastructure, and service business. Get the accounting wrong and the entire Balance Sheet is misstated."
8.1
What is PPE — Definition & Scope

Under IAS 16, PPE are tangible items held for use in production, supply of goods/services, rental to others, or administrative purposes — and expected to be used for more than one accounting period.

🏢
Property
  • Land (not depreciated — infinite useful life)
  • Buildings (offices, factories, warehouses)
  • Leasehold improvements
  • Investment property excluded → IAS 40
Land Never Depreciated
🏭
Plant
  • Manufacturing machinery and equipment
  • Production lines, assembly equipment
  • Industrial tools and heavy equipment
  • Power generators, compressors
Production Assets
🚗
Equipment
  • Computers, servers, office equipment
  • Vehicles — cars, trucks, forklifts
  • Furniture and fixtures
  • Medical equipment, lab instruments
All Other Tangibles
📌 What IAS 16 Does NOT Cover
IAS 2: Inventory (assets held for sale in ordinary course)  |  IAS 40: Investment property (held for rental income/capital appreciation)  |  IFRS 5: Non-current assets held for sale  |  IAS 41: Biological assets (living animals/plants)  |  IFRS 16: Right-of-use assets under leases
8.2
Recognition — When Does PPE Go on the Balance Sheet?

An item of PPE is recognised as an asset only when both of these criteria are met:

✅
Criterion 1 — Probable Future Benefits
  • It is probable that future economic benefits will flow to the entity
  • The asset will generate revenue, reduce costs, or provide service potential
  • Example: A delivery van will help generate delivery revenue
  • Not speculative — must be probable (more likely than not)
Economic Benefit Test
📏
Criterion 2 — Reliable Measurement
  • The cost of the item can be measured reliably
  • Usually straightforward — invoice, contract, receipt
  • For self-constructed assets — sum of all construction costs
  • Estimated costs acceptable if reliable estimate possible
Measurability Test
💡 Spare Parts & Servicing Equipment
Major spare parts and standby equipment qualify as PPE if used for more than one period. Routine servicing costs (oil, filters, small repairs) are expensed to P&L immediately — they are not capitalised. Only major inspections or overhauls that extend useful life can be capitalised.
8.3
Initial Measurement — What is the Cost?
Everything Included in Cost

When PPE is first recognised, it is measured at cost. Cost is not just the purchase price — it includes everything necessary to bring the asset to its intended location and condition for use.

Cost = Purchase Price (less trade discounts)
+ Import Duties & Non-refundable Taxes
+ Directly Attributable Costs
+ Initial Estimate of Dismantling/Restoration Costs
IAS 16 — The complete cost of a PPE item at initial recognition

Complete Cost Calculation Example

Ashish Manufacturing — New Production Machine

List price: ₹10,00,000 | Trade discount: 10% → Purchase price = ₹9,00,000
Import duty: ₹50,000
Site preparation: ₹30,000
Installation & testing: ₹40,000

Training cost of ₹20,000 is expensed to P&L — not part of asset cost.
8.4
Subsequent Measurement — Cost vs Revaluation Model

After initial recognition, IAS 16 allows TWO models for measuring PPE. The entity must choose one policy per class of assets and apply it consistently.

📌
Cost Model
  • Asset carried at: Cost − Accumulated Depreciation − Impairment
  • Simple, reliable, easy to apply
  • Most common in practice
  • No fair value movements through P&L or OCI
  • Preferred by most companies for simplicity
Simpler · Most Common
📈
Revaluation Model
  • Asset carried at: Fair Value − Subsequent Depreciation − Impairment
  • Revaluations must be regular enough to keep CV close to FV
  • Revaluation surplus → Other Comprehensive Income (OCI)
  • Revaluation deficit → P&L (unless reversing prior surplus)
  • Must apply to entire class of assets
Fair Value · Complex
8.5
Depreciation — Methods & Calculations
The Most Tested PPE Topic

Depreciation is the systematic allocation of the depreciable amount of an asset over its useful life. It is not about the market value of the asset — it is about spreading the cost over the periods that benefit from using it.

Depreciable Amount = Cost (or Revalued Amount) − Residual Value
Carrying Amount = Cost − Accumulated Depreciation
Key terms — know these definitions for every depreciation question

Three Depreciation Methods

📏
Straight-Line Method (SLM)
  • Equal depreciation charge every year
  • Formula: (Cost − Residual Value) ÷ Useful Life
  • Simple, consistent, widely used
  • Best for: buildings, furniture, office equipment
  • Most common method in practice
Equal Each Year
📉
Reducing Balance Method
  • Higher depreciation in early years, lower later
  • Formula: Carrying Amount × Depreciation %
  • Residual value not deducted in calculation
  • Best for: technology, vehicles (lose value fast early)
  • Reflects economic reality better for some assets
Higher Early Years
⚙️
Units of Production Method
  • Depreciation based on actual usage/output
  • Formula: (Cost − RV) ÷ Total Units × Units This Period
  • Best for: mining equipment, machines with known capacity
  • Zero depreciation if asset not used
  • Most accurate reflection of consumption
Usage Based

8.6
Revaluation Model — Deep Dive

When an entity chooses the revaluation model, the asset is carried at fair value. Revaluations create either a surplus (gain) or a deficit (loss) — and each is treated differently.

SituationTreatmentWhere it Goes
Revaluation Surplus (first time)Credit Revaluation SurplusOther Comprehensive Income (OCI) → Equity
Revaluation Deficit (first time)Debit P&L as expenseIncome Statement (loss)
Deficit after previous surplusFirst reduce existing surplus, then P&L for remainderOCI (up to surplus) then P&L
Surplus after previous deficitFirst reverse P&L loss, then OCI for remainderP&L (up to prior loss) then OCI
Revaluation surplus on disposalTransfer surplus to retained earningsWithin equity (not through P&L)

Revaluation Worked Example

Building Revaluation — Ashish Properties

Year 1: Building purchased for ₹50,00,000. Depreciated ₹2,00,000. Carrying Value = ₹48,00,000
Revaluation to ₹60,00,000: Surplus = ₹60,00,000 − ₹48,00,000 = ₹12,00,000
→ Dr Building ₹12,00,000 | Cr Revaluation Surplus (OCI) ₹12,00,000

Year 3: Market crashes. Revaluation to ₹44,00,000. New carrying value after depreciation = ₹56,00,000
Deficit = ₹56,00,000 − ₹44,00,000 = ₹12,00,000
→ First use revaluation surplus ₹12,00,000 (OCI reversal)
→ Remaining deficit ₹0 → P&L
If deficit exceeded surplus, the excess would go to P&L as a loss.
8.7
Impairment of PPE — IAS 36

An asset is impaired when its carrying amount exceeds its recoverable amount. IAS 36 requires entities to assess at each reporting date whether there are any indicators of impairment.

Recoverable Amount = Higher of:
Fair Value Less Costs of Disposal (FVLCD)
OR
Value in Use (VIU) — PV of future cash flows from asset
If Carrying Amount > Recoverable Amount → Impairment Loss = write down to Recoverable Amount
⚠️
Impairment Indicators
  • Significant decline in market value
  • Technological obsolescence
  • Asset damaged or idle
  • Poor economic performance of asset
  • Increase in market interest rates
  • Net assets exceed market capitalisation
Triggers Review
📉
Impairment Loss Treatment
  • Cost model: impairment loss → P&L immediately
  • Revaluation model: first reduce revaluation surplus
  • Then any excess → P&L
  • Impairment can be reversed in later periods (except goodwill)
  • Reversal credited to P&L (or OCI if revalued asset)
IAS 36
8.8
Derecognition — Disposal of PPE

An asset is derecognised (removed from the Balance Sheet) when it is disposed of, or when no future economic benefits are expected. A gain or loss arises on disposal.

Gain/Loss on Disposal = Proceeds Received − Carrying Amount at Date of Disposal
Carrying Amount = Cost − Accumulated Depreciation − Impairment
Positive = Gain (P&L Credit) | Negative = Loss (P&L Debit)

Complete Disposal Example

Disposal of Vehicle — Ashish Consulting

Vehicle cost: ₹8,00,000 | Accumulated depreciation: ₹5,50,000
Carrying Amount = ₹8,00,000 − ₹5,50,000 = ₹2,50,000
Sale proceeds received: ₹3,20,000
Gain on disposal = ₹3,20,000 − ₹2,50,000 = ₹70,000 (P&L Credit)

Key Takeaways & Action Steps
1️⃣
Recognition = 2 Criteria
  • Probable future economic benefits
  • Reliably measurable cost
  • Both must be met — not just one
2️⃣
Cost Includes More Than Price
  • Add: duties, installation, testing, dismantling PV
  • Exclude: training, admin, abnormal waste
  • Classic exam trap — always check inclusions
3️⃣
Land Never Depreciated
  • Infinite useful life
  • Separate land from buildings — different treatment
  • Buildings on land ARE depreciated
4️⃣
Two Models — Choose One Per Class
  • Cost: simple, reliable
  • Revaluation: fair value, OCI surplus
  • Cannot switch frequently
5️⃣
Revaluation — OCI Then P&L
  • Surplus first to OCI (equity)
  • Deficit first reverses OCI, then P&L
  • Most common exam mistake — getting this wrong

Disposal = Proceeds − CV
  • Always calculate CV at date of disposal
  • Include depreciation up to disposal date
  • Gain/loss → P&L

8.12
Leases (IFRS 16)

Part A — The Big Change IFRS 16 Made

Before IFRS 16, companies could keep most leases entirely OFF the balance sheet (as "operating leases"), simply expensing rent as incurred. IFRS 16 abolished this — with narrow exceptions, almost all leases now appear on the balance sheet as a right-of-use asset and a corresponding lease liability, dramatically increasing reported assets and liabilities for lease-heavy industries (retail, airlines, hospitality).

IFRS 16 — Initial Recognition
Lease Liability (Initial)
Present Value of Future Lease Payments, discounted at the interest rate implicit in the lease (or the lessee's incremental borrowing rate if that rate isn't readily determinable)
Right-of-Use (ROU) Asset (Initial)
Lease Liability + Initial Direct Costs + Payments Made Before Commencement − Lease Incentives Received
Subsequent Measurement — Liability
Increases by interest charged (unwinding the discount) each period, decreases by actual lease payments made — same logic as a loan amortisation schedule
Subsequent Measurement — ROU Asset
Depreciated on a straight-line basis (usually) over the shorter of the lease term or the asset's useful life

Part B — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: A 4-Year Equipment Lease

Given: Company leases equipment for 4 years, annual payments of ₹5,00,000 paid at the END of each year. Interest rate implicit in the lease = 8%. No initial direct costs or incentives.

Step 1 — Initial Lease Liability (PV of payments):
PV = ₹5,00,000 × [Annuity Factor, 8%, 4 years]
Annuity Factor (8%, 4 yrs) = 3.312
Lease Liability = ₹5,00,000 × 3.312 = ₹16,56,000

Step 2 — Initial ROU Asset:
ROU Asset = ₹16,56,000 (same as liability, since no other adjustments)

Step 3 — Year 1 Liability Movement:
Interest expense = ₹16,56,000 × 8% = ₹1,32,480
Liability before payment = ₹16,56,000 + ₹1,32,480 = ₹17,88,480
Less payment = ₹17,88,480 − ₹5,00,000 = ₹12,88,480 (closing liability, Year 1)

Step 4 — Year 1 P&L Impact:
Depreciation = ₹16,56,000 ÷ 4 years = ₹4,14,000
Interest expense = ₹1,32,480
Total Year 1 expense = ₹4,14,000 + ₹1,32,480 = ₹5,46,480

Key Point: Total Year 1 P&L expense (₹5,46,480) is HIGHER than the actual cash paid (₹5,00,000) — this "front-loading" effect is a direct, frequently-tested consequence of IFRS 16: interest expense is higher in early years (when the liability balance is largest), making reported expenses front-loaded compared to the old straight-line rent expense under the previous standard.
🔑 Recognition Exemptions — What's Still Off Balance Sheet
IFRS 16 provides two narrow exemptions where a lessee CAN still simply expense payments (no ROU asset/liability): short-term leases (12 months or less, with no purchase option) and low-value asset leases (e.g. laptops, small office furniture — based on the asset's value when new, not its lease term). Outside these two exceptions, virtually all leases now hit the balance sheet.
8.13
Provisions, Contingent Liabilities & Contingent Assets (IAS 37)

Part A — The Three-Way Classification

IAS 37 governs uncertain obligations and possible assets — a genuinely judgment-heavy area, since the treatment depends entirely on the PROBABILITY of the outcome.

ClassificationDefinitionAccounting Treatment
ProvisionA present obligation (legal or constructive) from a past event, where an outflow is PROBABLE (more likely than not) and can be reliably estimatedRECOGNISE as a liability on the balance sheet, at the best estimate of the amount required to settle it
Contingent LiabilityA possible obligation depending on uncertain future events, OR a present obligation where an outflow is POSSIBLE but not probable, OR the amount can't be reliably measuredDo NOT recognise — DISCLOSE in the notes (nature, estimated financial effect, uncertainties) unless the probability is remote
Contingent AssetA possible asset depending on uncertain future events (e.g. a pending legal claim IN THE COMPANY'S FAVOUR)Do NOT recognise — DISCLOSE only if the inflow is PROBABLE. If VIRTUALLY CERTAIN, it's no longer contingent — recognise as an actual asset
🔑 The Probability Thresholds — Memorise These
Probable (>50% likely) is the threshold for recognising a LIABILITY. Possible (some chance, but ≤50%) means disclose only, don't recognise. Remote (very unlikely) means no disclosure needed at all. Notice the ASYMMETRY: for liabilities, "probable" (>50%) triggers recognition — a relatively LOW bar, reflecting accounting's prudence principle. For ASSETS, the bar is much higher — "virtually certain" (near 100%) — asymmetric treatment that deliberately avoids overstating assets while ensuring liabilities aren't understated.

Part B — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: Classifying 3 Uncertain Items

Item 1: The company is being sued for ₹50,00,000 for a product defect. Legal counsel assesses a 70% chance of losing the case, with damages likely to be around ₹30,00,000.
Classification: PROVISION. Probability is 70% (probable, >50%) and the amount can be reliably estimated. Recognise a provision of ₹30,00,000 as a liability, with a corresponding expense in P&L.

Item 2: The company has provided a guarantee for a supplier's bank loan of ₹20,00,000. Legal counsel assesses only a 15% chance the supplier will default and the guarantee will be called upon.
Classification: CONTINGENT LIABILITY. Probability is only 15% (possible, not probable). Do NOT recognise a liability — disclose the nature and maximum exposure (₹20,00,000) in the notes.

Item 3: The company has filed an insurance claim for ₹10,00,000 following a warehouse fire. The insurer has verbally confirmed the claim will be paid in full, with only routine paperwork remaining.
Classification: Effectively VIRTUALLY CERTAIN — recognise as an ASSET (a receivable from the insurer), not merely disclosed as a contingent asset, since the inflow is no longer meaningfully uncertain.
8.14
Intangible Assets (IAS 38)

Part A — Recognition Criteria

An intangible asset (patents, licences, software, trademarks — excluding goodwill, which follows IFRS 3) can only be recognised if it's identifiable (separable from the business, or arising from contractual/legal rights), the entity has control over it, and it's expected to generate future economic benefits.

🛒
Purchased Intangibles
  • Recognised at COST (purchase price + directly attributable costs) — relatively straightforward, since there's an objective transaction price
  • Examples: a purchased patent, an acquired customer list, a purchased software licence
Capitalise at Cost
🔬
Internally Generated Intangibles — The R&D Split
  • Research phase: ALWAYS expensed immediately — too uncertain to demonstrate future economic benefit
  • Development phase: CAPITALISED only if ALL 6 strict criteria are met (technical feasibility, intention to complete, ability to use/sell, probable future benefit, adequate resources, ability to reliably measure the cost)
Research vs Development
🔑 Why Internally Generated Brands, Customer Lists & Goodwill Are NEVER Capitalised
IAS 38 specifically PROHIBITS capitalising internally generated brands, mastheads, customer lists, and goodwill — because their cost cannot be reliably distinguished from the cost of developing the business as a whole. This is a frequently tested contrast: a PURCHASED brand (bought from another company) CAN be capitalised at its purchase cost, but a brand a company builds up itself over years through marketing spend CANNOT be recognised as an intangible asset, no matter how valuable it's genuinely become.

Part B — Worked Example

📋 Scenario: R&D Cost Classification

Given: A pharma company spends ₹2 Crore over a year on a new drug project: ₹1.2 Crore on early-stage research (exploring biological compounds, no clear product yet identified) and ₹0.8 Crore on later-stage development (after regulatory approval pathway confirmed, technical feasibility demonstrated, and management has committed the budget to complete and commercialise the drug).

Research phase (₹1.2 Crore): Expensed immediately to P&L — the 6 capitalisation criteria clearly cannot be met at this exploratory stage (no confirmed technical feasibility, no confirmed commercial viability yet).

Development phase (₹0.8 Crore): Capitalised as an intangible asset — assuming all 6 criteria are genuinely met (which the facts given suggest: technical feasibility demonstrated, clear intention and resources to complete).

P&L impact: Only ₹1.2 Crore hits the Income Statement this year (research); the ₹0.8 Crore development cost sits on the Balance Sheet as an intangible asset, to be amortised over the drug's useful economic life once it's ready for use/sale — matching the cost against the future revenue it will help generate, rather than expensing it all upfront.
8.15
Common Mistakes, Flashcards & Interview Q&A

Part A — Common Mistakes (Don't Repeat These)

❌ MISTAKE 1
Depreciating Based on Market Value Instead of Cost/Revalued Amount
Depreciation under the cost model is calculated on original cost minus residual value — not on current market value. Confusing depreciation with a market value adjustment leads to incorrect annual charges. Under the revaluation model, depreciation restarts based on the revalued amount going forward.
❌ MISTAKE 2
Deducting Residual Value in the Reducing Balance Method
Unlike straight-line, the reducing balance method applies the depreciation rate to the full carrying amount each year — residual value is NOT subtracted before applying the rate. This is a frequent source of calculation errors in exams.
❌ MISTAKE 3
Sending a Revaluation Deficit Straight to P&L Without Checking for Prior Surplus
If there's an existing revaluation surplus on the same asset, a subsequent deficit must first be offset against that surplus (via OCI) before any excess goes to P&L. Skipping this check and expensing the full deficit to P&L is a very commonly tested error.
❌ MISTAKE 4
Capitalising Costs That Should Be Expensed
Only costs directly attributable to bringing the asset to its working condition (purchase price, delivery, installation, professional fees) are capitalised. General administrative costs, staff training on the new asset, and costs of relocating/reorganising operations must be expensed, not added to the asset's cost.
❌ MISTAKE 5
Forgetting to Test for Impairment Separately from Depreciation
Depreciation is a planned, systematic allocation of cost. Impairment (IAS 36) is a separate, unplanned write-down when recoverable amount falls below carrying amount — triggered by indicators like physical damage, obsolescence, or adverse market changes. Treating heavy depreciation as a substitute for impairment testing misses real economic losses.
❌ MISTAKE 6
Crediting the Asset Account Directly for Depreciation
Depreciation is always credited to Accumulated Depreciation (a contra-asset account), not directly to the asset account itself. This preserves the historical cost on the books while showing accumulated wear separately — crediting the asset account directly loses this audit trail.
❌ MISTAKE 7
Keeping Operating Leases Off the Balance Sheet
Under IFRS 16, almost all leases (outside short-term and low-value exemptions) must be recognised as a right-of-use asset and lease liability — the old "operating lease = just expense the rent" treatment no longer applies for the vast majority of leases.
❌ MISTAKE 8
Recognising a Provision for a Merely "Possible" Obligation
A provision requires the outflow to be PROBABLE (>50% likely). An obligation that's merely "possible" (≤50%) should only be disclosed as a contingent liability, not recognised as an actual liability on the balance sheet.
❌ MISTAKE 9
Capitalising Research Costs or Internally Generated Brands
Research phase costs must ALWAYS be expensed immediately, regardless of how promising the project looks — only development phase costs meeting all 6 strict criteria can be capitalised. Internally generated brands, customer lists, and goodwill can never be capitalised under IAS 38, no matter how valuable they've become.

Part D — Interview Questions & Model Answers

QuestionWhat They Are TestingModel Answer
"Explain the difference between the cost model and the revaluation model under IAS 16."Core IAS 16 conceptual understandingUnder the cost model, PPE is carried at historical cost less accumulated depreciation and impairment — simple and objective. Under the revaluation model, PPE is carried at fair value at the revaluation date less subsequent depreciation, with gains typically recognised in OCI/equity and losses in P&L (unless offsetting a prior surplus). Once chosen, the model must be applied consistently to an entire class of assets.
"Why does depreciation not represent a company setting aside cash?"Understanding depreciation as a non-cash accounting allocationDepreciation is purely an accounting allocation — it spreads the historical cost of an asset over its useful life to match the expense with the revenue it helps generate. It has no direct cash impact in the period it's charged; the actual cash outflow happened when the asset was originally purchased, which is why it's added back in the Cash Flow Statement.
"How would you account for a building that's been revalued upward and then downward in later years?"Applied knowledge of the OCI/P&L interaction ruleThe initial upward revaluation creates a surplus recognised in OCI, building up equity. If the asset is later revalued downward, that deficit is first offset against the existing OCI surplus (reducing it, potentially to zero) before any remaining excess deficit is charged to P&L as an expense — this prevents double-counting gains and losses on the same underlying asset.
