Inventory (stock) is often one of the largest assets on a company's Balance Sheet — and one of the easiest to manipulate. The way inventory is valued directly impacts Cost of Goods Sold, Gross Profit, and Net Profit. IAS 2 sets out the rules — and getting it right (or catching when it's wrong) is a core skill for every accountant and auditor.

"Inventory is where profits hide. Overstate closing inventory by ₹1, and profit increases by ₹1 — instantly, without selling anything."
9.1
What is Inventory — and Why It's Tricky

Inventory is assets held for sale in the ordinary course of business, or in the process of production for such sale, or materials/supplies to be consumed in production.

📦
Three Types of Inventory
  • Raw Materials: Inputs not yet used in production
  • Work in Progress (WIP): Partially completed goods
  • Finished Goods: Completed products ready for sale
  • A manufacturer holds all three; a retailer typically only finished goods
Manufacturing Cycle
🎯
Why Inventory Valuation Matters
  • Closing inventory directly affects COGS and Gross Profit
  • Higher closing inventory → lower COGS → higher profit
  • Inventory is a current asset on the Balance Sheet
  • Overstating inventory = classic fraud technique
  • Auditors physically count and verify inventory
Profit Impact
🔄
The COGS Connection
  • COGS = Opening Inventory + Purchases − Closing Inventory
  • This connects the Balance Sheet to the Income Statement
  • Closing inventory of this year = Opening of next year
  • An error here distorts TWO years of financial statements
Two-Period Impact
9.2
The Golden Rule — Lower of Cost and NRV
IAS 2's Core Principle

IAS 2 requires inventory to be measured at the lower of cost and net realisable value (NRV). This single rule is the foundation of all inventory accounting — and reflects the prudence concept from Chapter 2.

Inventory Value = LOWER OF:
Cost   OR   Net Realisable Value (NRV)

NRV = Estimated Selling Price − Costs to Complete − Costs to Sell
The single most important rule in IAS 2 — applied item by item (or group of similar items)
✅
Why "Lower Of"?
  • Reflects the prudence concept — don't overstate assets
  • If inventory cost more to make than it can be sold for, write it down
  • Recognise losses as soon as they're foreseeable
  • Never write inventory UP above cost — even if NRV rises
Prudence in Action
🔍
Applied Item by Item
  • NOT applied to total inventory value as a whole
  • Each item (or group of similar items) compared separately
  • One item can be at cost, another at NRV — in the same inventory
  • Cannot offset a write-down on one item against a gain on another
Item-by-Item Comparison
9.3
What's Included in the Cost of Inventory

"Cost" under IAS 2 is not just the purchase price. It includes all costs of purchase, costs of conversion, and other costs incurred in bringing inventory to its present location and condition.

✅ INCLUDED in Cost❌ NOT Included — Expensed Immediately
Purchase price (net of trade discounts/rebates)Abnormal waste of materials, labour, or other resources
Import duties and non-refundable taxesStorage costs (unless necessary part of production process)
Transport and handling costs to bring inventory inAdministrative overheads not related to production
Direct labour (conversion costs)Selling costs (marketing, sales commissions)
Production overheads (absorbed on normal capacity)Interest costs (unless IAS 23 qualifying asset)
Other costs to bring to present location/conditionForeign exchange differences from foreign currency purchases
⚡ Normal vs Abnormal Capacity — Critical Distinction
Fixed production overheads are allocated based on normal capacity — the expected average production over several periods. If actual production is abnormally low, unabsorbed overheads are expensed immediately, not added to inventory cost. This prevents inventory from being inflated during periods of low production (e.g. factory shutdown).
9.4
Inventory Valuation Methods
FIFO vs Weighted Average

When identical items are purchased at different prices, IAS 2 permits two cost formulas: FIFO (First In, First Out) and Weighted Average Cost (AVCO). LIFO is NOT permitted under IFRS (unlike US GAAP).

📥
FIFO (First In, First Out)
  • Assumes oldest inventory is sold first
  • Closing inventory valued at most recent purchase prices
  • In rising prices: higher closing inventory, lower COGS, higher profit
  • Reflects physical flow for perishables (food, pharma)
  • Closing inventory closer to current replacement cost
Permitted under IFRS
⚖️
Weighted Average Cost (AVCO)
  • Average cost recalculated after each purchase
  • Smooths out price fluctuations
  • In rising prices: COGS and profit between FIFO and LIFO
  • Common for commodities and bulk materials (oil, grain, chemicals)
  • Simpler in continuous inventory systems
Permitted under IFRS
❌
LIFO (Last In, First Out)
  • NOT permitted under IFRS / Ind AS
  • Permitted under US GAAP only
  • Assumes newest inventory sold first
  • In rising prices: lower profit, lower tax (popular in US for tax reasons)
  • Important difference for ACCA students working with US subsidiaries
US GAAP Only — Not IFRS

FIFO Valuation of Closing Inventory (170 units)

Sale of 180 units consumes: 100 units (opening) + 80 units (from 10 Jan batch)
Remaining from 10 Jan batch: 150 − 80 = 70 units @ ₹220
Plus all of 25 Jan batch: 100 units @ ₹240

Closing Inventory (FIFO) = (70 × ₹220) + (100 × ₹240) = ₹15,400 + ₹24,000 = ₹39,400

AVCO Valuation of Closing Inventory (170 units)

Step 1 — Weighted average after 10 Jan purchase:
(₹20,000 + ₹33,000) ÷ (100 + 150) = ₹53,000 ÷ 250 = ₹212 per unit

Step 2 — After sale of 180 units:
Remaining = 250 − 180 = 70 units @ ₹212 = ₹14,840

Step 3 — After 25 Jan purchase:
(₹14,840 + ₹24,000) ÷ (70 + 100) = ₹38,840 ÷ 170 = ₹228.47 per unit

Closing Inventory (AVCO) = 170 × ₹228.47 = ₹38,840
📌 Impact on Profit — FIFO vs AVCO
FIFO closing inventory (₹39,400) is higher than AVCO (₹38,840) — because FIFO values closing stock at the most recent (higher) prices in a rising price environment. Higher closing inventory → lower COGS → higher reported profit under FIFO when prices are rising. This is why the choice of method matters for comparability — and must be disclosed and applied consistently (IAS 8).
9.5
Net Realisable Value (NRV)
Calculation & Examples

NRV is the estimated selling price in the ordinary course of business, less estimated costs of completion and costs necessary to make the sale. When NRV falls below cost, inventory must be written down.

NRV = Estimated Selling Price
− Estimated Costs of Completion
− Estimated Selling Costs (commission, transport, marketing)
Compare NRV to Cost — record inventory at the lower of the two

🔄 Reversal of Write-Down
If circumstances change and NRV recovers (e.g. selling price increases), the write-down can be reversed — but only up to the original cost, never above it. The reversal is recognised as a reduction in Cost of Sales (increases profit) in the period the recovery occurs. This is one of the few areas of IFRS where write-downs CAN be reversed.
9.6
Inventory Counts & Closing Inventory Adjustments

At year-end, businesses perform a physical inventory count to verify what's actually on the shelves matches the accounting records. Several common adjustments arise.

📋
Goods in Transit
  • Goods sent by supplier but not yet received at year-end
  • Include if title has passed (per contract terms — FOB, etc.)
  • Common exam adjustment — add to closing inventory + payables
Title Transfer Test
🔄
Goods Sold but Not Delivered
  • Sale recorded but goods still in warehouse at count date
  • Exclude from closing inventory if risks/control transferred
  • Check against revenue recognition (IFRS 15)
Revenue Recognition Link
↩️
Goods on Sale or Return
  • Goods sent to customer but customer can return
  • Include in supplier's inventory until sale confirmed
  • Customer should NOT include in their own inventory
Consignment Stock
🔢
Continuous vs Periodic Inventory
  • Periodic: Inventory counted at period end only — COGS calculated as balancing figure
  • Continuous (Perpetual): Inventory updated after every transaction — modern ERP systems (SAP)
  • Most MNCs use continuous systems with periodic physical verification
System Types

🎯 ACCA Exam Trap — The Two-Period Effect
A closing inventory error in Year 1 doesn't just affect Year 1 — it automatically affects Year 2 too, because Year 1's closing inventory becomes Year 2's opening inventory. If closing inventory is overstated by ₹10,000 in Year 1: Year 1 profit is overstated by ₹10,000, AND Year 2 profit is understated by ₹10,000 (because opening inventory is now overstated, increasing Year 2's COGS). Over the two years combined, total profit is correct — but each individual year is wrong. This is a favourite ACCA FA/FR exam question.
9.8
Inventory Management in MNCs — Real World
📊
Inventory Days / Turnover
  • Inventory Days = (Avg Inventory ÷ COGS) × 365
  • Inventory Turnover = COGS ÷ Average Inventory
  • Lower days = faster moving inventory = efficient
  • Used in ratio analysis — major ACCA FR/FM topic
Working Capital KPI
🏭
Just-In-Time (JIT)
  • Minimise inventory holding — order only when needed
  • Reduces storage costs and obsolescence risk
  • Requires reliable suppliers and forecasting
  • Pioneered by Toyota — used globally now
Lean Management
🔍
Big 4 Audit Focus on Inventory
  • Physical inventory count attendance — mandatory audit procedure
  • Cut-off testing — goods received/shipped near year-end
  • NRV testing — checking for obsolete/slow-moving stock
  • One of the highest-risk audit areas
Audit & Assurance (AA)

Common Mistakes, Flashcards & Interview Q&A

Part A — Common Mistakes (Don't Repeat These)

❌ MISTAKE 1
Using LIFO Under IFRS/Ind AS
LIFO is explicitly prohibited under IAS 2 (and Ind AS 2) — only FIFO and Weighted Average Cost are permitted. This is one of the most frequently tested "trick" facts in ACCA FA/FR, especially for students familiar with US GAAP where LIFO is allowed.
❌ MISTAKE 2
Comparing Cost to NRV at a Total Level Instead of Item-by-Item
The lower-of-cost-and-NRV rule is generally applied item by item (or group of similar items), not by comparing the total cost of all inventory to total NRV of all inventory. Applying it in aggregate can hide write-downs needed on specific slow-moving or damaged items.
❌ MISTAKE 3
Including Selling Costs in the Cost of Inventory
The cost of inventory includes purchase cost, import duties, and costs of conversion (direct labour, production overheads) — but NOT selling costs, storage costs (unless necessary as part of production), or general admin overheads. These are period costs, expensed when incurred.
❌ MISTAKE 4
Reversing a Write-Down Above Original Cost
If NRV recovers after a write-down, the reversal is capped at the original cost of the inventory — you can never write inventory back up above what it originally cost, even if NRV has risen significantly higher.
❌ MISTAKE 5
Forgetting the Cut-Off Principle at Year-End
Goods in transit, goods on consignment, and goods received but not yet invoiced must be correctly included/excluded from closing inventory based on when legal ownership/risk transfers — not simply based on physical location on the count date. Cut-off errors are a classic audit risk area.
❌ MISTAKE 6
Assuming Higher Closing Inventory Always Means Better Performance
A high closing inventory can also signal slow-moving stock, overproduction, or poor demand forecasting — not just strong operations. Analysts always check inventory turnover/days alongside the absolute inventory value to judge whether inventory levels are healthy.
